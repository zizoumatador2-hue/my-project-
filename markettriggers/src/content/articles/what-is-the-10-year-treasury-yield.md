**The 10-year Treasury yield is the annual return investors earn for lending to the U.S. government for ten years, set every day by trading in the bond market. It matters because it acts as a benchmark for mortgage rates, corporate borrowing and stock valuations. On November 13, 2026, it closed at 5.15 percent.**

If you only track one interest rate besides the Fed's own, make it this one. The Federal Reserve sets the overnight rate banks charge each other, but it doesn't set the 10-year yield. Investors do, by buying and selling Treasury notes every trading day. That makes the 10-year yield a running vote on growth, inflation and the government's finances, and it explains why a quiet week in the bond market can still rearrange stock prices. For how this fits with the Fed and the rest of the rate picture, see the [Treasury yields section of our guide to what moves the stock market](/#treasury-yields-and-the-yield-curve).

## What a 10-year Treasury note actually is

A Treasury note is a loan to the federal government. The 10-year version pays interest every six months, which works out to 20 coupon payments, and returns the face value at maturity. According to TreasuryDirect, the minimum purchase is $100, with additional amounts in $100 increments.

New notes are sold at regular auctions. In those auctions, competitive bidders name the yield they will accept, and every winning bidder gets the same price, based on the highest accepted yield. Treasury's tentative schedule shows a new 10-year note auctioned on November 10, 2026, with a reopening of that issue set for December 8.

After the auction, the notes trade freely. That trading is what moves the yield you see quoted on financial sites. The Federal Reserve publishes a daily "constant maturity" 10-year yield in its H.15 release, which smooths across actual securities to estimate what a note with exactly ten years left would yield. That is the figure most people mean when they say "the 10-year."

## Why yields and prices move in opposite directions

This trips up a lot of new investors, so it is worth slowing down. Once a note is issued, its coupon never changes. If market rates rise after you buy, new notes pay more than yours, and nobody will pay full price for your lower coupon. Your note's price falls until its effective yield matches what the market now demands.

Investor.gov walks through a clean example. Take a $1,000 bond with a 3 percent coupon and ten years to maturity. A year later, market rates have climbed to 4 percent. The bond's price falls to $925, and its yield to maturity rises to 4 percent. The coupon didn't move. The price did the adjusting.

How much the price moves depends on duration. FINRA's rule of thumb is that a bond's price moves in the opposite direction by roughly its duration number for each 1 percentage point change in rates. A bond with a duration of 10 would lose about 10 percent if rates rose a full point. That is why long-term bonds feel so much riskier than Treasury bills when rates are climbing.

So when you read that "the 10-year yield jumped," it also means 10-year note prices fell, and anyone holding them took a loss on paper.

## What drives the 10-year Treasury yield

Economists usually break a long-term yield into two parts. Researchers at the New York Fed put it this way: Treasury yields can be decomposed into expected future short-term rates and the term premium, which they describe as compensation investors require for bearing the risk that short-term rates won't evolve as expected.

In practice, four forces show up again and again:

| Driver | What it means | Effect on the 10-year yield |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Expected Fed policy | Where traders think the Fed's overnight rate will average over the next decade | Higher expected rates push the yield up |
| Inflation expectations | How much inflation lenders expect to eat into their returns | Higher expected inflation pushes the yield up |
| Term premium | Extra pay for the risk of locking money up for ten years | Rises with uncertainty, heavy supply or volatility |
| Demand for safety | Investors fleeing to Treasuries in a crisis | Strong demand pushes prices up and yields down |

You can see part of this split in public data. The Treasury also issues inflation-protected securities (TIPS), and the gap between the regular 10-year yield and the TIPS yield is a market-based measure of expected inflation, which FRED publishes as the 10-year breakeven rate.

| November 13, 2026 | Yield |
| --- | --- |
| 10-year Treasury (nominal) | 5.15% |
| 10-year TIPS (real yield) | 2.91% |
| 10-year breakeven inflation | 2.24% |

*Sources: FRED, Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, series [DGS10](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DGS10), [DFII10](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DFII10) and [T10YIE](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/T10YIE).*

That breakdown tells a useful story. Expected inflation over the next decade is running a little above 2 percent. Most of the 10-year yield is the real, inflation-adjusted return investors are demanding, and that real yield is what climbed sharply this month.

Here is how the past week looked in the Federal Reserve's daily data:

| Date (2026) | 10-year nominal | 10-year TIPS (real) | 2-year |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| November 9 | 4.88% | 2.66% | 4.56% |
| November 10 | 4.98% | 2.78% | 4.73% |
| November 12 | 5.10% | 2.86% | 4.86% |
| November 13 | 5.15% | 2.91% | 4.88% |

*Source: FRED series [DGS10](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DGS10), [DFII10](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DFII10) and [DGS2](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DGS2). FRED shows no value for November 11, Veterans Day.*

From November 9 to November 13, the 10-year yield rose 27 basis points (a basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point). The real yield rose 25 basis points over the same stretch. In other words, nearly all of the move came from the real component, not from a jump in expected inflation. The 2-year yield, which tracks expected Fed policy more closely, rose even more.

How would an analyst read that? A move led by short-term and real yields usually points to traders pricing a tighter or longer-lasting Fed path, rather than panic about inflation. It is the pattern our [yield curve explainer](/articles/yield-curve-inversion-explained/) calls bear flattening. It doesn't tell you the economy is about to break. It does tell you money just got more expensive across the board, and that matters for everything priced off this benchmark.

## Mortgage rates and the 10-year

Most American mortgages run 30 years, so why does the 10-year note get the attention? The St. Louis Fed's answer is that borrowers often sell, refinance or pay off early, so the average mortgage lasts much closer to ten years. The bank sums up the relationship as a simple formula: mortgage rate is roughly the Treasury yield plus a mortgage spread.

That spread is not fixed. A May 2026 analysis from the Boston Fed found it ranged from more than 300 basis points during the 2007 to 2009 financial crisis to under 100 basis points in 2021. It covers the guarantee fees Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac charge, lender costs and profit, and the value of the borrower's option to prepay. The Boston Fed found that the part tied to that prepayment option tends to widen when interest rate volatility rises and narrow when the yield curve steepens.

Here is where things stand now:

| Measure | Latest reading |
| --- | --- |
| Freddie Mac 30-year fixed average (week of November 12, 2026) | 7.11% |
| Same measure one year earlier | 6.24% |
| 10-year Treasury yield, November 12, 2026 | 5.10% |
| Gap between the two | About 2.0 percentage points |

*Sources: [Freddie Mac Primary Mortgage Market Survey](https://www.freddiemac.com/pmms); FRED series [DGS10](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DGS10).*

The practical takeaway: when the 10-year climbs, mortgage rates tend to move the same way, though changes in the spread can cushion or amplify the move. That flows straight into homebuilder stocks, mortgage lenders, and the home improvement and furniture companies that depend on housing turnover.

## Treasury yields and stocks: the three channels

The 10-year yield reaches stock prices in a few distinct ways.

**1. The discount rate.** A stock is worth the present value of its future profits. The 10-year yield is a common starting point for the rate used to discount those profits. When it rises, distant profits are worth less today. Companies whose earnings are expected far in the future, typically fast-growing tech names, feel it most. We walk through the math in [how interest rates affect stock valuations](/articles/how-interest-rates-affect-stock-valuations/).

**2. Competition for money.** When a risk-free government note pays above 5 percent, some investors who reached for stocks or high-yield bonds to earn income can get a solid return without that risk. That doesn't force anyone to sell stocks, but it raises the bar stocks have to clear.

**3. Borrowing costs.** Corporate bonds, mortgages and many business loans price off Treasury yields. Higher yields raise interest expense, especially for heavily indebted companies and smaller firms that refinance often. Real estate investment trusts and utilities, which borrow heavily and pay steady dividends, are classic rate-sensitive groups.

Here is the nuance that headlines tend to skip: *why* the yield moved matters as much as how far. If yields rise because the economy is strong and earnings are growing, stocks can climb right alongside them. If yields rise because investors fear inflation or demand a bigger term premium, earnings don't get any help, and valuations take the hit. That is part of why stocks and bonds sometimes fall together, a pattern our guide to [how inflation affects stocks](/articles/how-does-inflation-affect-stocks/) explores.

Falling yields aren't automatically good news either. A rapid drop driven by a flight to safety often comes with a sell-off in stocks, because investors are buying Treasuries precisely because they're worried.

## How to read the 10-year without overreacting

1. **Check the level and the speed.** A yield that drifts up 25 basis points over a quarter is a different story from one that jumps that much in a week.
2. **Split it into real yield and breakeven.** FRED's DFII10 and T10YIE series show whether a move came from inflation fears or from real rates.
3. **Compare it with the 2-year.** If the 2-year moved more, Fed expectations are likely driving. If the 10-year moved more, look at inflation, supply or the term premium.
4. **Watch auction results.** Weak demand at a 10-year auction can push yields up on the day. Treasury's schedule tells you when they're coming.
5. **Match the move to the news.** Big shifts usually line up with [CPI reports](/articles/what-is-core-cpi/), the [jobs report](/articles/how-the-jobs-report-moves-the-stock-market/) or a Fed decision.
