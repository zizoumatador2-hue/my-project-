**Quantitative tightening (QT) is when the Federal Reserve shrinks its balance sheet by letting the bonds it owns mature without buying replacements. That drains reserves from the banking system and removes a large buyer from the bond market. It is the reverse of quantitative easing, and the Fed used it in 2017-2019 and again from June 2022 to December 2025.**

If you want to know what is quantitative tightening in plain terms, think of the Fed as the largest single holder of U.S. Treasury debt deciding to quietly step back from the market. Interest rate decisions get the headlines, but the size of the Fed's balance sheet is a second lever, and it works on longer-term yields and market liquidity. It belongs on any list of [what moves the stock market](/#the-federal-reserve-the-most-powerful-market-trigger), even when it moves slowly.

## QE first: how the balance sheet got so big

You can't understand QT without its mirror image. During crises, when the Fed has already cut short-term rates close to zero, it can buy Treasury securities and agency mortgage-backed securities (MBS) in bulk. That is quantitative easing. The Fed pays for those bonds by crediting banks' reserve accounts, so its assets (the bonds) and its liabilities (bank reserves) grow together.

QE does two things markets care about. It pushes down longer-term yields, because a giant buyer is soaking up supply. And it floods the system with reserves, the cash banks hold at the Fed. Both tend to support stock prices: lower yields make future earnings worth more today, and abundant cash finds its way into risk assets.

The trouble is that a central bank can't keep buying forever. Once the emergency passes and inflation becomes the bigger worry, the Fed wants to unwind some of that support. QT is how it does that without dumping bonds on the market.

## How quantitative tightening actually works

Here's the mechanism, and it is less dramatic than it sounds. When a Treasury note the Fed owns matures, the Treasury pays the Fed the principal. Normally the Fed rolls that money into new securities. Under QT, it stops doing that, at least up to a monthly limit.

The Fed's 2014 normalization principles set the template: reduce holdings "in a gradual and predictable manner primarily by ceasing to reinvest repayments of principal." The 2022 plan spelled out the cap mechanics. Principal payments would be reinvested only "to the extent that they exceed monthly caps." So if $70 billion of Treasuries matured in a month and the cap was $60 billion, the Fed let $60 billion roll off and reinvested the other $10 billion.

When that principal is not reinvested, the money effectively disappears from the system. The Fed's own Q&A puts it simply: runoff "typically leads to a reduction in reserve balances held by banks." Meanwhile, the Treasury still has to fund the government, so it sells more debt to private investors to replace what the Fed no longer holds. Private buyers have to absorb that extra supply.

| | Quantitative easing (QE) | Quantitative tightening (QT) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What the Fed does | Buys Treasuries and MBS | Lets holdings mature without replacing them, up to caps |
| Balance sheet | Grows | Shrinks |
| Bank reserves | Rise | Fall |
| Pressure on longer-term yields | Downward | Upward, usually modest |
| Typical setting | Crisis or weak economy, rates near zero | Recovery, inflation concerns |
| Policy signal | Easing | Tightening, in the background |

## The two U.S. rounds of QT

The U.S. has run the experiment twice, and the second round was much faster than the first.

**QT1 (2017-2019).** A June 2017 addendum to the Fed's normalization plans set the caps: $6 billion a month for Treasuries and $4 billion for agency securities to start, rising every three months until they reached $30 billion and $20 billion. In September 2017, the FOMC agreed to start the program in October. It didn't last long. In July 2019, the Committee said it would stop the runoff, with reinvestment beginning in August. By October 2019, the Fed was buying Treasury bills again to keep reserves ample.

**QT2 (2022-2025).** After the pandemic-era buying spree, the Fed issued principles in January 2022 and a detailed plan on May 4, 2022. Runoff began June 1, 2022. Caps started at $30 billion a month for Treasuries and $17.5 billion for agency debt and MBS, and after three months they doubled to $60 billion and $35 billion. The Fed's Q&A says conditions "warranted a faster pace of balance sheet runoff than over the 2017-2019 period."

Then the Fed began easing off the brake. Starting in June 2024, it cut the Treasury cap from $60 billion to $25 billion, keeping the MBS cap at $35 billion. Starting in April 2025, it cut the Treasury cap again, to $5 billion. On October 29, 2025, the FOMC announced it would end runoff on December 1, 2025.

| QT round | Start | Peak monthly caps (Treasuries / agency) | End of runoff |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| QT1 | October 2017 | $30B / $20B | Runoff stopped; reinvestment began August 2019 |
| QT2 | June 1, 2022 | $60B / $35B | December 1, 2025 |
| QT2 slowdown 1 | June 2024 | $25B / $35B | |
| QT2 slowdown 2 | April 2025 | $5B / $35B | |

*Sources: Federal Reserve normalization history and FOMC statements of May 1, 2024 and March 19, 2025.*

The scorecard for QT2 is large. According to the Fed, holdings fell by more than $2.2 trillion from June 2022, about $1.6 trillion in Treasuries and $600 billion in agency MBS. Measured against the economy, the securities holdings fell from 33 percent of nominal GDP to 20 percent.

## Why the Fed stopped

QT has a natural limit. The banking system needs a certain level of reserves to work smoothly, and the Fed runs what it calls an "ample reserves" framework. Drain too much and short-term funding markets get tight: banks hoard cash, overnight borrowing rates jump, and the Fed can lose clean control of the federal funds rate.

The trick is that nobody knows the exact floor in advance. As Julie Remache of the New York Fed put it in a February 2026 speech, "ample is not a fixed point." So the Fed slowed runoff in stages, watched money markets, and stopped while reserves were still comfortable. That is why the cap went from $60 billion to $25 billion to $5 billion before ending. The slowdowns were about landing the plane, not about loosening policy.

Year-end 2025 showed why the caution made sense. Funding pressures spiked around December 31, and use of the Fed's standing repo facility reached $75 billion that day, the highest since the standing operations were introduced in 2021, according to the New York Fed's Teller Window blog. The same post noted the pressures were "short-lived" and overall market functioning stayed orderly.

## After QT: reserve management purchases

QT ending did not mean QE started again. Two things changed instead.

First, the Fed began rolling over maturing Treasuries in full and reinvesting MBS principal into Treasury bills. MBS holdings keep shrinking as homeowners pay down and refinance mortgages, but the cash now goes into short-term government debt. Over time, that tilts the balance sheet toward Treasuries.

Second, at its December 2025 meeting the FOMC directed the New York Fed to begin reserve management purchases, or RMPs, of Treasury bills. Purchases started December 12, 2025, at about $40 billion for the first monthly period. The New York Fed said the pace would stay elevated for a few months to offset expected large increases in other Fed liabilities in April, when the Treasury's cash account was expected to peak, and then likely be "significantly reduced."

The Fed is careful to separate this from QE. In Remache's words, RMPs "are designed to preserve effective rate control and do not represent a change in the stance of monetary policy." QE buys longer-term bonds to push down long yields. RMPs buy short-term bills to keep reserves from drifting too low as other Fed liabilities, like currency and the Treasury's account, grow.

Where does that leave the balance sheet now? FRED data show total Fed assets at about $6.74 trillion on November 18, 2026. The Fed's H.4.1 release for that week lists roughly $4.59 trillion of Treasury securities, up about $395 billion from a year earlier, and roughly $1.88 trillion of MBS, down about $186 billion. Reserve balances stood near $2.98 trillion. In other words, the total is roughly flat to slightly higher, but the mix keeps shifting away from mortgages.

A useful detail for 2026: the Fed raised its target range to 3.75 to 4 percent in September and held it there in October, even as the balance sheet grew modestly. That combination confuses people. It's the clearest sign that, for now, the Fed treats the rate as the policy tool and the balance sheet as plumbing.

## How QT can move markets

QT's effects on stocks run through a few channels, and none of them are as fast as a rate surprise.

**Longer-term yields.** When the Fed steps back from buying, private investors must hold more Treasuries. To be persuaded, they may demand a bit more yield for locking up money for ten years, the part of the yield known as the term premium. Higher long yields raise the discount rate used to value future profits, which hits long-duration growth stocks hardest. We walk through that math in [how interest rates affect stock valuations](/articles/how-interest-rates-affect-stock-valuations/), and the [10-year Treasury yield explainer](/articles/what-is-the-10-year-treasury-yield/) shows why that one rate matters so much.

**Liquidity.** Fewer reserves mean less spare cash sloshing around the financial system. Traders often talk about "liquidity" driving stocks, and QT is the reverse of the QE tailwind they credit for past rallies. The effect is real but hard to measure, and it shows up most clearly when funding markets get stressed.

**Mortgages and housing.** As the Fed lets MBS roll off, private investors need to absorb more mortgage bonds. That can widen the gap between mortgage rates and Treasury yields, which matters for homebuilders, lenders and housing-linked stocks.

**Signaling.** QT starts and stops are announced months ahead and repeated at every meeting. That predictability is deliberate. Markets usually react more to changes in the plan, such as a faster pace or an earlier end, than to the runoff itself.

| QT development | Typical market read |
| --- | --- |
| Fed announces a faster pace or higher caps | Mild upward pressure on long yields; tighter financial conditions |
| Fed slows or ends runoff | Less pressure on long yields; read as easing funding strain, not a policy pivot |
| Money market rates spike above the Fed's range | Sign reserves may be getting scarce; Fed may adjust purchases |
| Large bill purchases begin | Supports reserves; usually not treated as stimulus |

The honest summary: QT is a slow-moving headwind, not a crash trigger. It rarely explains a single day's move. Over months, it tilts the playing field toward higher yields and tighter conditions, which is why bond and stock markets can sometimes fall together, a pattern we cover in [why stocks and bonds sometimes fall together](/articles/stocks-and-bonds-falling-together/).

## Common misconceptions

**"QT means the Fed is selling bonds."** In both U.S. rounds, it hasn't. The Fed let securities mature and simply didn't replace them.

**"The balance sheet growing again means QE is back."** Not necessarily. Bill purchases aimed at keeping reserves ample are a technical operation. QE is about pushing down longer-term rates.

**"QT and rate hikes are the same thing."** They point in the same direction, but rates work on borrowing costs across the economy immediately, while QT works gradually through bond supply and reserves. In 2026, the Fed has been raising rates without restarting QT.
