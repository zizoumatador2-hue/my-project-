**The jobs report affects the stock market mainly through what it signals about the Federal Reserve. Nonfarm payrolls, the unemployment rate and average hourly earnings tell traders whether the economy is overheating or cooling. That shifts expectations for interest rates, which move Treasury yields first and stocks right after, often within minutes of the 8:30 a.m. release.**

Few scheduled numbers carry as much weight as the first-Friday jobs report. It covers the whole labor market in one release, it arrives before the opening bell, and it lands squarely on half of the Fed's mandate. If you want the wider framework for how jobs data fits alongside inflation, the Fed and earnings, start with the [employment section of our guide to what moves the stock market](/#employment-the-jobs-report-and-weekly-claims). This piece goes deeper on the report itself: what's in it, how to read it, and why the market's reaction so often looks backward at first glance.

## What's in the monthly jobs report

The official name is the Employment Situation, and it's published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. It is really two reports stapled together, built from two separate surveys.

The **establishment survey** asks employers how many people are on their payrolls. According to the [BLS technical note](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.htm), it covers about 119,000 businesses and government agencies, representing roughly 622,000 worksites. This is where the headline number comes from: nonfarm payrolls, plus average hourly earnings and the average workweek.

The **household survey**, run by the Census Bureau for BLS, interviews about 60,000 households. It asks people whether they're working, looking for work or neither. This survey produces the unemployment rate, the labor force participation rate and the employment-population ratio.

Both surveys refer to the same window: the pay period or calendar week that includes the 12th of the month. Because the samples and methods differ, the two can disagree for a month or two. Payrolls might rise while household employment falls, or the reverse. That isn't an error. It's a reason to look at both before drawing conclusions.

| Measure | Survey | What it tells you | Why markets care |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nonfarm payrolls | Establishment | Net jobs added or lost by employers | The headline; the pace of hiring |
| Revisions to prior two months | Establishment | Corrections as more employers report | Can change the trend more than the new month |
| Average hourly earnings | Establishment | Pay growth per hour worked | A read on wage-driven inflation |
| Average workweek | Establishment | Hours worked per week | Employers often cut hours before jobs |
| Unemployment rate | Household | Share of the labor force without a job and looking | Recession signal; core of the Fed's mandate |
| Participation rate | Household | Share of adults working or looking | Explains why unemployment moved |

## How does the jobs report affect the stock market?

The short answer: through the Fed and through earnings, and the two can pull in opposite directions.

Congress gave the Federal Reserve two goals, maximum employment and stable prices, which the Fed describes as its ["dual mandate"](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/what-economic-goals-does-federal-reserve-seek-to-achieve-through-monetary-policy.htm). The jobs report is the most complete monthly read on the first goal, and through wages, it says something about the second. So every number in it gets translated, almost instantly, into a guess about the Fed's next move.

Here's the typical chain:

1. **Bond yields move first.** Short-term Treasury yields, especially the 2-year, track expectations for the federal funds rate. A strong report usually pushes them up. A weak one pulls them down.
2. **Rate odds reprice.** Futures-based tools like the [CME FedWatch Tool](https://www.cmegroup.com/markets/interest-rates/cme-fedwatch-tool.html) show traders adjusting the probability of a cut or hike at upcoming meetings.
3. **Stocks react to both rates and growth.** Higher expected rates lower the present value of future profits, a mechanism we walk through in [how interest rates affect stock valuations](/articles/how-interest-rates-affect-stock-valuations/). But stronger hiring also means more paychecks and more spending, which supports company revenue.

Which force wins depends on the mood of the market. When inflation is the main worry, a blowout payroll number can send stocks down, because it makes rate cuts less likely. When recession is the worry, the same number can send stocks up, because it suggests the economy is holding together. And a very weak report can hurt stocks even if it raises the odds of a cut, because the market starts pricing in falling earnings.

That's why you'll hear the phrase "good news is bad news." It isn't a law. It's a description of one particular regime, when the Fed is fighting inflation and investors care more about rates than growth.

## Reading the September 2026 report

The most recent report, released October 2, 2026, is a good example of why the headline alone can mislead. Here are the key figures from the [BLS release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.htm):

| September 2026 Employment Situation | Value | Prior month |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nonfarm payrolls | +29,000 | +133,000 (revised from +162,000) |
| July payrolls (revised) | -10,000 | Previously +21,000 |
| Unemployment rate | 4.2% | 4.1% |
| Labor force participation rate | 61.8% | 61.6% |
| Employment-population ratio | 59.2% | 59.1% |
| Average hourly earnings, monthly change | +0.1% ($37.81) | n/a |
| Average hourly earnings, 12-month change | +3.0% | n/a |
| Average workweek | 34.4 hours | 34.4 hours |

*Source: [BLS Employment Situation, September 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.htm).*

Several things stand out once you read past the top line.

**The revisions mattered as much as the new number.** July and August were cut by a combined 60,000 jobs, and July turned negative. Averaging the three months as now reported gives roughly 51,000 jobs a month. That's a much softer trend than August's first print of 162,000 suggested.

**The unemployment rate rose for a reason that isn't all bad.** It ticked up to 4.2 percent, but participation and the employment-population ratio both rose too. More people entered the labor force, and some hadn't found work yet. That's a different story from a rise driven by layoffs.

**Wage growth cooled.** Average hourly earnings rose just 0.1 percent for the month and 3.0 percent over the year. For comparison, the [September CPI](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.nr0.htm) showed consumer prices up 3.7 percent from a year earlier, so hourly pay was not keeping up with headline inflation. That gap matters for consumer spending, and it also undercuts the idea that wages are fueling inflation.

**Hiring was narrow.** Health care added 17,000 jobs, construction 11,000 and manufacturing 9,000, while financial activities lost 7,000. BLS described most other major industries as little changed.

Put together, the report pointed to a labor market losing momentum without collapsing. That left the Fed in an awkward spot, with inflation running well above its 2 percent goal. At its October 27-28 meeting, the FOMC [held the federal funds rate at 3-3/4 to 4 percent](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/files/monetary20261028a1.pdf), saying job gains "have kept pace with the workforce" and unemployment "has changed little." Two officials dissented in favor of a quarter-point hike. A soft jobs market and stubborn inflation pulling the Fed in opposite directions is exactly the setup in which each new jobs report can move markets sharply.

## Why revisions and benchmarks deserve your attention

New investors often treat the first payroll number as fact. It's an estimate, and BLS says so.

Each month, the agency revises the prior two months as late survey responses come in. On top of that, once a year BLS benchmarks payrolls to near-complete counts from unemployment insurance tax records. The [technical note](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.htm) puts the average absolute benchmark revision over the prior 10 years at 0.2 percent of total employment, which sounds tiny until you remember total payrolls are well over 150 million.

Some years are much bigger. The benchmark for March 2025 lowered the seasonally adjusted payroll level by 898,000 jobs, or 0.6 percent, according to [BLS](https://www.bls.gov/web/empsit/cesbmart.htm). The preliminary estimate for March 2026 was far smaller, at -79,000, or -0.1 percent, and the final version will be folded in with the January 2027 report, published in February 2027 ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/prebmk_08282026.htm)).

There's also plain sampling noise. BLS says a monthly change in total nonfarm employment needs to be larger than about 122,000 to be statistically significant at its 90 percent confidence level, and the unemployment rate needs to move by about 0.3 percentage point. A 30,000-job miss against the forecast is, statistically speaking, inside the margin of error. Markets react to it anyway, because traders are reacting to what the number does to expectations, not to its confidence interval.

## A real example: the August 2024 jobs report

On August 2, 2024, BLS reported that payrolls rose by 114,000 in July 2024 and the unemployment rate climbed to 4.3 percent from 4.1 percent, compared with 3.5 percent a year earlier ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/empsit_08022024.pdf)). May and June were revised down by a combined 29,000. Average hourly earnings rose 0.2 percent for the month and 3.6 percent over the year.

Compared with the September 2026 report, those numbers weren't dramatic. What made the release a market event was the direction of travel. The jobless rate had been drifting higher for months, and this report raised the question of whether the labor market was cracking rather than cooling. BLS also addressed Hurricane Beryl, which hit Texas during the survey week, and said it had no discernible effect on the national figures, which took one easy excuse off the table.

Six weeks later, on September 18, 2024, the FOMC [cut its target range by half a percentage point, to 4-3/4 to 5 percent](https://federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20240918a.htm). Its statement noted that "job gains have slowed, and the unemployment rate has moved up but remains low." The jobs data didn't decide that move by itself, since inflation had been easing too, but it shifted the Fed's attention from the price side of its mandate toward the employment side.

The lesson for investors: a single rise of two-tenths in the unemployment rate can matter a lot when it confirms a trend the market was already nervous about. Context sets the size of the reaction. ## Which parts of the market react most

Not every stock responds to the jobs report the same way. Some general patterns, which hold more often than not but are far from guaranteed:

- **Rate-sensitive sectors** such as utilities, real estate and homebuilders tend to move opposite to Treasury yields. A report that pushes yields up often weighs on them.
- **Long-duration growth stocks**, whose value rests heavily on profits far in the future, are usually more sensitive to rate expectations than mature, cash-generating companies.
- **Banks** sit in the middle. Stronger growth supports loan demand and credit quality, while the shape of the yield curve affects their lending margins.
- **Consumer discretionary companies**, from retailers to restaurants, care about the paychecks behind the numbers. Weak wage growth or rising unemployment can hit their earnings outlook directly.
- **Small caps** often react more than large caps because they tend to rely more on the domestic economy and on floating-rate borrowing.

The dollar typically moves with Treasury yields after the report, which matters for large U.S. multinationals that earn much of their revenue abroad.

## How to read the jobs report on release day

You don't need a trading desk to make sense of the report. A simple checklist covers most of what professionals look at in the first few minutes:

- **Compare payrolls with the consensus forecast**, not with zero. Markets trade the surprise.
- **Add up the revisions.** Net the new month against changes to the prior two months to see how the picture really shifted.
- **Look at the three-month average** to smooth out a noisy single month.
- **Check why unemployment moved.** Pair it with the participation rate. Rising unemployment with rising participation reads differently from rising unemployment with falling employment.
- **Watch wages and hours together.** Average hourly earnings speak to inflation pressure; a shrinking workweek can be an early sign of softening demand for labor.
- **Scan the industry breakdown.** Broad gains signal a healthier market than gains concentrated in one or two sectors.
- **Then look at the 2-year Treasury yield** to see how the market interpreted all of the above.
