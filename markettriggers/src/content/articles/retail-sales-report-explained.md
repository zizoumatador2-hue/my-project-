**The retail sales report explained in one line: it is the Census Bureau's monthly estimate of what Americans spent at stores, restaurants and online sellers. Released about two weeks after each month ends, it is one of the earliest reads on consumer spending, so traders use it to update forecasts for growth, earnings and interest rates.**

Most economic data arrives slowly. GDP comes out a month after the quarter ends, and the detailed spending numbers lag too. Retail sales show up fast, and they answer the question every retailer, economist and Fed official keeps asking: are consumers still spending? For the bigger picture of how spending data fits with jobs, inflation and growth reports, see the [consumer spending section of our guide to what moves the stock market](/#consumer-spending-and-sentiment).

## Retail sales report explained: what it measures

The official name is the Advance Monthly Sales for Retail and Food Services, often shortened to MARTS. The Census Bureau surveys a sample of about 4,800 retail and food services firms, drawn from its larger Monthly Retail Trade Survey, and weights those answers to represent a universe of more than three million businesses, according to the methodology notes in the [September 2026 release](https://www.census.gov/retail/marts/www/marts_current.pdf).

A few details shape how the numbers should be read:

- **It counts dollars, not units.** The headline figures are seasonally adjusted but *not* adjusted for price changes. When prices climb, sales can rise even if shoppers walk out with the same amount of stuff.
- **It is mostly goods.** Stores, car dealers, gas stations and online sellers sell physical products. Restaurants and bars are the main service category included. Rent, health care, insurance, airfare and most other services are outside the report.
- **"Advance" means early.** The first estimate comes from a subsample, so it has a margin of error and gets revised the following month, and again when annual benchmarks arrive.
- **Online sales live in "nonstore retailers."** That category covers e-commerce and other sellers without a physical storefront. It does not capture every online purchase, since a big-box chain's web orders may be counted with its stores.

Total sales came to $745.0 billion for the month. The monthly gain carried a margin of error of plus or minus 0.4 percentage points, so the Census Bureau considered it statistically significant. August was revised up to a 1.3 percent gain from the 1.1 percent first reported.

## The retail sales control group

If you read market coverage on report day, you will see the phrase "control group" almost as often as the headline. The Census Bureau doesn't publish it. The concept comes from the Bureau of Economic Analysis, which builds much of its estimate of consumer goods spending by tracking matching categories in the Census retail data.

A [Richmond Fed explainer on "core" retail sales](https://www.richmondfed.org/research/national_economy/macro_minute/2023/mm_05_30_23) lays out why four categories are stripped out:

| Excluded category | Why it is left out |
| --- | --- |
| Motor vehicle and parts dealers | BEA uses manufacturer and industry data on car sales, which are more complete than the dealer sample |
| Gasoline stations | BEA uses Department of Energy data, partly because a lot of fuel is sold outside gas stations |
| Building materials stores | BEA treats many of these purchases as residential investment, not consumption |
| Food services and drinking places | A service rather than a good; BEA uses this data for its estimate of spending on purchased meals and beverages |

What remains, the control group, should roughly track nominal consumer spending on goods outside food, energy and vehicles. That's why economists often revise their GDP estimates within minutes of the release. A strong headline driven by gas prices tells you little about the economy. A strong control group tells you more.

In September 2026 the gap between those two views was wide. Using the seasonally adjusted dollar figures in Table 1 of the Census release, the control group works out to about $400.2 billion, up roughly 0.1 percent from August's $399.6 billion. That figure is our own arithmetic from the published table, and analysts' calculations can differ slightly as data are revised. Either way, it is a much quieter number than the 0.8 percent headline.

## Why the September report looked stronger than it was

The September release is a good lesson in reading past the headline. Three numbers explain most of it.

First, gas stations. Their sales jumped 6.4 percent in the month and 28.1 percent over the year. The [September Consumer Price Index](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_10142026.htm) showed gasoline prices up 9.1 percent for the month and 34.2 percent over 12 months. Gas station sales rose by less than pump prices did, which hints that drivers bought somewhat fewer gallons, or shifted some spending to the snacks and coffee inside the store. Either way, the jump in that category was a price story, not a sign of booming demand.

Second, the "excluding gas" line. Strip out gas stations and total sales rose just 0.2 percent for the month. That's the number that better reflects what households did with everything else in their budget.

Third, inflation. Total sales were up 6.3 percent from a year earlier, but the CPI was up 3.7 percent over the same period. Because retail sales aren't adjusted for prices, a big chunk of that annual gain reflects higher prices rather than more purchases. Retail sales and the CPI cover different baskets, so you can't subtract one from the other precisely, but the comparison is a useful reality check. Our explainer on [how inflation affects stocks](/articles/how-does-inflation-affect-stocks/) covers why that distinction matters for corporate earnings.

None of this makes the report weak. Restaurant spending rose 1.1 percent, furniture stores gained 1.3 percent, and the three-month total from July through September was up 5.5 percent from a year earlier. It just means the headline overstated the strength of underlying demand.

## How retail sales can move stocks and bonds

Retail sales move markets through the same basic channel as other economic data: surprises change expectations. A report in line with forecasts often barely registers. A big miss in either direction can move several markets at once.

**Treasury yields.** A hot report suggests the economy can handle higher interest rates, and it can raise worries that strong demand will keep inflation sticky. Bond traders may then push back the expected timing of Fed rate cuts, which lifts yields, especially on two-year notes. At its October 27-28 meeting, the FOMC [held the federal funds rate at 3-3/4 to 4 percent](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/files/monetary20261028a1.pdf), so each spending report feeds the debate over its next move. We explain why higher yields weigh on stock prices in [how interest rates affect stock valuations](/articles/how-interest-rates-affect-stock-valuations/).

**The broad stock market.** Here the reaction is less predictable. Strong spending is good for corporate revenue, but if it pushes yields up sharply, the rate effect can win out. Weak spending can lift stocks if traders think it will bring rate cuts sooner, or sink them if it looks like the start of a slowdown. The same tension shows up with the [monthly jobs report](/articles/how-the-jobs-report-moves-the-stock-market/).

**Consumer stocks.** Retailers, restaurant chains, home furnishing companies and e-commerce firms tend to react most directly, because the categories map onto their businesses. A weak month for building materials stores, for example, can draw attention to home improvement chains. Category data is noisy, though, and a single company's sales can diverge from its category.

### Which sectors feel it

| If the report shows... | Sectors often in focus | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strong control group | Consumer discretionary, retailers, payment networks | Broad demand for goods and card spending |
| Strong restaurant sales | Restaurant chains, travel and leisure | Signals willingness to spend on experiences |
| Gains driven by gas prices | Energy producers; pressure on other consumer names | Fuel costs can crowd out other spending |
| Weak spending overall | Consumer staples, utilities (relative strength) | Investors shift toward steadier demand |

These are tendencies, not rules. The market's reaction depends on positioning, the rest of that week's data and what was already priced in.

## Revisions: the number that changes

The advance estimate is a first draft. The September release revised August's gain from 1.1 percent to 1.3 percent, and the Census Bureau also revised months of history on September 28, 2026, using results from its annual surveys.

That matters for anyone reading the report. A modest headline paired with a big upward revision to the prior month can add up to a stronger picture than the headline suggests. The reverse is also true. A good habit is to look at the two-month or three-month trend, not one print.

## Holiday sales and seasonal adjustment

The holiday shopping season is when retail sales get the most attention, and it's also when the seasonal adjustment does the heaviest lifting. Spending naturally surges in November and December every year. The Census Bureau's seasonally adjusted figures try to remove that predictable bump, so a December "gain" means spending rose more than a typical December would explain.

That adjustment can be tricky. Thanksgiving's date moves, shopping promotions start earlier, and more buying shifts online. A weak adjusted reading for November followed by a strong one for December can simply mean shoppers shopped earlier or later than the model expected. Reading the two months together usually gives a cleaner picture than reacting to either alone. Company results add more context later, when retailers report their holiday quarters; our guide on [how to read an earnings report](/articles/how-to-read-an-earnings-report/) walks through the comparable-store sales figures they publish.

## How retail sales fit with other data

No single report tells the whole story. Retail sales mostly cover goods, while services make up a large share of what households spend. The broader measure is personal consumption expenditures from the Bureau of Economic Analysis, which includes health care, housing and other services. In the [advance estimate for third-quarter GDP](https://www.bea.gov/news/2026/gdp-advance-estimate-3rd-quarter-2026/), released October 29, real GDP grew at a 3.4 percent annual rate, with consumer spending on both goods and services increasing. Services growth was led by health care and by food services and accommodations, a category retail sales capture only partly.

It also helps to check spending against income. If people are spending more while weekly [initial jobless claims](/articles/initial-jobless-claims-explained/) climb, the strength may not last. If paychecks are growing, it is more likely to hold up. The inflation side comes from the CPI; our piece on [CPI vs. PCE](/articles/cpi-vs-pce/) explains how the two price measures differ.

## How to read a retail sales report in five minutes

1. **Start with the headline, then move straight to "excluding gasoline stations" and "excluding motor vehicles."** If those lines tell a different story, the headline is being driven by prices or car sales.
2. **Work out the control group.** Many financial news outlets report it; you can also calculate it from Table 1 of the release.
3. **Compare with the consensus forecast.** Markets react to the surprise, not the level.
4. **Check the revision to last month.** It can matter as much as the new number.
5. **Look at the margin of error.** A 0.1 percent change with a plus or minus 0.4 point range is statistically indistinguishable from zero.
6. **Put it next to the CPI.** Nominal growth minus inflation gives a rough sense of real spending.
