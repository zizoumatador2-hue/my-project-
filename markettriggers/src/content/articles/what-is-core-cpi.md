**Core CPI is the Consumer Price Index with food and energy prices removed. The Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes it every month as "all items less food and energy." Because grocery and gas prices swing so much, core gives investors and the Fed a cleaner view of the inflation trend, and it usually drives the market's reaction on CPI day.**

On the morning the CPI report comes out, two numbers flash across trading screens almost at once. The headline figure gets the newspaper headlines. The core figure tends to move bond yields. Knowing why those two can tell very different stories is one of the most useful skills for reading the economic calendar. For the bigger picture of how inflation data fits with jobs, rates and earnings, see the [inflation section of our guide to what moves the stock market](/#inflation-cpi-pce-and-ppi).

## What is core CPI, exactly?

The Consumer Price Index tracks the prices urban consumers pay for a fixed basket of goods and services, from rent and car insurance to haircuts and airline tickets. The broad version, CPI-U, is the "headline" number.

Core CPI takes that same basket and drops two groups: food (both groceries and restaurant meals) and energy (gasoline, fuel oil, electricity and utility gas). What remains is still most of the index. In the CPI's relative importance weights as of August 2026, the index for all items less food and energy accounted for about 79.1 percent of the basket, with food at about 13.5 percent and energy at about 7.4 percent, according to [BLS Table 1](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.t01.htm).

So core CPI isn't a niche slice. It's roughly four-fifths of what consumers buy, with the two most erratic categories set aside.

BLS itself is careful with the label. In its [CPI FAQ](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/questions-and-answers.htm), the agency explains that some users prefer the index excluding food and energy because those prices are relatively volatile, and they want a read on the underlying rate of inflation. "Core" is the market's nickname for it.

## Headline vs. core CPI: the latest numbers

The September 2026 report, released October 14, is a textbook case of why the distinction matters. Gasoline jumped, and headline inflation jumped with it. Core barely budged.

| September 2026 | Weight in CPI (Aug. 2026) | Change from August (seasonally adjusted) | Change from a year earlier |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| All items (headline) | 100.0% | +0.6% | +3.7% |
| Food | 13.5% | 0.0% | +2.5% |
| Energy | 7.4% | +5.2% | +20.4% |
| All items less food and energy (core) | 79.1% | +0.3% | +2.6% |
| Core goods (commodities less food and energy) | 18.8% | +0.3% | +0.7% |
| Core services (services less energy services) | 60.2% | +0.4% | +3.1% |
| Shelter | 35.3% | +0.4% | +3.2% |

*Source: [BLS CPI release and Table 1, September 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.nr0.htm). Weights are relative importance as of August 2026. Twelve-month changes are not seasonally adjusted.*

Gasoline alone rose 9.1 percent for the month and 34.2 percent over the year. That's enough to push headline inflation more than a full percentage point above core. Someone reading only the headline would think inflation had taken off. Someone reading only core would see a steady 0.3 percent monthly pace and a 12-month rate of 2.6 percent.

Neither view is wrong. They answer different questions. Headline tells you what happened to the cost of living last month. Core is a better guide to where inflation is likely to settle once the gas-price spike works through.

## Why food and energy get stripped out

The habit of excluding food and energy dates to the 1970s, when oil and crop shocks sent measured inflation lurching around. A [2019 Federal Reserve research note](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/comparing-two-measures-of-core-inflation-20190802.html) describes the approach as removing items judged more volatile and more exposed to one-off shocks. It also points out that the choice wasn't originally based on a formal statistical test of which prices are noisiest. It was a practical rule of thumb that stuck.

The logic is easiest to see from a central banker's chair. Former Fed Governor Frederic Mishkin laid it out in a 2007 speech, [Headline versus Core Inflation in the Conduct of Monetary Policy](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/mishkin20071020a.htm). Temporary supply shocks, like a drought or a refinery outage, raise food and energy prices relative to everything else. Those moves often reverse on their own. Changes in core inflation are much less likely to be reversed, so core gives a better first approximation of lasting changes in inflation. Reacting to every swing in the headline number, he argued, risks overreacting to things that fade.

Interest rates work with a lag. The Fed can't lower the price of crude oil next month by changing its policy rate today. What it can influence, over time, is the broad pace of price increases across services, goods and wages. Core is a closer proxy for that.

## Why the Fed watches core CPI, even though it targets PCE

Here's a detail that trips up a lot of investors: the Federal Reserve doesn't target the CPI at all. Its 2 percent goal is defined in terms of the PCE price index from the Bureau of Economic Analysis. We break down the differences in [CPI vs. PCE: which inflation number moves markets?](/articles/cpi-vs-pce/)

The same headline-versus-core split applies to PCE, though. In its [July 2026 Monetary Policy Report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/2026-07-mpr-part1.htm), the Fed notes that core inflation, which excludes food and energy items, "historically has been a better gauge of future inflation." That's the reason policymakers lean on core measures when they talk about the trend.

Core CPI matters to the Fed for a practical reason: timing. The CPI arrives about two weeks before PCE, and forecasters translate its components into estimates of core PCE well before BEA publishes the official number. In other words, core CPI is the Fed's first good look at the month, even if it isn't the final scorecard.

## What's inside core CPI

Core is dominated by services. In the August 2026 weights, services less energy services made up about 60.2 percent of the entire CPI, while core goods made up about 18.8 percent ([BLS Table 1](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.t01.htm)). That split shapes how core behaves.

- **Shelter** is the single biggest piece, at about 35.3 percent of the whole CPI. It includes rent and owners' equivalent rent, an estimate of what homeowners would pay to rent their own homes. Shelter tends to change slowly, which gives core much of its persistence. In September 2026 it rose 0.4 percent for the month.
- **Other core services** include medical care services, car insurance, airline fares, recreation and education. These often track wages, since labor is a big cost for service businesses. Airline fares rose 1.2 percent in September 2026, a reminder that core services aren't immune to fuel costs.
- **Core goods** cover new and used vehicles, apparel, furniture, appliances and the like. These swing with supply chains, tariffs and import prices. Used cars and trucks rose 0.7 percent in September 2026 but were down 1.5 percent from a year earlier.

When analysts dissect a core CPI print, they usually ask which of these three buckets drove it. A hot reading from shelter tells a different story than a hot reading from used cars, and it carries a different weight in PCE.

## How the CPI report moves markets

BLS releases the CPI at 8:30 a.m. Eastern, an hour before the stock market opens. What traders compare is not the number itself but the number against the consensus forecast. And the comparison that matters most is usually core CPI, month over month.

Why month over month? The 12-month rate includes 11 months the market already knows about. The monthly change is the only truly new information. A core reading of 0.4 percent against a 0.3 percent forecast can sound tiny, but annualized, the difference between those two paces is more than a full percentage point of inflation.

The usual chain reaction looks like this:

1. **Treasury yields move first.** Short-term yields, especially the 2-year, are the most sensitive to Fed expectations. A hotter core print tends to push them up; a cooler one pulls them down.
2. **Rate expectations reprice.** Futures-based tools like the [CME FedWatch Tool](https://www.cmegroup.com/markets/interest-rates/cme-fedwatch-tool.html) show traders adjusting the odds of rate moves at upcoming meetings.
3. **Stocks follow.** Higher expected rates lower the present value of future earnings, which typically hits long-duration growth stocks hardest. Our explainer on [how inflation affects stocks](/articles/how-does-inflation-affect-stocks/) covers the channels in detail, and [what happens to stocks when the Fed cuts rates](/articles/what-happens-to-stocks-when-the-fed-cuts-rates/) looks at the other side of the cycle.

A big headline surprise driven purely by gasoline often gets a muted reaction if core comes in on target. Markets have learned to look through it.

## A real example: August 2022

The clearest illustration in recent memory came in September 2022, when BLS released the August CPI. Gasoline prices had been falling hard. According to the [BLS release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_09132022.htm), gasoline dropped 10.6 percent for the month and the energy index fell 5.0 percent. Headline CPI rose just 0.1 percent.

On the surface, that looked like relief. Underneath, core CPI rose 0.6 percent for the month, double July's 0.3 percent, and the 12-month core rate climbed to 6.3 percent from 5.9 percent. Shelter rose 0.7 percent and accounted for about 40 percent of core's 12-month increase. Medical care, household furnishings, car insurance and new vehicles all rose too.

Falling gas prices had masked broad price pressure everywhere else. About a week later, on September 21, 2022, the FOMC [raised its target range for the federal funds rate to 3 to 3-1/4 percent](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20220921a.htm). Anyone who read only the headline number that month would have misjudged both the inflation picture and the Fed.

The reverse happened earlier that year. In June 2022, headline CPI rose 9.1 percent from a year earlier, the largest 12-month increase since November 1981, with energy up 41.6 percent. Core was up a smaller 5.9 percent ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_07132022.htm)). Energy was inflating the headline; core showed a serious, but less extreme, problem.

## Alternatives to core CPI

Excluding food and energy is a blunt tool. It always removes the same items, even in months when used cars or airline fares are the wild cards. That's why economists also follow measures that trim outliers instead.

The Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland publishes two of them from the same BLS data ([Cleveland Fed](https://www.clevelandfed.org/indicators-and-data/median-cpi)):

- **Median CPI** ranks every component by its price change and reports the one sitting at the middle of the spending-weighted distribution.
- **16% trimmed-mean CPI** drops the components in the top 8 percent and bottom 8 percent of price changes, then averages the rest.

| September 2026 | Monthly change | 12-month change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Headline CPI | +0.6% | +3.7% |
| Core CPI (less food and energy) | +0.3% | +2.6% |
| Median CPI | +0.2% | +2.6% |
| 16% trimmed-mean CPI | +0.3% | +2.7% |

*Sources: [BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.nr0.htm); [Cleveland Fed](https://www.clevelandfed.org/indicators-and-data/median-cpi).*

When all three underlying measures line up, as they roughly did in September 2026, you can be more confident the trend is real. When they diverge, it usually means one or two categories are doing the heavy lifting, and it pays to read the component tables.

## The limits of core CPI

Core is a tool, not a verdict. A few cautions:

- **Food and energy are real costs.** Households pay for groceries and gas. A measure that leaves them out can understate how inflation feels at the checkout line and the gas pump.
- **Shocks can leak into core.** Mishkin noted that if an energy shock persists, higher costs tend to push up core prices too. Airline fares and delivery costs are obvious channels.
- **No single measure catches everything.** As Mishkin put it, a core measure is no substitute for careful analysis. That's why the Fed looks at several.
- **Seasonal adjustment gets revised.** BLS re-estimates seasonal factors each year, and seasonally adjusted figures can be revised for up to five years, according to its [FAQ](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/questions-and-answers.htm). A single month's core print is a first estimate.

## How to read core CPI on release day

- **Start with core, month over month,** and compare it with the consensus forecast.
- **Check the 12-month core rate** for the trend, knowing it moves slowly.
- **Split it into shelter, other services and goods** to see what drove the number.
- **Cross-check the median and trimmed-mean CPI** for confirmation.
- **Think ahead to PCE.** A hot reading led by shelter matters less for core PCE than one led by medical services.
