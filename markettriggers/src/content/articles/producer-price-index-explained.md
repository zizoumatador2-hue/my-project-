**The producer price index (PPI) measures the average change in the prices that U.S. producers receive for the goods and services they sell. Published monthly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, it tracks inflation from the seller's side, before retail markups and sales taxes. Investors watch it for early signs of cost pressure that could later reach consumers.**

Most people meet inflation at the checkout counter, which is what the Consumer Price Index tries to capture. The PPI looks one step back, at the prices farms, refineries, factories, trucking companies and service firms get paid. It usually gets less attention than CPI, but when energy or commodity prices move fast, it can be the report that explains what is coming next. For the wider framework of how inflation data moves stocks and bonds, see the [inflation section of our guide to what moves the stock market](/#inflation-cpi-pce-and-ppi).

## What the producer price index measures

The BLS defines the PPI as a measure of "the average change over time in the selling prices received by domestic producers of goods and services." The key word is *received*. The price that counts is the revenue the producer actually collects, not what a shopper pays at the register.

That seller's-eye view leads to a few features that surprise people:

- **It is not just "wholesale" prices.** The PPI replaced the old Wholesale Price Index in 1978, and wholesale trade now makes up less than 8 percent of its coverage, according to a [BLS article on common PPI misconceptions](https://www.bls.gov/opub/btn/volume-13/misconceptions-ppi.htm). The nickname "wholesale inflation" stuck anyway.
- **Services are the bigger part.** Services indexes began in 1985, and by December 2023 they accounted for about 67.2 percent of the PPI for final demand. Hospitals, trucking, portfolio management and retail margins are all in there.
- **It is a big survey.** Each month the BLS solicits over 100,000 prices from roughly 25,000 reporters, per the [PPI FAQ](https://www.bls.gov/ppi/faqs/questions-and-answers.htm), and it publishes about 10,000 separate indexes.
- **Imports are excluded.** A good made overseas isn't produced by a domestic firm, so it falls outside the PPI's scope.

## How the PPI report is organized

Since January 2014, the BLS has organized the PPI around a structure called Final Demand-Intermediate Demand. It sounds technical, but the idea is simple. Final demand covers things sold to their last buyer: consumers, businesses buying equipment as capital investment, government and exporters. Intermediate demand covers goods, services and construction sold to businesses as inputs to make something else.

The headline number you see in the news is the final demand index. Here are the main pieces a reader will find in each release:

| PPI measure | What it covers | Why investors look at it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Final demand (headline) | Goods, services and construction sold to final buyers | The overall read on producer inflation |
| Final demand goods | Foods, energy and other goods | Sensitive to oil, crops and commodities |
| Final demand services | Trade, transportation and warehousing, and other services | The larger share of the index; slower to turn |
| Final demand less foods, energy and trade services | Headline minus its three most volatile parts | The closest thing to a "core" PPI trend |
| Intermediate demand | Inputs sold to businesses (processed, unprocessed, services) | A look further up the supply chain |

### What "trade services" means

Trade services trip up a lot of readers. The PPI doesn't measure what a retailer charges for a TV. It measures the retailer's *margin*, which the BLS defines as "the difference between the selling price for a good and the acquisition price to replace that same item." If a store pays more for its inventory but can't raise its shelf price, its margin shrinks, and the trade services index falls. That's why the BLS strips trade out of its preferred underlying measure: margins can swing a lot from month to month, especially when wholesale prices move quickly. Trade services made up about 19.2 percent of final demand as of December 2023.

## PPI vs. CPI: the key differences

The two indexes overlap but are not built to match. The BLS lays out the main gaps in its [comparison of the PPI and the CPI](https://www.bls.gov/ppi/methodology-reports/comparing-the-producer-price-index-for-personal-consumption-with-the-us-all-items-cpi-for-all-urban-consumers.htm):

| Feature | Producer Price Index | Consumer Price Index |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Point of view | Seller: revenue the producer receives | Buyer: what consumers pay out of pocket |
| Imports | Excluded | Included |
| Sales and excise taxes | Excluded | Included |
| Owners' equivalent rent | Not in scope | About 24 percent of the CPI |
| Medical care | Includes payments by employers, insurers and government | Only payments made directly by consumers |
| Who the buyers are | Consumers, businesses, government, exports | Urban consumers |

Two of those rows explain a lot. Housing costs, the largest single piece of the CPI, are basically missing from the PPI. And because the PPI counts what insurers and Medicare pay hospitals and doctors, it often captures health care price changes the CPI can't see.

## What the latest PPI report showed

The most recent report, released on October 15, 2026, covered September. It was a hot one, driven almost entirely by energy:

| PPI, September 2026 (released Oct. 15, 2026) | Monthly change (seasonally adjusted) |
| --- | --- |
| Final demand | +1.3% |
| Final demand goods | +2.6% |
| Final demand energy | +10.8% |
| Final demand foods | +1.2% |
| Final demand services | +0.8% |
| Final demand less foods, energy and trade services | +0.5% |

*Source: [BLS Producer Price Index news release, September 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/ppi.nr0.htm).*

Over the 12 months ended in September, final demand prices rose 6.3 percent, and the measure excluding foods, energy and trade services rose 5.0 percent. Final demand had increased 0.5 percent in August and 0.2 percent in July, so September marked a sharp acceleration. Gasoline prices received by producers jumped 20.3 percent in the month, and crude petroleum rose 28.3 percent within intermediate demand. One item went the other way: chicken eggs fell 39.7 percent.

The release also contains a small detail that tells a bigger story. Margins for automotive fuels and lubricants retailing fell 30.3 percent. In plain terms, gas stations paid much more for fuel and couldn't pass all of it on right away, so their margins got squeezed. That is the PPI showing who absorbed a price shock, something the CPI can't do.

Compare that with the [September CPI](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.htm), released a day earlier. Consumer gasoline prices rose 9.1 percent and the CPI energy index rose 5.2 percent, both much smaller moves than in the PPI. That gap is expected. A pump price includes taxes and the retailer's margin, which don't move one-for-one with the fuel itself, so a large percentage jump at the refinery shows up as a smaller percentage jump at the pump. Headline CPI rose 3.7 percent over 12 months, while core CPI rose 2.6 percent.

## Does PPI predict CPI?

This is the most common question about the report, and the honest answer is: sometimes, partly. The logic of "pass-through" is easy to follow. If a bakery pays more for flour, fuel and delivery, it may eventually charge more for bread. Rising producer prices can be a warning that consumer prices will follow.

But several things weaken the link:

- **Margins act as a cushion.** As the September fuel data showed, retailers and manufacturers sometimes eat higher costs instead of passing them on, at least for a while.
- **The baskets differ.** Shelter dominates the CPI and is mostly absent from the PPI. A month with hot producer prices and cooling rents can produce very different readings.
- **Imports go the other way.** The CPI includes imported goods, and the PPI doesn't. Import prices can offset or amplify domestic cost trends.
- **Timing varies.** Pass-through can take weeks for gasoline and many months for manufactured goods, if it happens at all.

So a hot PPI is best read as a sign of cost pressure building in the pipeline, not as a forecast of the next CPI print.

## Why the PPI report matters to investors

The PPI moves markets through three main channels.

**1. It feeds into the Fed's preferred inflation gauge.** The Federal Reserve defines its 2 percent goal using the PCE price index, not the CPI, as stated in its [Statement on Longer-Run Goals](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/monetary-policy-strategy-tools-and-communications-statement-on-longer-run-goals-monetary-policy-strategy-2025.htm). The PCE uses PPI industry indexes for hospitals, physicians and nursing care facilities, according to a [BLS fact sheet on health care in the PPI](https://www.bls.gov/ppi/factsheets/producer-price-index-healthcare-factsheet.htm). Once both CPI and PPI are out, economists can estimate PCE for that month fairly closely. That's why a PPI surprise in a few service categories can shift expectations for the PCE report weeks later, even when the headline looks unremarkable. Our explainer on [CPI vs. PCE](/articles/cpi-vs-pce/) covers how those two measures differ.

**2. It shapes interest rate expectations.** At its October 27-28 meeting, the FOMC [held the federal funds rate at 3-3/4 to 4 percent](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/files/monetary20261028a1.pdf) with inflation still above its goal. When producer prices accelerate, bond traders may push back the expected timing of rate cuts, which tends to lift Treasury yields. Higher yields reduce the present value of future earnings, a mechanism we walk through in [how interest rates affect stock valuations](/articles/how-interest-rates-affect-stock-valuations/).

**3. It hints at profit margins.** For individual companies, the PPI can show whether input costs are rising faster than the prices they can charge. Investors listening to earnings calls often hear management talk about "input cost inflation" or "price/cost." The PPI is a public, independent check on that story, as we explain in [how inflation affects stocks](/articles/how-does-inflation-affect-stocks/).

### Which sectors feel it

When PPI is hot because of energy, oil and gas producers can benefit, since they are the ones receiving higher prices. Companies that buy fuel, such as airlines, trucking firms and chemical makers, can face pressure. Retailers and consumer brands sit in the middle: higher costs squeeze their margins unless they can raise prices. These are tendencies, not rules, and the market's reaction also depends on what was already expected.

## A look back: producer inflation in 2022

The PPI's limits and strengths both showed up in 2022. In the BLS release for [March 2022](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/ppi_04132022.htm), final demand prices rose 1.4 percent for the month and 11.2 percent over 12 months. The BLS called it "the largest increase since 12-month data were first calculated in November 2010." Energy prices jumped 5.7 percent in March alone, accounting for more than half of the rise in goods, and the measure excluding foods, energy and trade services was up 7.0 percent over the year.

Notice the gap between those two numbers. Energy pushed the headline far above the underlying measure, and energy prices can reverse quickly when commodity markets turn. That is one reason investors look past the headline to the measure excluding foods, energy and trade: it says more about whether price pressure is broad or concentrated.

The September 2026 report rhymes with that episode in one way: an energy shock dominated the headline. It differs in another. The 12-month pace of 6.3 percent is well below the 2022 peak, and the stripped-down measure, at 5.0 percent, is the number that will tell investors whether the pressure is spreading beyond fuel.

## How to read a PPI report in five minutes

1. **Start with final demand, then go straight to the version excluding foods, energy and trade services.** If the headline is hot but this measure is calm, the move is likely a commodity story.
2. **Compare with the consensus forecast.** Markets react to surprises. A big number that was widely expected may barely move prices.
3. **Split goods from services.** Goods tend to reflect commodity swings. Services are stickier and say more about underlying inflation.
4. **Check trade margins.** A big drop in margins means businesses absorbed costs this month; a rebound can follow.
5. **Scan the health care categories.** Hospital, physician and nursing care prices feed into the PCE price index, so they can matter more for the Fed's gauge than their size in the PPI suggests.
6. **Remember revisions.** The BLS revises the previous four months as late reports come in, so last month's number may have changed.

You can pull the full history from the [BLS PPI database](https://www.bls.gov/ppi/) or chart the series on FRED.

## Where PPI fits on the economic calendar

Think of the inflation reports as a relay. The CPI usually arrives first and gets the biggest reaction. The PPI comes out within a day or two of it, sometimes before and sometimes after; the August 2026 PPI, for example, was released on September 10, a day before that month's CPI. The PCE price index then arrives near the end of the month, partly assembled from the first two. Reading all three together gives a fuller picture than any one of them alone.
