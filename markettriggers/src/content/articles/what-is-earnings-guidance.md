**Earnings guidance is a company's own forecast of its future results, usually revenue and earnings per share for the next quarter or full year, often given as a range. It moves stocks because share prices are built on expected future profits, so a raised or cut outlook can matter more than the quarter just reported.**

Every quarter, the same headline confuses people: a company beats estimates and its stock drops anyway. Most of the time the explanation sits a few paragraphs down in the press release, under a heading like "Outlook" or "Fiscal Year Guidance." This article explains what that section is, why traders read it first, and how to judge whether a change is as big as the headline makes it sound. It builds on the [earnings section of our guide to what moves the stock market](/#earnings-reports).

## What is earnings guidance, exactly?

Guidance is management telling investors what it expects to happen next. It usually appears in the quarterly earnings release and gets discussed on the conference call that follows. Some companies update it every quarter. Others set it once a year and simply say whether they still stand by it.

It comes in several shapes:

| Type of guidance | What it looks like | How precise it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Point estimate | "We expect full-year EPS of $3.20." | Very precise, rarely used |
| Range | "Revenue of $1.15 billion to $1.20 billion." | The most common format |
| Directional | "We expect margins to improve in the second half." | No number, just a direction |
| Single metric | Comparable-store sales, subscriptions, capital spending | Focused on what drives the business |
| Long-term targets | Multi-year growth or margin goals, often at an investor day | Broad, and revisited less often |

The numbers in guidance can be GAAP figures, which follow standard accounting rules, or adjusted (non-GAAP) figures that exclude items such as restructuring charges or stock-based compensation. That difference matters, and we come back to it below.

Guidance is not the same thing as the analyst **consensus estimate**. The consensus is the average forecast of outside analysts who cover the stock. Guidance is the company's own view. In practice the two are closely linked, because analysts lean heavily on guidance when they build their models.

## Why guidance often matters more than the quarter

Think about what a stock price represents. Investors are paying today for a share of the profits the company will earn in the years ahead. The quarter that just ended is already history. It matters mainly as evidence about what comes next.

Guidance is direct evidence about what comes next, and it comes from the people with the best view of the business: the executives who see the order book, the pricing, the hiring plans and the costs. When they change their forecast, three things tend to happen quickly:

1. **Analysts rebuild their models.** Within hours or days, estimates for coming quarters move toward the new guidance.
2. **The consensus shifts.** As individual analysts revise, the average forecast moves with them.
3. **The valuation gets recalculated.** If investors were willing to pay a certain multiple of next year's earnings, and next year's earnings estimate drops, the price they will pay drops with it, even if the multiple stays the same.

That chain explains most of the "beat but fell" puzzles we cover in [why stocks fall after beating earnings](/articles/why-do-stocks-fall-after-earnings/). A company can beat this quarter by a few cents and still lower the forecast for the next four quarters. The market cares more about the four.

## Raised guidance, guidance cuts and everything in between

Not every guidance change sends the same message. Here is how the main outcomes are often read:

| What the company does | What it signals | How the market often reads it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raises guidance above consensus | Business is running ahead of plan | Usually positive, often the strongest earnings-day setup |
| Raises guidance, but still below consensus | Better than before, but not as good as investors hoped | Mixed or negative, because expectations were higher |
| Reaffirms guidance | Nothing has changed, at least officially | Depends on whether investors expected a raise |
| Narrows the range | More certainty as the year goes on | Read the new midpoint: did it move up or down? |
| Cuts guidance | Business is running behind plan | Usually negative, sometimes sharply |
| Withdraws or suspends guidance | Management can't forecast with confidence | Often read as a sign of high uncertainty |

Two patterns deserve special mention.

**"Beat and raise."** A company beats the quarter and lifts its full-year outlook. This is the outcome investors hope for, and stocks that deliver it repeatedly tend to attract a following. But the size of the raise matters, which brings us to the math below.

**The guidance cut.** A **guidance cut** is the classic trigger for a sharp one-day drop. It is especially painful when it comes in the middle of a year, because it implies that the remaining quarters will be weaker than management itself thought just three months earlier. Investors also tend to worry that a first cut won't be the last.

## How to judge a guidance change in five questions

Headlines report guidance changes in a few words. The real signal takes a minute of arithmetic.

### 1. Compared with what?

The most common mistake is comparing new guidance with old guidance. The market compares it with the **consensus for that same period**. A company that raises its full-year EPS range can still disappoint if analysts were already above the new top end.

### 2. Where is the midpoint?

With a range, most people focus on the midpoint. A company that raises only the top of its range while leaving the bottom alone has moved the midpoint up by half as much as the headline suggests. A narrower range around a lower midpoint can actually be a quiet cut.

### 3. Is the raise bigger than the beat?

This is the beat-and-raise math. Suppose (hypothetically) a company beats first-quarter EPS by $0.05 and raises its full-year EPS guidance by $0.05. It has simply added the beat to the annual number. Its outlook for the remaining nine months hasn't changed at all. If it raised the full-year number by $0.15 instead, the implied outlook for the rest of the year improved by about $0.10. That second case is the stronger signal.

### 4. What is driving the change?

A raise driven by stronger demand is different from one driven by a lower tax rate, a currency swing, a share buyback or an acquisition. Management usually explains the drivers in the release or on the call. Demand-driven changes tend to tell you more about the business itself.

### 5. Is the guidance GAAP or adjusted?

Many companies guide on adjusted EPS. For historical results, SEC rules require a reconciliation to the comparable GAAP number. For forward-looking figures there is an exception. According to the SEC staff's interpretations on non-GAAP measures (Question 102.10(b)), a company presenting a forward-looking non-GAAP measure may leave out the quantitative reconciliation if it relies on the exception in Item 10(e)(1)(i)(B) of Regulation S-K, but it must disclose that reliance, identify the information that is unavailable and explain its probable significance. In plain terms: adjusted guidance sometimes arrives without a full bridge to GAAP, so read the footnote that says why.

## The rules behind guidance

Guidance is voluntary, but it lives inside a legal framework that shapes how companies give it.

**Results are required; forecasts are not.** Form 8-K Item 2.02 requires a company that publicly announces material nonpublic information about its results for a *completed* quarterly or annual period to furnish that release to the SEC as an exhibit. Nothing in that item requires a forecast. That is why some companies give detailed guidance and others give none.

**The safe harbor.** The Private Securities Litigation Reform Act of 1995 added a safe harbor for forward-looking statements to federal securities law (15 U.S.C. § 78u-5). Its definition of a forward-looking statement includes projections of revenues, income and earnings per share, along with management's plans and statements about future economic performance. In private lawsuits, a company is generally protected if the statement is identified as forward-looking and accompanied by "meaningful cautionary statements identifying important factors" that could make actual results differ, or if the plaintiff can't prove the statement was made with actual knowledge that it was false. That is the reason every earnings release ends with a dense paragraph of risk factors. It is a legal requirement for protection, not boilerplate for its own sake.

**No duty to update.** The same law says: "Nothing in this section shall impose upon any person a duty to update a forward-looking statement." In practice, many companies still update guidance each quarter because investors expect it, but the statute doesn't force them to.

**Regulation FD.** When the SEC adopted Regulation FD in 2000, its release called the practice of analysts seeking guidance about earnings estimates in private conversations a situation that "raises special concerns about selective disclosure," and said an official who engages in it "takes on a high degree of risk under Regulation FD." The release described a model for doing it publicly instead: issue a press release, give advance notice of the conference call, and hold that call in an open manner so any investor can listen. That is essentially how earnings day works today.

## Why some companies stop giving guidance

Not every company forecasts, and some that did will pull back. The reasons tend to fall into a few groups:

- **Uncertainty.** When conditions change too fast to forecast, such as a sudden demand shock, management may suspend guidance rather than publish a number it can't stand behind.
- **Short-termism.** Some executives argue that quarterly guidance pushes management and investors to obsess over the next 90 days. They may switch to annual guidance or long-term targets.
- **Business model.** Companies with lumpy revenue, such as those that depend on a few large contracts or on approvals they don't control, may find precise forecasts unhelpful.

For investors, the absence of guidance shifts weight to other evidence: management's tone on the call, the questions analysts keep asking, and the key metrics in the release. ## A worked example (hypothetical)

Here's how a guidance change can swamp a good quarter. The company and every number below are made up for illustration.

Ridgeway Software reports its second quarter:

- **Quarter:** revenue and adjusted EPS both come in above consensus.
- **Prior full-year revenue guidance:** $4.00 billion to $4.10 billion.
- **Analyst consensus for the full year, before the report:** $4.08 billion.
- **New full-year revenue guidance:** $3.95 billion to $4.05 billion.

The new midpoint is $4.00 billion, down $0.05 billion from the old midpoint of $4.05 billion. More important, it sits about 2% below the $4.08 billion consensus. Analysts will likely trim their estimates for the rest of the year and for next year too, since slower growth this year usually means a lower base to grow from.

On the call, the CFO says large customers are "taking longer to sign." Two analysts ask about the sales pipeline. The headline reads "Ridgeway beats on revenue and earnings." The stock, quite plausibly, opens lower. The quarter was fine. The outlook got worse, and the outlook is what investors are pricing.

Now flip it. Had Ridgeway raised its range to $4.10 billion to $4.20 billion, the new midpoint of $4.15 billion would sit above the consensus, and the same quarter could have produced a very different reaction.

## Common mistakes when reading guidance

- **Treating guidance as a promise.** It's a forecast, protected by cautionary language precisely because it can be wrong.
- **Ignoring the base.** A raise from a low starting point may only bring the company back to where analysts already were.
- **Missing currency and one-time effects.** For multinationals, a move in the dollar can shift reported revenue without any change in underlying demand.
- **Forgetting the macro backdrop.** Guidance often bakes in assumptions about interest rates, consumer spending or inflation. If those assumptions look stale, the guidance may be too. Our explainers on [how inflation affects stocks](/articles/how-does-inflation-affect-stocks/) and [what happens when the Fed cuts rates](/articles/what-happens-to-stocks-when-the-fed-cuts-rates/) cover those forces.
- **Assuming conservative guidance is bad.** Some management teams set targets they expect to beat. If a company has a long record of guiding low and then beating, investors may already adjust for that habit.
