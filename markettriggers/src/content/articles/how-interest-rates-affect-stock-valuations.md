**How do interest rates affect stocks? Mostly through valuation. A share is a claim on future profits, and investors convert those profits into today's dollars using a discount rate tied to interest rates. When rates rise, future profits are worth less today, so the price investors will pay per dollar of earnings tends to fall. Lower rates work in reverse.**

That is the core idea, but it leaves out the part that trips people up: rates and earnings often move together, and the reason rates are moving matters as much as the size of the move. This guide walks through the math in plain terms, shows why some stocks are far more rate-sensitive than others, and explains what to watch. For how rates fit among every other market trigger, see the [interest rates section of our guide to what moves the stock market](/#interest-rates-and-stock-valuations).

## The discount rate: why a future dollar is worth less today

Start with a simple question. Would you rather have $100 today or $100 five years from now? Almost everyone picks today, because money in hand can earn interest. If a safe bond pays 5% a year, $100 today grows to about $128 in five years. Run that backward and $128 five years from now is worth roughly $100 today at a 5% rate.

That backward calculation is called discounting, and the rate used is the discount rate. Valuing a stock works the same way, just with many cash flows stretching far into the future. An analyst estimates the profits or dividends a company will produce, then discounts each one back to the present and adds them up.

The discount rate for stocks has two pieces:

- **A risk-free rate**, usually anchored to Treasury yields, because Treasuries are the benchmark for "safe" money.
- **A risk premium**, the extra return investors demand for owning something whose profits are uncertain.

When Treasury yields rise, the first piece goes up. Unless the risk premium shrinks to compensate, the total discount rate rises, and the present value of those future profits falls. The stock price follows.

Bonds show the same mechanism in its cleanest form. The SEC's [investor bulletin on interest rate risk](https://www.sec.gov/files/ib_interestraterisk.pdf) puts it simply: market interest rates and bond prices generally move in opposite directions. In one of its examples, a 10-year bond paying a 3% coupon, with nine years left, drops from $1,000 to $925 when market rates climb from 3% to 4%. A stock has no fixed coupon or maturity date, but the same pressure applies to its expected cash flows.

## How rates show up in the P/E ratio

Most investors don't build discounted cash flow models. They look at the price-to-earnings ratio, which tells you how many dollars the market is paying for each dollar of a company's annual earnings. A P/E of 20 means investors pay $20 for $1 of earnings.

Flip that ratio and you get the **earnings yield**. A P/E of 20 equals an earnings yield of 5% (1 divided by 20). That number is useful because you can compare it directly with a bond yield.

The Federal Reserve does exactly that in its [Financial Stability Report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/november-2025-financial-stability-report-asset-valuations.htm). It tracks the forward earnings-to-price ratio of S&P 500 firms, based on expected earnings for the next 12 months, and subtracts the expected real (inflation-adjusted) 10-year Treasury yield. The Fed calls the gap a crude measure of the extra return investors require for holding stocks instead of risk-free bonds: the equity premium.

Here is why that matters for valuations. If bond yields rise and investors still want the same premium for owning stocks, the earnings yield has to rise too. With earnings unchanged, the only way for that to happen is for prices to fall, which means a lower P/E.

The table below is a hypothetical illustration, not a forecast. It holds the required premium fixed at 3 percentage points and shows what P/E that implies at different real bond yields.

| Real 10-year yield (hypothetical) | Required equity premium | Required earnings yield | Implied P/E |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.5% | 3.0% | 3.5% | About 28.6 |
| 1.0% | 3.0% | 4.0% | 25.0 |
| 2.0% | 3.0% | 5.0% | 20.0 |
| 3.0% | 3.0% | 6.0% | About 16.7 |

Notice that the drop is steepest at low rates. Going from a 0.5% to a 1% real yield cuts the implied P/E by about 3.6 points. Going from 2% to 3% cuts it by about 3.3 points, from a lower base. That asymmetry helps explain why markets that had grown used to very low yields reacted so sharply when yields began to climb.

Real markets are messier than this table. Investors' required premium shifts with fear and confidence, and earnings estimates change constantly. In its [May 2026 report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-may-financial-stability-report-overview.htm), the Fed said the ratio of equity prices to earnings for S&P 500 companies stayed in the upper range of its historical distribution, while its estimate of the equity premium remained well below its historical average. In plain terms, stock investors were accepting a smaller cushion over bonds than usual. When that cushion is thin, a rise in yields leaves less room before valuations feel stretched.

## Why growth stocks are hit hardest

Not every stock reacts to rates the same way. The biggest difference comes from timing: when a company is expected to earn most of its money.

Consider two hypothetical companies, each worth $100 a share today:

- **Steady Co.**, a mature business that earns most of its value in the next several years and pays a dividend.
- **Future Co.**, a fast-growing business that earns little today and is valued mainly on profits expected 10 or 15 years out.

Discounting punishes distant cash flows far more than near ones. At a 6% discount rate, $1 received in 2 years is worth about 89 cents today. At 7%, it is worth about 87 cents, a drop of roughly 2%. The same 1-point increase applied to $1 received in 15 years cuts its value from about 42 cents to about 36 cents, a drop of roughly 13%.

So when rates rise, Future Co. loses much more value than Steady Co., even if nothing about either business changes. Investors describe this by saying growth stocks have a long "duration," borrowing the bond-market term for how sensitive a price is to rate changes.

| Type of stock | Where the value comes from | Typical sensitivity to rising rates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High-growth technology and biotech | Profits expected far in the future | High: long duration |
| Unprofitable or early-stage companies | Distant profits, plus reliance on outside funding | Very high |
| Utilities, REITs, consumer staples | Steady payouts that compete with bond yields | Moderate to high, through the income channel |
| Banks and insurers | Earnings can rise with rates | Mixed, often helped by a steeper yield curve |
| Value and cyclical stocks | Profits closer to today | Lower on valuation, but exposed to the economy |

Dividend-heavy groups like utilities and real estate investment trusts get hit through a different door. Their appeal often rests on income. When a Treasury pays more, a 4% dividend looks less special, so some investors swap the stock for the bond.

## Rates also change the earnings, not just the multiple

The discount rate is the cleanest channel, but rates reach stock prices in at least three other ways.

1. **Interest expense.** Companies that borrow pay more when rates rise, especially those with floating-rate loans or debt that must be refinanced soon. That comes straight out of profits.
2. **Customer demand.** Higher mortgage, auto and credit card rates slow spending on houses, cars and big purchases. Homebuilders and consumer discretionary companies feel this first.
3. **Competition from cash.** When money market funds and Treasury bills pay a meaningful yield, investors don't have to take stock market risk to earn something. That can pull money away from stocks at the margin.

The Fed has studied how much of the market's reaction comes from each channel. In a [Federal Reserve working paper](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/what-explains-the-stock-market39s-reaction-to-federal-reserve-policy.htm), Ben Bernanke and Kenneth Kuttner found that, on average, a hypothetical unanticipated quarter-point cut in the federal funds rate target was associated with roughly a 1% increase in broad stock indexes. They also found that the biggest part of the response came through changes in the extra return investors expect from stocks, rather than through changes in expected dividends or real interest rates alone. Two details stand out: the effect applies to surprises, not to moves the market already expected, and it is partly about investors' appetite for risk.

## When rising rates don't sink stocks

If higher rates always meant lower prices, investing would be easy. They don't, because of the other half of the valuation equation: growth.

Rates often rise because the economy is strong. Strong demand lifts revenue and profits. If earnings grow fast enough, a stock can rise even as its P/E slips. Think of it as a tug-of-war between a smaller multiple and a bigger earnings number.

The question to ask is why rates are moving:

| Why rates are rising | What it usually means for stocks |
| --- | --- |
| Faster economic growth | Earnings gains can offset a lower P/E |
| Higher inflation | Pressure on valuations and on profit margins |
| A hawkish Fed surprise | Quick hit to valuations, especially growth stocks |
| Investors demanding more to hold long bonds (higher term premium) | Pressure on valuations without any improvement in earnings |

The same logic runs in reverse. Falling rates are not automatically good news. If yields drop because a recession is coming, falling earnings can swamp the benefit of a lower discount rate. Our explainer on [what happens to stocks when the Fed cuts rates](/articles/what-happens-to-stocks-when-the-fed-cuts-rates/) covers that split between "good" and "bad" cuts in detail.

## A real example: 2022

The clearest recent case of rates squeezing valuations came in 2022. In March of that year, the Fed [raised its target range to 1/4 to 1/2 percent](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20220316a.htm), and said it expected ongoing increases to be appropriate. By December, after a string of large moves, it had [raised the range to 4-1/4 to 4-1/2 percent](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20221214a.htm).

The Fed's own reporting traced the effect on markets. Its [2022 annual report summary of monetary policy](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2022-ar-monetary-policy.htm) noted that yields on nominal Treasury securities across maturities had risen considerably since late February, and that equity prices were volatile and declined sharply, on net, in the first half of the year. It also said the drop in equity prices suggested that valuation pressures had eased.

That is the discount rate channel at work: as the risk-free part of the discount rate climbed, investors were willing to pay less for each dollar of future earnings. The cause behind the rate increases mattered too: the Fed was raising rates to fight high inflation, the combination in the table above that tends to pressure valuations and margins at the same time. Our guide on [how inflation affects stocks](/articles/how-does-inflation-affect-stocks/) explains that link.

## Where rates stand now

The backdrop in late 2026 makes this topic more than academic. At its September 15-16 meeting, the Fed [raised its target range to 3-3/4 to 4 percent](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20260916a.htm), saying inflation remained elevated. The next decision comes at the October 27-28 meeting, listed on the Fed's [meeting calendar](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm).

Pair that with the Fed's own observation that stock valuations sit in the upper part of their historical range while the equity premium is thin, and you have a market where rate surprises can matter. That isn't a prediction about where stocks go next. It's a description of how much room there is for the discount rate channel to work in either direction.

## How to use this as an investor

You don't need a valuation model to put this to work. A few habits help:

- **Check the 10-year yield alongside the market.** When stocks fall on a day yields jump and no company news is out, the discount rate is often the story.
- **Know your portfolio's duration.** A portfolio heavy in high-growth stocks will move more with rates than one tilted toward profitable, dividend-paying companies.
- **Compare earnings yields with bond yields.** If the earnings yield on a stock or index is barely above what a Treasury pays, you're accepting a thin premium for the risk.
- **Ask why rates are moving.** Growth-driven increases and inflation-driven increases have different implications.
- **Separate expected moves from surprises.** The market prices in what it expects. The reaction comes from the gap.
