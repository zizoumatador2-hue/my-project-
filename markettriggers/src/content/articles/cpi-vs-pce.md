**CPI vs. PCE comes down to two jobs. The PCE price index is the gauge the Federal Reserve uses for its 2% inflation goal. The Consumer Price Index arrives about two weeks earlier, so it usually delivers the bigger surprise and the bigger market move. Traders react to CPI first, then check PCE to see what the Fed will see.**

Every month, investors get two major readings on consumer inflation. They measure roughly the same thing, come from two different federal agencies, and often disagree by a few tenths of a percentage point. Those tenths matter, because they feed straight into expectations for interest rates. If you want the wider framework for how scheduled data moves prices, start with the [inflation section of our guide to what moves the stock market](/#inflation-cpi-pce-and-ppi).

## Two agencies, two inflation gauges

**The Consumer Price Index (CPI)** is produced by the [Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/). The headline version, CPI-U, tracks prices paid by all urban consumers, a group BLS says represents over 90 percent of the U.S. population. It measures what households pay out of their own pockets.

**The Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index** is produced by the [Bureau of Economic Analysis](https://www.bea.gov/data/personal-consumption-expenditures-price-index) as part of its monthly Personal Income and Outlays report. It is built from the national accounts, the same data system behind GDP, and it covers spending by households and spending made on their behalf.

Both come in headline and core versions. Headline includes everything. Core strips out food and energy, which swing sharply from month to month. When people say "core PCE," they mean the PCE price index excluding food and energy, which many Fed watchers treat as the best read on the underlying trend.

Here is how the two compare side by side:

| | CPI | PCE price index |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Published by | Bureau of Labor Statistics | Bureau of Economic Analysis |
| Release timing | Around mid-month | Near the end of the month |
| What it covers | Out-of-pocket spending by urban consumers | Spending by and on behalf of households |
| Where spending weights come from | Surveys of consumers | Largely business data in the national accounts |
| Index formula | Modified Laspeyres | Fisher-Ideal (chain-type) |
| Shelter's role | Large weight | Much smaller weight |
| Used for | Social Security COLAs, TIPS, many contracts | The Fed's 2% inflation goal |
| Typical market impact | Larger, because it comes first | Smaller, because much of it can be estimated |

## Why the gap exists: four effects

BEA and BLS economists have spent years reconciling the two indexes. Both agencies group the differences into the same four buckets: formula, weight, scope and "other."

### 1. The weight effect: how much each item counts

This is usually the biggest piece. The two indexes give different importance to the same goods and services. A [BLS research paper](https://www.bls.gov/osmr/research-papers/2017/st170010.htm) explains that the difference starts with where the spending data come from: the CPI sources it from consumers, while PCE sources it from businesses.

Shelter is the standout. In the CPI's August 2026 weights, shelter made up about 35.3 percent of the entire index, according to the [September 2026 CPI release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_10142026.htm). BLS has noted that shelter has a "significantly higher relative weight in the CPI than the PCE." So when rents and housing costs rise faster than other prices, CPI tends to run hotter than PCE. When gasoline swings, the weight effect can shift too. In a [2011 BLS comparison](https://www.bls.gov/opub/btn/archive/differences-between-the-consumer-price-index-and-the-personal-consumption-expenditures-price-index.pdf), gasoline was the largest contributor to the weight effect in the fourth quarter of 2010.

### 2. The scope effect: whose spending counts

The CPI only counts what consumers pay directly. PCE also includes spending made on people's behalf. Medical care is the classic example. If your employer pays most of your health insurance premium, or Medicare pays for a hospital stay, that spending shows up in PCE but not in the CPI. As a result, medical care carries a much larger weight in PCE.

### 3. The formula effect: how substitution is handled

According to the [BEA](https://bea.gov/help/faq/555), the PCE price index uses a Fisher-Ideal formula, while the CPI uses a modified Laspeyres formula. In plain terms, PCE does a better job of capturing what happens when shoppers switch from one product to a cheaper one as prices change. If beef gets expensive and people buy more chicken, a Fisher index picks that up more fully. That tends to pull PCE inflation slightly below CPI inflation, all else equal. The catch, as BLS has pointed out, is that formulas like this need current spending data, which is hard to get in real time.

### 4. "Other" effects

The leftovers include differences in seasonal adjustment, small differences in the prices used for the same items, and a residual that doesn't fit neatly anywhere else.

How big is the gap in practice? It varies. In the BLS comparison covering 2002 through the first half of 2007, the CPI averaged 2.9 percent annual growth versus 2.5 percent for PCE. In a single quarter, the fourth quarter of 2010, the CPI rose at a 2.6 percent annualized rate against 1.7 percent for PCE. But the gap isn't fixed, and it can even flip, as the latest data show.

## What the latest data show

Here are the most recent matching months from the two agencies. August 2026 is the latest month for which both indexes are out.

| August 2026 | CPI | PCE price index |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Headline, change from July | +0.4% | +0.3% |
| Headline, change from a year earlier | +3.4% | +3.4% |
| Core, change from July | +0.3% | +0.2% |
| Core, change from a year earlier | +2.4% | +3.0% |

*Sources: [BLS CPI release for August 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_09112026.htm); [BEA Personal Income and Outlays, August 2026](https://www.bea.gov/sites/default/files/2026-09/pi0826.pdf). CPI monthly changes are seasonally adjusted; the 12-month CPI changes are not.*

Two things stand out. Headline inflation was identical on a 12-month basis. And core PCE ran well above core CPI, the opposite of the pattern many investors take for granted. That's the practical lesson of the four effects: which index runs hotter depends on *which prices* are rising. A rule of thumb like "PCE is always a few tenths lower" can mislead you in any given month.

Since then, the September CPI has come out. Released October 14, it showed headline CPI up 0.6 percent for the month and 3.7 percent over 12 months, with gasoline up 9.1 percent and accounting for over half of the monthly increase. Core CPI rose 0.3 percent for the month and 2.6 percent from a year earlier, per [BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_10142026.htm). The matching PCE figures are scheduled for October 29.

## Why the Fed watches PCE

The Federal Open Market Committee spells out its target in its [Statement on Longer-Run Goals and Monetary Policy Strategy](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/monetary-policy-strategy-tools-and-communications-statement-on-longer-run-goals-monetary-policy-strategy-2025.htm), first adopted in 2012 and most recently amended in August 2025. It says inflation of 2 percent, "as measured by the annual change in the price index for personal consumption expenditures," is most consistent over the longer run with the Fed's mandate. The [Fed's own FAQ](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/economy_14400.htm) repeats the same definition.

The reasons follow from the four effects. PCE covers more of what households consume, including the health care others pay for. Its formula reflects how people adjust when prices change. And because it sits inside the national accounts, it draws on the same detailed spending data used to measure the economy as a whole.

That doesn't mean the Fed ignores CPI. Officials cite it, and in practice CPI shapes the conversation for two weeks before PCE confirms or adjusts the picture. Fed projections, though, are framed in PCE terms, so that's the number that ultimately gets compared with the 2% goal.

## Why CPI usually moves markets more

If PCE is the Fed's target, why do traders treat CPI day as the bigger event? Timing.

The CPI is released at 8:30 a.m. Eastern, before the stock market opens, usually around the middle of the month. September 2026 data arrived October 14, for example. The PCE index follows near the month's end, also at 8:30 a.m. Eastern; August data came out September 30.

By the time PCE lands, much of it is already known. Forecasters translate CPI components, plus producer price data, into PCE estimates. So the consensus for PCE tends to be well informed, and the surprise is usually smaller. Not always, but usually.

That creates a rough division of labor:

1. **CPI day is the shock.** Treasury yields, especially the 2-year, react to any surprise in core CPI. Stocks follow, with rate-sensitive growth names often moving most. Our explainer on [how inflation affects stocks](/articles/how-does-inflation-affect-stocks/) walks through why.
2. **The days in between are translation.** Economists publish PCE estimates. If CPI was hot because of shelter, the PCE read may come in cooler, since shelter carries less weight there. If it was hot because of medical services or other categories that weigh more in PCE, the PCE estimate may look worse.
3. **PCE day is confirmation.** A PCE reading close to estimates rarely moves markets much. A real miss can, because it changes what the Fed itself is looking at.

Market pricing for future Fed decisions, visible in tools like the [CME FedWatch Tool](https://www.cmegroup.com/markets/interest-rates/cme-fedwatch-tool.html), often shifts on CPI day and then adjusts more modestly after PCE.

## A practical example (hypothetical)

The numbers below are invented to show the mechanics.

Suppose core CPI comes in at 0.4 percent for the month against a 0.3 percent forecast. The 2-year Treasury yield jumps and stock index futures slip. Reading the details, you see that most of the overshoot came from shelter and car insurance, while medical services were soft.

Over the next few days, forecasters plug those components into their models. Because shelter weighs much less in PCE, and medical services weigh more, their core PCE estimate lands at a more modest 0.25 percent. Bond yields drift back part of the way. When PCE arrives near the forecast, the market barely reacts.

Now flip it. A tame core CPI hides a sharp rise in hospital and other medical services, which carry more weight in PCE. Core PCE estimates climb after the CPI release, and the "good" CPI day gets partly reversed later in the month. Same data, very different story once you know the weights.

## How CPI is used outside the Fed

PCE drives monetary policy, but CPI touches more people's wallets directly:

- **Social Security.** Annual cost-of-living adjustments are based on a related index, the CPI-W, comparing third-quarter averages, according to the [Congressional Research Service](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-05-27_94-803_762bfeccaf329e58861fb430cc0290720125f04e.html). The 2026 COLA was 2.8 percent.
- **TIPS.** Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities adjust their principal using the CPI, as [TreasuryDirect](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/tips/) explains.
- **Contracts.** A BLS research paper notes that many contracts and government programs are tied to inflation measures, from rental agreements to Social Security.

That's another reason CPI gets so much attention: it has real cash consequences beyond the Fed's reaction.

## Common mistakes when comparing CPI and PCE

- **Assuming a fixed gap.** The difference depends on which prices are rising. August 2026 showed core PCE above core CPI.
- **Comparing different months.** CPI for September and PCE for August are not apples to apples. Line up the same reference month.
- **Mixing headline and core.** Markets and the Fed usually start with core, month over month, then check the 12-month rate.
- **Forgetting the weights.** A hot shelter reading hits CPI harder; a hot medical care reading matters more for PCE.
- **Reading only the headline on CPI day.** The components tell you how PCE is likely to look, which is what the Fed will weigh.
