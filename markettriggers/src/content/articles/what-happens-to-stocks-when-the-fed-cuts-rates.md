**When the Fed cuts interest rates, stocks often rise, but not always. The outcome depends on three things: why the Fed is cutting, how much of the cut investors already expected, and what the Fed signals about future moves.** A cut that supports a healthy economy is usually good news for stocks. A cut forced by a recession or financial crisis often arrives while stocks are falling.

That's why "rate cuts are bullish" is one of the most misleading rules of thumb in investing. This guide explains the mechanics, the history, and how to read a Fed decision the way professional investors do. For the bigger picture of how the Fed fits among all market triggers, see our [guide to what moves the stock market](/#the-federal-reserve-the-most-powerful-market-trigger).

## How a Fed rate cut works

The Federal Reserve's [Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC)](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm) sets a target range for the **federal funds rate**, the rate banks charge each other for overnight loans. The committee holds eight scheduled meetings a year and can act between meetings in an emergency.

When the Fed lowers that target, the effect ripples outward:

- Short-term Treasury yields usually fall.
- Rates on credit cards, adjustable-rate loans, and business credit lines tend to follow.
- Savings accounts and money market funds gradually pay less.
- Longer-term rates such as mortgages may fall too, though they also depend on inflation and growth expectations, so they don't always move in step.

## Why lower rates can lift stocks

Lower interest rates support stock prices through the same channels that higher rates hurt them, in reverse:

1. **Future earnings are worth more today.** A lower discount rate raises the present value of profits expected years from now. Growth stocks feel this most.
2. **Stocks look better next to bonds.** When cash and bonds pay less, investors are more willing to own stocks.
3. **Borrowing gets cheaper.** Companies can refinance debt and invest for less, and consumers may spend more on homes, cars, and big purchases.
4. **Risk appetite improves.** Easier financial conditions tend to encourage investors to take more risk.

## Why stocks can fall anyway

### Reason 1: The cut was already priced in

Markets look ahead. If investors spent weeks expecting a cut, prices adjusted before the meeting. On the day itself, the decision carries little new information. If the Fed cuts by less than hoped, stocks can fall even though rates went down.

You can see what's priced in through **fed funds futures**. The [CME FedWatch Tool](https://www.cmegroup.com/markets/interest-rates/cme-fedwatch-tool.html) converts those futures prices into implied probabilities for each possible decision.

### Reason 2: The guidance disappointed

The decision is only one part of an FOMC meeting. Investors also study:

- **The policy statement**, compared word by word with the previous one.
- **The Summary of Economic Projections**, including the "dot plot" of where each official expects rates to go. It's released at four of the eight meetings each year.
- **The press conference**, where the Fed chair's answers can shift expectations in real time.

A cut paired with signals that few further cuts are coming can be read as hawkish, and stocks may fall.

### Reason 3: The cut confirms bad news

This is the most important distinction. The Fed doesn't cut rates in a vacuum. Sometimes it cuts because inflation has cooled and it can ease off. Other times it cuts because the economy is weakening fast. In the second case, falling corporate profits can easily outweigh the benefit of cheaper money.

## Two kinds of rate cuts

| | "Insurance" or normalization cuts | Emergency or recession cuts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Why the Fed cuts | Inflation is easing, or it wants to guard against a slowdown | The economy or financial system is under serious stress |
| Economic backdrop | Growth still positive | Recession, crisis, or sharp downturn |
| Pace | Gradual, often 0.25 point at a time | Fast and large, sometimes between meetings |
| Typical stock reaction | Often supportive | Stocks often keep falling until the downturn eases |
| Examples | 2019 | 2001, 2007–2008, March 2020 |

### Examples from history

- **2001 and 2007–2008:** The Fed cut rates repeatedly as the economy weakened. In both cases, stocks went through bear markets despite the cuts, because earnings were falling and recession fears dominated.
- **2019:** The Fed cut rates three times as a precaution against slowing global growth and trade tensions, while the U.S. economy was still expanding. Stocks rose strongly that year.
- **March 2020:** As the COVID-19 pandemic hit, the Fed made two emergency cuts between scheduled meetings, bringing rates near zero. Stocks were already in freefall; the recovery came only as massive monetary and fiscal support took hold.
- **September 2024:** The Fed delivered its first cut since 2020, a half-point reduction, after inflation had come down substantially. It was a normalization cut rather than a crisis response.

## Which sectors tend to react most

When cuts come in a healthy economy, the groups that tend to respond most are those most sensitive to borrowing costs:

- **Homebuilders and real estate:** Lower rates can bring mortgage rates and financing costs down.
- **Small caps:** Smaller companies tend to rely more on floating-rate debt, so cheaper borrowing helps them directly. The Russell 2000 is the usual benchmark.
- **Utilities:** Their steady dividends look more attractive when bond yields fall.
- **Growth stocks:** Their value depends heavily on distant earnings, which a lower discount rate boosts.

**Banks** have a more complicated reaction. Lower rates can reduce what they earn on loans, but a steeper yield curve and healthier borrowers can offset that. For the full picture, see our [Market Sectors hub](/market-sectors/).

## How to read an FOMC decision in real time

The decision is released at 2:00 p.m. Eastern Time on the final day of the meeting, followed by the chair's press conference about 30 minutes later.

1. **Before the meeting:** Check what fed funds futures imply. Note whether a cut is fully priced in.
2. **At 2:00 p.m.:** Compare the decision with expectations, then scan the statement for changed wording on inflation and the job market.
3. **At meetings with projections:** Look at the median dot for the coming year. Fewer cuts than expected is a hawkish surprise; more is a dovish one.
4. **During the press conference:** Watch the 2-year Treasury yield. It reacts quickly to any hint about the path of rates.
5. **Next day and week:** The first move often reverses. Follow-up data such as the [jobs report and CPI](/economic-indicators/) will decide whether the market's first read holds.

## The bottom line for investors

A rate cut is a starting point for analysis, not a buy signal. Ask why the Fed is cutting, what was expected, and what the Fed says about what comes next. A cut in a healthy economy has usually been a tailwind. A cut in a crisis has often marked a period of pain before recovery.
