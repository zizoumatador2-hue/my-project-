**The Fed dot plot is a chart, released four times a year, showing where each Federal Reserve official expects the federal funds rate to be at the end of the next few years and over the longer run. Each dot is one person's projection. Investors focus on the median dot and compare it with market pricing to judge whether the Fed is more hawkish or dovish than expected.**

That's the Fed dot plot explained in one paragraph. In practice the chart rewards a slower read. A single dot can matter less than how the whole cluster shifted since the last release, and the median can move markets even when the rate decision itself was fully expected. The dot plot is one of the main channels through which the Fed tells investors where it thinks rates are heading, which is why it shows up in our [guide to what moves the stock market](/#the-federal-reserve-the-most-powerful-market-trigger).

## Where the dot plot comes from

The dot plot is one piece of a larger release called the Summary of Economic Projections, or SEP. According to the Fed's guide to the SEP, projections are collected from each member of the Board of Governors and each Reserve Bank president four times a year, in connection with the meetings in March, June, September and December. The charts and tables are released shortly after the meeting ends.

Each participant submits forecasts for five things: real GDP growth, the unemployment rate, overall PCE inflation, core PCE inflation and the federal funds rate. The forecasts cover the current year, the next two or three years and the "longer run," which the Fed defines as where things would settle in maybe five or six years, absent further shocks and under appropriate policy.

That last phrase matters. The Fed says each participant's projections "are based on his or her assessment of appropriate monetary policy." So an official who expects stubborn inflation will usually pencil in higher rates, and one who expects a weakening job market will pencil in lower ones. The dots are the rate half of a full economic forecast, not a free-floating guess.

The interest rate projections, plotted as dots, are Figure 2 in the SEP. In the Fed's words, each shaded circle shows the value, rounded to the nearest one-eighth of a percentage point, of an individual participant's judgment of the midpoint of the appropriate target range. Names are never attached.

## How to read the chart

Picture a grid. The horizontal axis lists years: 2026, 2027, 2028, 2029 and "Longer run." The vertical axis is the federal funds rate. Above each year sits a stack of dots, one per official, placed at the rate that person thinks fits the end of that year.

Four readings come out of it:

1. **The median.** The Fed's guide defines it as the middle value when projections are ranked from lowest to highest. With an even number of projections, it's the average of the two middle ones. This is the number headlines quote.
2. **The spread.** A tight cluster signals agreement. A wide spread signals a committee that sees the outlook very differently, which often means the path is less predictable than the median implies.
3. **The change.** Compare each year's median with the previous SEP. A median that rose since June tells you officials as a group grew more hawkish over the summer.
4. **The longer-run dot.** This is the committee's rough estimate of a "neutral" rate, the level that neither stimulates nor restrains the economy. When it creeps up, officials are saying rates may need to stay higher for structural reasons, not just because of current inflation.

The SEP's tables also report a central tendency, which the Fed says excludes the three highest and three lowest projections for each variable in each year, and a full range. Those help you see whether the median sits in the middle of the pack or near one edge.

## What the September 2026 dot plot showed

The most recent SEP came out on September 16, 2026, the same day the FOMC raised its target range by a quarter point to 3.75 to 4 percent on a 12-0 vote. Eighteen participants submitted projections. Here is how the medians compared with June:

| Median projection | 2026 | 2027 | 2028 | Longer run |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Federal funds rate, September | 4.1% | 4.1% | 3.9% | 3.2% |
| Federal funds rate, June | 3.8% | 3.6% | 3.4% | 3.1% |
| PCE inflation, September | 3.7% | 2.3% | 2.1% | 2.0% |
| Core PCE inflation, September | 3.4% | 2.5% | 2.2% | n/a |
| Unemployment rate, September | 4.1% | 4.1% | 4.1% | 4.2% |
| Real GDP growth, September | 2.3% | 2.4% | 2.2% | 2.0% |

*Source: [Federal Reserve, Summary of Economic Projections, September 16, 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomcprojtabl20260916.htm). The September SEP also includes 2029, where the rate median is 3.6%. Core PCE is not projected for the longer run.*

Now translate it. The target range after the hike was 3.75 to 4 percent, a midpoint of 3.875 percent. A 2026 median of 4.1 percent (the 4.125 percent midpoint, rounded) meant the typical official expected one more quarter-point increase before year-end. The 2027 median at the same level meant no cuts next year. The St. Louis Fed's FRED blog summed up the revision: the rate medians were 0.3 percentage points higher than June for 2026 and 0.5 points higher for both 2027 and 2028. Half a point is two quarter-point moves.

The individual dots tell more than the median does:

| End-of-year rate (midpoint) | 2026 dots | 2027 dots |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 4.375% | 4 | 8 |
| 4.125% | 12 | 6 |
| 3.875% | 2 | 0 |
| 3.625% | 0 | 3 |
| 3.125% | 0 | 1 |

*Source: Federal Reserve, September 16, 2026 SEP, Figure 2. Each row counts how many of the 18 participants placed their dot at that level.*

For 2026, 16 of 18 officials saw at least one more hike, and four saw two. Only two expected the rate to stay where it was. For 2027, the picture splits: eight officials placed their dot at 4.375 percent, implying further tightening, while four expected the rate to end 2027 below the September level. That 2027 spread, running from 3.1 to 4.4 percent in the SEP's range, is a reminder of how uncertain the path beyond a few months really is.

The projections also explain the dots. Officials expected PCE inflation of 3.7 percent for 2026, slightly above June's 3.6 percent, with unemployment steady at 4.1 percent. A committee seeing inflation well above its 2 percent goal and a labor market that isn't cracking has little reason to pencil in cuts.

## Why the dot plot moves markets

Markets already price expectations for Fed policy every day through fed funds futures, which CME Group's FedWatch tool translates into implied probabilities for each meeting. The dot plot matters when it disagrees with that pricing.

If futures expect two cuts next year and the median dot shows none, traders have to decide whether to trust the market or the Fed. Usually some of that gap closes on the spot. The 2-year Treasury yield, which tracks expected policy closely, tends to react first. The 10-year can follow, and from there the move reaches stock valuations, mortgage rates and the dollar. We cover that chain in [how interest rates affect stock valuations](/articles/how-interest-rates-affect-stock-valuations/) and [what the 10-year Treasury yield is](/articles/what-is-the-10-year-treasury-yield/).

A historical example shows how the dots can outweigh the decision. In December 2024, the Fed cut rates, which sounds dovish. But the same release moved the median projection for the end of 2025 up to 3.9 percent, from 3.4 percent in September. That half-point shift equaled two fewer quarter-point cuts. Investors who read only the headline missed the bigger message, a pattern our [FOMC meeting guide](/articles/fomc-meeting-day-what-to-watch/) walks through hour by hour.

The rough playbook looks like this:

| Dot plot surprise | Typical first reaction |
| --- | --- |
| Median shows fewer cuts or more hikes than futures price | Short-term yields rise; rate-sensitive and long-duration growth stocks often lag |
| Median shows more cuts than futures price | Short-term yields fall; small caps and rate-sensitive sectors often gain |
| Median matches pricing but spread widens | Smaller move; uncertainty about later years can lift volatility |
| Longer-run dot rises | Pressure on long-term yields and valuations, since "neutral" is seen higher |

These are tendencies, not rules. The chair's press conference shortly after the release can soften or reinforce the dots, and the first move sometimes reverses by the close.

## Common mistakes when reading the dots

**Treating the median as a forecast of the committee.** The FRED blog stresses that the projections represent neither a committee plan nor a binding decision. The median official doesn't vote on behalf of the others, and the chair's dot is not identified.

**Ignoring who votes.** Every participant submits a dot, but not every participant votes. Eighteen officials sent in projections in September, while the October 28 statement lists 12 voting members. Reserve Bank presidents rotate into voting seats, so a cluster of hawkish dots from non-voters carries less immediate weight than the same view from this year's voters.

**Reading old dots as current.** The September projections were made before October's data and before the October 28 meeting, where the Fed held the range at 3.75 to 4 percent on a 10-2 vote. The two dissenters, Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack, preferred another quarter-point hike. Views can drift a long way in three months, so the dots age quickly.

**Forgetting the forecasts behind them.** If the inflation projection jumps, a higher rate dot is the logical result. If you only look at the rate column, you miss why it moved, and the "why" tells you what data could move it back.

## A quick checklist for December

When the next SEP drops, work through it in this order:

1. Compare the 2026 median with the current 3.875 percent midpoint. Does the committee still expect a move this year, or did the December decision settle it?
2. Look at the 2027 median versus September's 4.1 percent. A drop signals cuts are back on the table; a rise signals more tightening.
3. Count the dots above and below the median for 2027. Is the committee converging or splitting further?
4. Check the longer-run dot against September's 3.2 percent.
5. Read the inflation and unemployment projections. They tell you what officials think will justify the rate path.
6. Compare everything with futures pricing just before the release.
