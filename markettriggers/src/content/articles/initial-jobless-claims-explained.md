**Initial jobless claims count how many people filed for unemployment insurance for the first time in a given week. The Labor Department releases them every Thursday at 8:30 a.m. Eastern, making them the most timely official read on layoffs in the U.S. economy. A sustained rise is one of the earliest signs that the job market is weakening.**

Most economic data arrives a month or more after the fact. Jobless claims are different: the number published on Thursday describes the week that ended the previous Saturday. That speed is why traders, economists and Fed officials all keep an eye on it. For the bigger picture of how labor data fits next to inflation, rates and earnings, see the [employment section of our guide to what moves the stock market](/#employment-the-jobs-report-and-weekly-claims). This article focuses on the weekly claims report itself: what it measures, what it misses and how to read it without overreacting.

## What initial jobless claims measure

When someone loses a job through no fault of their own, they can apply for unemployment benefits through their state. That application is an initial claim. The Labor Department's Employment and Training Administration collects the counts from every state and publishes a national total each week in the [Unemployment Insurance Weekly Claims report](https://www.dol.gov/ui/data.pdf).

The report itself describes initial claims as a leading indicator of emerging labor market conditions, while warning that weekly administrative data can be volatile. Put simply, claims tell you about layoffs as they happen. If companies start cutting staff, the filings show up within days, long before the effect reaches the monthly unemployment rate.

A few details are worth knowing before you read the numbers:

- **The headline is seasonally adjusted.** Layoffs follow predictable calendar patterns: retailers let holiday workers go in January, schools let some staff go in summer, and auto plants have retooling shutdowns. The Labor Department adjusts for these patterns so one week can be compared with the next. It also publishes the unadjusted count.
- **The latest week is an "advance" estimate.** It gets revised a week later as states finish their counts. Revisions are usually small, but they happen.
- **Federal workers and veterans are counted separately.** Claims from federal civilian employees (UCFE) and newly discharged veterans (UCX) appear in their own lines in the release, not in the state headline.
- **Not everyone who loses a job files.** People who quit, workers who aren't eligible under their state's rules and many self-employed or gig workers generally won't show up. Claims measure insured layoffs, not all job losses.

## Initial claims vs. continuing claims

The same release contains a second number that tells a different story. Continuing claims, which the Labor Department calls insured unemployment, count people who are still receiving benefits after their first week. The Labor Department's [claims data page](https://oui.doleta.gov/unemploy/claims.asp) describes initial claims as measuring "emerging unemployment" and continued weeks claimed as measuring "the number of persons claiming unemployment benefits."

Think of initial claims as the inflow and continuing claims as the pool. If layoffs stay low but continuing claims climb, people who do lose their jobs are taking longer to find new ones. That usually points to slow hiring rather than heavy firing. The weekly release calls continuing claims a confirming indicator of the economy's direction, rather than a leading one.

| Measure | What it counts | Timing | What it signals |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Initial claims | First-time filings for state unemployment benefits | Week ending the prior Saturday | New layoffs; an early warning signal |
| 4-week moving average | Average of the last four weeks of initial claims | Same as initial claims | The underlying layoff trend, minus weekly noise |
| Continuing claims (insured unemployment) | People still receiving state benefits | One week behind initial claims | How quickly laid-off workers find new jobs |
| Insured unemployment rate | Continuing claims as a share of covered employment | One week behind initial claims | Pressure in the insured labor force |

## The latest weekly jobless claims report

Here is what the Labor Department reported on Thursday, October 29, 2026, for the week ending October 24:

| Weekly claims, released October 29, 2026 | Latest | Prior week (revised) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initial claims, seasonally adjusted | 194,000 | 195,000 |
| 4-week moving average | 196,750 | 198,000 |
| Continuing claims (week ending Oct. 17) | 1,701,000 | 1,722,000 |
| Insured unemployment rate | 1.1% | 1.1% |
| Initial claims, not seasonally adjusted | 178,851 | 173,203 |

*Source: [U.S. Department of Labor, Unemployment Insurance Weekly Claims, October 29, 2026](https://www.dol.gov/ui/data.pdf).*

The unadjusted count was also about 24,000 lower than the same week in 2025, when it stood at 203,012. Among states, Michigan reported the biggest weekly jump in filings for the week ending October 17, up 1,906, and the state attributed it to layoffs in manufacturing.

On its own, that is a picture of very few layoffs. But it sits awkwardly next to the most recent monthly jobs report. In September, employers added just 29,000 jobs and the unemployment rate rose to 4.2 percent, according to the [BLS Employment Situation](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.htm). How can hiring be that weak while layoffs stay that low?

The two reports measure different things. Claims track firing. Payrolls track the net result of hiring minus separations. An economy can be in a "low hire, low fire" state, where companies hold on to the workers they have but add few new ones. In that kind of market, claims stay quiet even as job growth slows, and the people who are out of work have a harder time getting back in. That's why many analysts read initial claims and continuing claims side by side, and then check them against the monthly payroll numbers. We break down the monthly report in detail in [how the jobs report moves the stock market](/articles/how-the-jobs-report-moves-the-stock-market/).

## Why initial jobless claims matter to investors

Claims matter for the same two reasons almost every labor statistic does: the Federal Reserve and corporate earnings.

**The Fed connection.** Maximum employment is half of the Fed's dual mandate. A steady rise in claims would be one of the first hard signs that the labor market is cracking, and that tends to push markets to price in rate cuts. At its October 27-28 meeting, the FOMC [held the federal funds rate at 3-3/4 to 4 percent](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/files/monetary20261028a1.pdf) with inflation still above its goal. In that kind of standoff, every labor data point gets extra attention, because a clear break in either direction could tip the debate. Our [FOMC meeting day guide](/articles/fomc-meeting-day-what-to-watch/) explains how those expectations get built into prices.

**The earnings connection.** Layoffs reduce household income, and household income pays for consumer spending. A broad rise in claims would raise questions about sales for retailers, restaurants, travel companies and lenders.

**A recognized leading indicator.** The Conference Board includes average weekly initial claims, inverted, as one of the ten components of its [Leading Economic Index](https://www.conference-board.org/pdf_free/press/US%20LEI%20Technical%20Notes%20-%20July%202026.pdf). Inverted means rising claims pull the index down. Its role there reflects the same idea: layoffs tend to turn before the broader economy does.

How the stock market actually reacts depends on what investors are worried about at the moment:

- **When recession is the fear,** a jump in claims can hit stocks, especially cyclical sectors and small caps, because it hints at falling earnings.
- **When inflation is the fear,** low claims can be read as a sign the labor market is still tight, which can push Treasury yields higher and lower the odds of cuts. Rate-sensitive groups such as homebuilders, utilities and long-duration growth stocks often feel that most. We explain the mechanism in [how interest rates affect stock valuations](/articles/how-interest-rates-affect-stock-valuations/).
- **In calm periods,** a weekly claims number within the usual range often barely registers. The report tends to move markets only when it surprises forecasts by a wide margin or breaks an established trend.

## A real example: March 2020

The most dramatic claims reports on record came at the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, and they show how fast this indicator can turn.

For the week ending March 14, 2020, seasonally adjusted initial claims were 282,000, a normal level. One week later, everything changed. The Labor Department's [March 26, 2020 release](https://dol.gov/newsroom/releases/eta/eta20200326) reported 3,283,000 initial claims for the week ending March 21, an increase of 3,001,000. The release called it the highest level in the history of the seasonally adjusted series; the previous high was 695,000, in October 1982. It added that nearly every state providing comments cited the impact of the COVID-19 virus.

The next week was worse. The [April 2, 2020 release](https://dol.gov/newsroom/releases/eta/eta20200402) put claims for the week ending March 28 at 6,648,000, and revised the prior week up to 3,307,000.

Two lessons stand out. First, claims caught the collapse in the job market weeks before the monthly unemployment rate could. Second, continuing claims lagged: insured unemployment for the week ending March 21 was 3,029,000, up 1,245,000, while initial claims were already showing millions of new filings. The inflow shows up first; the pool fills afterward.

March 2020 was an extreme case driven by a sudden shutdown. Normal downturns build more slowly, with claims creeping up over several months rather than exploding in a week. That's why the trend matters more than any single print.

## How to read weekly jobless claims without overreacting

A single Thursday number rarely tells you much. Here is a simple routine for reading the report:

1. **Start with the 4-week moving average.** It filters out the holiday weeks, storms and one-off plant closures that distort single weeks.
2. **Compare with the consensus forecast.** Markets trade surprises. A number that matches expectations usually does little, even if it looks high or low in isolation.
3. **Check continuing claims.** Rising continuing claims with flat initial claims points to slow hiring, not mass layoffs.
4. **Look at the unadjusted numbers around holidays.** Seasonal factors can struggle around Thanksgiving, the year-end holidays and summer auto shutdowns. If the adjusted number looks odd, compare the raw count with the same week a year earlier.
5. **Read the state comments.** The release lists the states with the largest changes and often notes why, such as manufacturing layoffs or weather. One state's event can move the national total.
6. **Watch the 2-year Treasury yield.** It tells you how bond traders read the report for Fed policy, which usually sets the tone for stocks.

You can pull the full history of the series from the Labor Department's [weekly claims data page](https://oui.doleta.gov/unemploy/claims.asp), or chart it through the St. Louis Fed's [FRED database](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/ICSA).

## Where jobless claims fit in the economic calendar

Claims are one piece of a larger labor picture. They come out weekly, the monthly jobs report arrives on the first Friday of most months, and inflation data like the [CPI](/articles/what-is-core-cpi/) shapes how the Fed weighs all of it. A useful way to think about it: claims are the smoke detector, payrolls are the full inspection. A smoke detector can go off over burnt toast, so you confirm with the bigger report before deciding the house is on fire.
