**To read an earnings report in about 10 minutes, check five things in order: revenue and earnings per share against analyst estimates, the company's guidance for the next period, the quality of the profit (GAAP vs. adjusted), margins and cash flow, and what management said on the earnings call. Guidance usually matters most to the stock.**

Earnings season can feel like drinking from a fire hose. Hundreds of companies report within a few weeks, each with a press release, a slide deck, a conference call and eventually a long SEC filing. You don't need to read all of it. You need to know which pages answer which questions, and in what order. This guide gives you that routine. For how earnings fit alongside inflation data and the Fed, see the [earnings section of our guide to what moves the stock market](/#earnings-reports).

## What an earnings report actually is

"Earnings report" is shorthand for a bundle of documents that arrive over a few hours or weeks:

| Document | When it appears | What it's good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Earnings press release | Usually before the open or after the close on earnings day | Headline numbers, guidance, management's framing |
| Form 8-K | Often filed the same day as the release | The official SEC copy of the release, on EDGAR |
| Investor slides | Same day, on the investor relations site | Segment details and the metrics management wants you to see |
| Earnings call | Usually within hours of the release | Tone, analyst questions, details not in the release |
| Form 10-Q (or 10-K for the year) | Days to weeks later | Full financial statements, footnotes, risk factors, MD&A |

The SEC's Investor.gov bulletin on Form 8-K notes that companies often release quarterly results in a press release and an 8-K at the same time, and that those figures typically summarize the full financial statements that come later in the 10-Q or 10-K. Most 8-K disclosures are due within four business days of the triggering event.

One detail worth knowing: the 10-Q includes **unaudited** financial statements. The audited set comes once a year in the 10-K. That doesn't make quarterly numbers unreliable, but it's one reason companies sometimes revise them.

For a 10-minute read, the press release does most of the work. The 10-Q is where you go later if something looks off.

## Minute 1–2: Revenue and EPS against expectations

Start with the two headline numbers.

**Revenue** is the top line. The SEC's *Beginners' Guide to Financial Statements* describes it as the total amount of money brought in from sales, shown at the top of the income statement. Revenue tells you about demand: are customers buying more or less?

**Earnings per share (EPS)** is the bottom line, scaled to one share. The same SEC guide explains the calculation: take total net income and divide it by the number of outstanding shares. Most releases show both *basic* EPS and *diluted* EPS. Diluted EPS assumes things like stock options and convertible securities turn into shares, so it is usually a little lower than basic EPS.

Neither number means much in isolation. What moves a stock is the gap between the result and what investors expected. Analysts publish forecasts before each report, and their average is the **consensus estimate**. You'll typically find it on financial news sites or your brokerage's quote page, since companies don't publish it themselves.

Here's the quick read on EPS vs. revenue:

- **Beat on both:** Demand was strong and the company turned it into profit. The cleanest outcome.
- **EPS beat, revenue miss:** Profit came from cost cuts, a lower tax rate, buybacks or one-off items rather than sales. Investors often treat this skeptically.
- **Revenue beat, EPS miss:** Sales were fine but costs rose faster. Check whether that spending is a deliberate investment or a margin problem.
- **Miss on both:** Usually bad, unless expectations were already very low and the guidance surprises to the upside.

Also compare with the same quarter last year, not just the prior quarter. Many businesses are seasonal, and a retailer's holiday quarter will dwarf its spring quarter every year.

## Minute 3–4: Guidance, the part the stock actually trades on

Next, find the outlook. Many companies give **guidance**: a forecast, often a range, for next quarter's or the full year's revenue, EPS, margins or other key figures. It's usually near the top of the release or in its own section labeled "Outlook."

Guidance matters because a stock's price reflects expected future profits. A great quarter followed by a lowered forecast tells investors the next year looks worse than they assumed. That's the most common reason a stock drops after a beat, which we break down in [why stocks fall after beating earnings](/articles/why-do-stocks-fall-after-earnings/).

When you read guidance, ask three questions:

1. **Compared with what?** Measure the new range against the analyst consensus for that future period, not just against the company's old guidance.
2. **Midpoint or edges?** If the range is wide, the midpoint is what most people compare. A raised top end with an unchanged bottom end is a weaker signal than a raised midpoint.
3. **What changed?** A company that raises full-year guidance by roughly the amount it just beat by is mostly passing through the beat. A raise larger than the beat signals that management expects the rest of the year to improve too.

Not every company gives guidance, and some only give it once a year. If there's none, the call becomes more important, because analysts will try to pull an outlook out of management.

## Minute 5–6: GAAP vs. adjusted earnings

Most large companies report two versions of their profit. **GAAP** results follow generally accepted accounting principles. **Adjusted** or **non-GAAP** results strip out items management considers unusual or non-cash, such as restructuring charges, acquisition costs, stock-based compensation or legal settlements.

Analyst consensus is often built on the adjusted number, so headlines usually report beats and misses on that basis. That's fine, as long as you look at what was removed.

SEC rules help you here. A company that publishes a non-GAAP measure must also present the most directly comparable GAAP measure and a quantitative reconciliation between the two. In filed documents, the GAAP figure has to be shown with equal or greater prominence. Find the reconciliation table, usually near the back of the release, and scan it for two things:

- **Size of the gap.** If adjusted EPS is far above GAAP EPS, the adjustments are doing a lot of work.
- **Repeat offenders.** A "one-time" restructuring charge that shows up every quarter for three years isn't one-time. Stock-based compensation is a real cost to shareholders even though no cash leaves the building, because it dilutes ownership.

We cover [GAAP vs. adjusted earnings](/articles/gaap-vs-adjusted-earnings/) in depth in a separate guide. For now, the rule of thumb: the adjusted number tells you how management wants to be measured, the GAAP number tells you what actually happened, and the reconciliation tells you why they differ.

## Minute 7–8: Margins and cash flow

Profit can rise because sales rose, or because the company kept more of each dollar. Margins tell you which.

The SEC's guide walks down the income statement: subtract the cost of sales from revenue to get **gross profit**, then subtract operating expenses to get **operating profit**, and further down you reach **net income**, the literal bottom line. Divide each by revenue and you have gross, operating and net margin.

| Metric | Formula | What a change usually signals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gross margin | Gross profit ÷ revenue | Pricing power and input costs |
| Operating margin | Operating income ÷ revenue | Cost discipline across the whole business |
| Net margin | Net income ÷ revenue | Everything, including interest and taxes |
| Operating cash flow | From the cash flow statement | Whether profits are turning into actual cash |

A falling gross margin often points to discounting or rising costs the company couldn't pass on, the same squeeze we describe in [how inflation affects stocks](/articles/how-does-inflation-affect-stocks/). A rising operating margin with flat gross margin usually means the company cut overhead.

Then glance at cash. The SEC guide describes the cash flow statement as a record of a company's cash inflows and outflows, split into operating, investing and financing activities. Not every release includes a full cash flow statement, but many include operating cash flow or free cash flow. If net income keeps climbing while operating cash flow lags quarter after quarter, find out why. Sometimes it's harmless, like customers paying later at year-end. Sometimes it's a warning.

Also look for the **key metric** that defines the business. For a retailer it may be comparable-store sales; for a software company, subscriptions or remaining performance obligations; for a bank, net interest income. Management usually highlights it in the first paragraph or the first few slides. If it's suddenly missing, that can be telling too.

## Minute 9–10: The earnings call

Calls typically start with prepared remarks from the CEO and CFO, followed by questions from analysts. You don't need to listen to the whole thing live. Many companies post a replay and a transcript on their investor relations site.

Under the SEC's Regulation FD, when a company shares material nonpublic information with market professionals such as analysts, or with shareholders who might trade on it, it must also disclose that information publicly. That's a big reason calls are open to everyone by webcast: you hear the same answers the analysts do.

In 10 minutes, focus on:

- **The first analyst question.** It's often about whatever worried investors most in the release.
- **Repeated topics.** If three analysts ask about the same customer, product or margin line, that's where the uncertainty is.
- **Changes in language.** Words like "cautious," "softness," "normalizing" or "elongated sales cycles" can matter more than a decimal in EPS.
- **Non-answers.** A company that declines to repeat or update guidance it gave last quarter is sending a message.

It's common for a stock to move one way in after-hours trading on the release, then reverse during the call. The call is where the market finishes forming its view.

## A worked example (hypothetical)

Imagine a fictional retailer, Northfield Outfitters, reports its third quarter. All numbers below are made up for illustration.

- **Revenue:** $2.10 billion vs. a $2.05 billion consensus. Beat.
- **Adjusted EPS:** $1.42 vs. $1.35 consensus. Beat. **GAAP EPS:** $1.10.
- **Reconciliation:** The $0.32 gap is mostly a store-closure charge, the third quarter in a row it has appeared.
- **Gross margin:** down from a year earlier, which the release attributes to heavier discounting.
- **Guidance:** full-year EPS range trimmed at the top end, midpoint now below the full-year consensus.
- **Call:** two analysts ask about holiday inventory levels; management calls the consumer "choppy."

Headline: "Northfield beats on revenue and earnings." Likely reality: a falling stock. The beat came alongside thinner margins, a recurring "one-time" charge and a lower outlook. Ten minutes of reading tells you more than the headline does.

## Where to find the documents

- **Investor relations site:** Search the company name plus "investor relations." Releases, slides and call replays live here.
- **SEC EDGAR:** The [EDGAR full-text search](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/) has every 8-K, 10-Q and 10-K, free. For a walkthrough of the annual report, the SEC's Investor.gov has a guide on how to read a 10-K.
- **Earnings calendar:** Companies usually announce their report date and call time a few weeks ahead in a press release on their IR site.

## A quick checklist to keep

1. Revenue and EPS vs. consensus, and vs. the same quarter last year.
2. Guidance vs. consensus for the next period.
3. Size and nature of the GAAP-to-adjusted gap.
4. Gross and operating margin direction, plus operating cash flow.
5. The business's key metric.
6. The call: first question, repeated topics, tone.

None of these on its own tells you whether to buy or sell. Together, they tell you what changed, and that's what the market is trying to price.
