**Inflation affects stocks mainly through three channels: it pushes interest rates higher, it squeezes the profit margins of companies that can't pass on rising costs, and it lowers the value investors place on future earnings.** Mild, steady inflation is usually manageable for the market. It's high or *surprising* inflation that tends to cause real damage, because it forces the Federal Reserve to act.

That distinction matters. A 3% inflation rate that everyone expected barely registers in stock prices. A monthly CPI report that comes in a few tenths of a percent above forecasts can move the entire market in minutes. If you want the broader picture first, our [complete guide to what moves the stock market](/) explains how expectations shape every market reaction.

## What inflation actually measures

Inflation is the rate at which the general level of prices rises. In the U.S., the two numbers investors watch most are:

- **The Consumer Price Index (CPI)**, published monthly by the [Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/). It tracks prices urban consumers pay for a broad basket of goods and services.
- **The Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index**, published by the [Bureau of Economic Analysis](https://www.bea.gov/data/personal-consumption-expenditures-price-index). This is the gauge the Federal Reserve uses for its 2% inflation goal.

Both come in two flavors. *Headline* inflation includes everything. *Core* inflation strips out food and energy, which swing sharply from month to month, and is usually a better read on the underlying trend. Markets also care a great deal about the month-over-month change, because it shows current momentum rather than what happened a year ago.

## Channel 1: Inflation pushes interest rates up

This is the biggest channel by far, and it runs through the Federal Reserve.

The Fed's job, set by Congress, is to pursue maximum employment and stable prices. When inflation runs above its target, the Fed's main tool is to raise the federal funds rate or keep it high. Even the *expectation* of that response moves markets immediately: Treasury yields rise as traders price in tighter policy.

Higher interest rates hurt stock prices in several ways at once:

1. **Future profits are worth less today.** A stock's value reflects the earnings a company is expected to produce for years to come, discounted back to the present. A higher discount rate shrinks that present value.
2. **Bonds become real competition.** When safe Treasury bonds pay more, investors demand a higher expected return from riskier stocks, which means lower prices for the same earnings.
3. **Borrowing gets more expensive.** Companies pay more to finance growth, and consumers pay more on mortgages, car loans, and credit cards, which can slow spending.

The effect is strongest for **growth stocks**, companies whose value depends on profits expected far in the future. A small change in the discount rate makes a big difference to a cash flow ten years out. That's why fast-growing technology companies often fall hardest on a hot inflation report. Our [Fed section](/#interest-rates-and-stock-valuations) walks through this math in plain English.

## Channel 2: Inflation squeezes profit margins

Inflation raises a company's costs: wages, raw materials, shipping, energy, rent. What happens next depends on **pricing power**, a company's ability to raise its own prices without losing customers.

| Company type | Typical position during inflation | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strong brands, essential products | Can often pass costs on | Customers keep buying even at higher prices |
| Commodity producers (oil, metals) | Revenue may rise with prices | They sell the thing that's getting more expensive |
| Businesses with fixed-price contracts | Margins get squeezed | Costs rise but revenue is locked in |
| Highly competitive, low-margin sellers | Margins get squeezed | Raising prices sends customers to rivals |
| Heavily indebted companies with floating-rate debt | Squeezed twice | Higher costs *and* higher interest payments |

These are general tendencies, not rules. During earnings season, listen for how management talks about pricing, input costs, and margins. Those comments often matter more to the stock than the headline profit number. Our explainer on [why stocks sometimes fall after beating earnings](/articles/why-do-stocks-fall-after-earnings/) covers how to read that commentary.

## Channel 3: Inflation lowers valuations

Even if a company's earnings keep up with inflation, investors typically pay *less* for each dollar of those earnings when inflation is high and uncertain. This shows up as a lower price-to-earnings ratio across the market.

Part of this is the interest-rate effect from Channel 1. Part of it is uncertainty itself: when nobody knows how high prices will go or how far the Fed will need to push rates, investors demand extra compensation for taking risk. That extra compensation, the risk premium, comes out of today's stock prices.

## Real returns: the number that actually matters

If your portfolio rises 6% in a year when inflation runs at 4%, your purchasing power grew by only about 2%. That's your **real return**, and it's the number that tells you whether you're actually getting wealthier.

Over long periods, stocks have generally delivered positive real returns, because company revenues and earnings tend to rise along with prices. Over shorter stretches, especially when inflation jumps unexpectedly, stocks can lose ground in real terms even when their price in dollars looks stable.

## What history shows

Two periods illustrate the range of outcomes:

- **The 1970s:** High and persistent inflation, combined with slow growth (often called *stagflation*), was a difficult environment for stocks. Real, inflation-adjusted returns were poor for much of the decade.
- **2022:** As inflation climbed far above the Fed's target and the central bank raised rates aggressively, U.S. stocks fell, and the S&P 500 entered a bear market. Unusually, bonds fell at the same time, because rising rates hurt both. It was a textbook case of the interest-rate channel dominating.

By contrast, long stretches of low, stable inflation, such as much of the 2010s, coincided with rising stock prices and relatively low interest rates.

## Which sectors tend to hold up

No sector is immune, but some have historically fared better when inflation is high:

- **Energy and materials:** When inflation is driven by commodity prices, producers of oil, gas, and metals can see revenue rise.
- **Consumer staples:** Makers of everyday necessities often have enough pricing power to protect margins, though not always.
- **Financials:** Some banks benefit from higher interest rates on loans, although rising rates can also create losses on bond holdings.

Sectors that tend to struggle include **long-duration growth stocks**, **real estate** (sensitive to borrowing costs), and **utilities** (often owned for steady income that looks less attractive when bond yields rise). ## How markets react on CPI day

The CPI report comes out at 8:30 a.m. Eastern Time, an hour before the stock market opens. Here's how to read the reaction like an analyst:

1. **Check the consensus forecast** before the release. The surprise, not the number itself, drives the move.
2. **Look at core, month over month.** That's what the Fed and traders focus on first.
3. **Watch the 2-year Treasury yield.** It reacts fastest to changes in Fed expectations. A sharp rise signals that traders now expect tighter policy.
4. **Read the details.** Shelter costs and services prices have been closely watched components. A hot headline driven by a one-time energy spike is read differently from broad-based increases.
5. **Wait for the dust to settle.** The first few minutes are often noisy. The move that lasts usually reflects the details, not the headline.

## What this means for long-term investors

Understanding inflation helps you make sense of market swings. It isn't a reason to overhaul a long-term plan every time a CPI report surprises. A few principles hold up well:

- **Diversification helps.** Different assets respond differently to inflation, so a mix reduces the impact of any one scenario.
- **Time horizon matters.** Inflation scares can be painful in the short run. Over decades, earnings growth has historically done much of the work.
- **Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS)** are U.S. government bonds whose principal adjusts with CPI. They are designed specifically to protect against inflation, and the [U.S. Treasury](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/tips/) explains how they work.
