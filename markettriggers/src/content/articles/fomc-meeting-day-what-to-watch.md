**An FOMC meeting is a two-day private session of the Fed's policy committee that ends with a public release at 2:00 p.m. Eastern on day two: the policy statement, an Implementation Note and, four times a year, new economic projections. The chair's press conference follows at 2:30. Markets react to each piece separately.**

That's the short version of the FOMC meeting explained. The longer version matters because the decision itself is often the least surprising part of the day. Most of the market's reaction comes from wording, projections and tone, and those arrive in a set order. If you know the order, you can follow a Fed day the way a bond trader does. For how the Fed fits alongside inflation data, jobs and earnings, see the [Fed section of our guide to what moves the stock market](/#what-actually-moves-markets-on-fed-day).

## FOMC meeting explained: the two-day structure

The Federal Open Market Committee sets the target range for the federal funds rate. It holds eight scheduled meetings a year, and each one now runs two days. According to the [Fed's meeting calendar](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm), the remaining 2026 meetings are October 27-28 and December 8-9. The calendar also notes that each date is tentative until confirmed at the meeting before it.

Day one is closed, and nothing is published. Day two is when the committee votes and the public finally sees the result.

Four of the eight meetings each year come with a Summary of Economic Projections, marked with an asterisk on the calendar. In 2026 those were March, June and September, with December still ahead. October 27-28 is a meeting without new projections, which changes what investors focus on. More on that below.

## The quiet before: the blackout period

The run-up to a meeting starts well before day one. Under the FOMC's [policy on external communications](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/files/FOMC_ExtCommunicationParticipants.pdf), participants enter a blackout at 12:00 a.m. Eastern on the second Saturday before a meeting. During that window they refrain from sharing views on the economy or monetary policy with the public. The blackout ends at 11:59 p.m. Eastern the day after the meeting.

The Fed publishes the dates in advance. For the October 27-28 meeting, the [blackout calendar](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/files/fomc-blackout-period-calendar.pdf) shows it running October 17 through October 29. For December 8-9, it runs November 28 through December 10.

Why it matters: during the blackout, no Fed speeches nudge expectations. That makes the last week or so of pre-meeting pricing a fairly clean read of what the market expects, and it's the baseline every surprise gets measured against.

Before 2:00 p.m. on decision day, it helps to check that baseline. Fed funds futures show market-implied odds for each outcome, and the [CME FedWatch Tool](https://www.cmegroup.com/markets/interest-rates/cme-fedwatch-tool.html) turns those prices into percentages. If a move is priced at very high odds, the decision alone shouldn't shock anyone.

## Fed day, hour by hour

Here's how the public side of a meeting unfolds. All times are Eastern. The press conference time comes from the Fed's own [events calendar for October 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/2026-october.htm).

| When | What happens | What to look for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Second Saturday before the meeting | Blackout begins | Last Fed speeches before it start shaping expectations |
| Day 1 | Committee meets privately | No Fed release; markets trade on other news |
| Day 2, morning | Committee concludes and votes | Positioning, quiet trading in rate futures |
| Day 2, 2:00 p.m. | Statement and Implementation Note; SEP at four meetings a year | The rate decision, wording changes, dissents, the median dot |
| Day 2, 2:30 p.m. | Chair's press conference | Tone, answers on the next meeting, pushback on market pricing |
| Day after, 11:59 p.m. | Blackout ends | Fed officials can explain their votes in speeches |
| Three weeks later, 2:00 p.m. | Minutes released | How many officials leaned each way |

### 2:00 p.m.: the statement

The statement is short, usually a handful of paragraphs, and investors read it against the previous one almost word by word. It contains:

- **The decision** on the federal funds target range.
- **A description of the economy**, covering growth, the job market and inflation.
- **Forward guidance**, a sentence or two on how the committee is thinking about future moves.
- **The vote**, listing every voting member and naming anyone who dissented, along with what that person preferred.

A dissent is easy to miss and often important. It shows the committee isn't unanimous and hints at which way the next debate leans.

### 2:00 p.m.: the Implementation Note

Released alongside the statement and linked from it, the Implementation Note is the technical instruction sheet. It tells the New York Fed's trading desk how to carry out the decision and sets the administered rates the Fed uses to keep the federal funds rate inside its target range, including interest on reserve balances and the overnight reverse repo rate. These appear among the Fed's listed [policy tools](https://federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/policytools.htm). Stock investors tend to skip it; money market traders read it closely.

### 2:00 p.m.: projections and the dot plot (four meetings a year)

At meetings marked with an asterisk, the Fed also releases the [Summary of Economic Projections](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/summary-economic-projections-sep.htm). Each participant submits projections for growth, unemployment, inflation and the appropriate policy rate for the current year, the next two years and the longer run. The rate projections are what the market calls the dot plot.

The Fed describes these as each participant's view of the most likely outcome. They're forecasts from individuals, not a committee promise. Even so, the median dot for the current and next year is often the single number that moves markets most on an SEP day, because it compares directly with what futures are pricing.

### 2:30 p.m.: the press conference

Thirty minutes after the release, the chair takes questions. The prepared opening remarks usually restate the statement. The live answers are where surprises happen. Reporters ask whether the next meeting is "live," whether markets are pricing too many or too few moves, and how the chair reads the latest data. A single phrase can shift rate expectations within seconds.

## What each piece can tell you

| Release | Main question it answers | Market most sensitive to it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Statement decision | Did rates change? | Fed funds futures, 2-year Treasury |
| Statement wording | Has the committee's view shifted? | Short-term yields, the dollar |
| Dissents | Is the committee split? | Expectations for the next meeting |
| Implementation Note | How will the decision be carried out? | Money markets, repo rates |
| SEP and dot plot | Where do officials see rates heading? | 2-year yield, growth stocks |
| Press conference | How firm is that view? | Stocks and bonds, often with sharp intraday swings |
| Minutes | How broad was the agreement? | Expectations for the next meeting |

The 2-year Treasury yield is a good real-time gauge because it is closely tied to where investors expect the policy rate to go over the next couple of years. Stocks follow through the valuation channel: higher expected rates reduce the present value of future earnings. Our explainer on [how inflation affects stocks](/articles/how-does-inflation-affect-stocks/) walks through that mechanism.

## A real example: December 18, 2024

The December 2024 meeting shows how the decision can be the least surprising part of the day.

The committee [cut its target range by a quarter point to 4-1/4 to 4-1/2 percent](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20241218a.htm). The cut itself was widely expected. Three other details carried the message.

First, the wording. The November 2024 statement had said the committee would weigh new data "[in considering additional adjustments](https://federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20241107a.htm)" to the target range. December's version added a phrase: the committee would consider "the extent and timing of additional adjustments." Four extra words told readers that the pace of cuts was now up for debate.

Second, a dissent. The vote was 11 to 1. Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack voted against the cut, preferring to hold the range at 4-1/2 to 4-3/4 percent.

Third, the dots. The [December 2024 projections](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomcprojtabl20241218.htm) moved the median expected federal funds rate for the end of 2025 up to 3.9 percent, from 3.4 percent in September.

| Median federal funds rate projection | September 2024 SEP | December 2024 SEP |
| --- | --- | --- |
| End of 2025 | 3.4% | 3.9% |
| End of 2026 | 2.9% | 3.4% |
| Longer run | 2.9% | 3.0% |

*Source: [Federal Reserve, Summary of Economic Projections, December 18, 2024](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomcprojtabl20241218.htm).*

A half-point gap in the 2025 median roughly equals two fewer quarter-point cuts. So the Fed cut rates and, in the same release, signaled fewer cuts ahead. Anyone watching only the headline decision would have read the day as dovish. The wording, the dissent and the dots all pointed the other way. That's the pattern our article on [what happens to stocks when the Fed cuts rates](/articles/what-happens-to-stocks-when-the-fed-cuts-rates/) describes: guidance can outweigh the move itself.

## Where things stand before October 27-28, 2026

The backdrop for the next meeting is unusual. At its September 15-16 meeting, the FOMC [raised the target range by a quarter point to 3-3/4 to 4 percent](https://federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20260916a.htm), in a 12-0 vote. The statement described inflation as still elevated and said the increase would support a timelier return to the 2 percent goal.

The [September 2026 projections](https://federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomcprojtabl20260916.htm) put the median federal funds rate at 4.1 percent for the end of 2026 and 4.1 percent for 2027. The median for core PCE inflation in 2026 was 3.4 percent. With the range now centered at 3.875 percent, a 4.1 percent median for year-end is consistent with one more quarter-point increase in 2026 for the median participant.

Inflation data since then has been mixed in a way that matters for Fed watchers. As we covered in [What Is Core CPI and Why Does the Fed Watch It?](/articles/what-is-core-cpi/), September's headline CPI rose 3.7 percent from a year earlier, lifted by gasoline, while core CPI rose 2.6 percent. The Fed's 2 percent goal is defined using PCE, and our [CPI vs. PCE](/articles/cpi-vs-pce/) explainer covers why that distinction matters.

Because October is a meeting without projections, there's no new dot plot to anchor the reaction. That puts more weight on the statement's wording and the press conference. Questions likely to matter: whether the statement's inflation language changes, whether anyone dissents in either direction, and whether the chair treats December as a live meeting.

## A hypothetical: reading a "no change" day

Here's a hypothetical, not a forecast, to show how the pieces interact.

Say futures price a high chance the Fed holds rates steady, and it does. At 2:00 p.m., the statement repeats September's inflation language almost unchanged. Short-term yields barely move. At 2:30, a reporter asks whether another increase is likely this year, and the chair answers that the committee is prepared to act if inflation doesn't improve. The 2-year yield ticks up, and rate-sensitive stocks give back early gains.

Nothing in the decision surprised anyone. The reaction came from 30 seconds of a press conference answer. Then, three weeks later, the minutes show how many participants agreed with that view, and the market adjusts again.

## A Fed-day checklist

- **The week before:** Note what futures imply for the decision and for the next meeting. That's the expectation baseline.
- **At 2:00 p.m.:** Check the decision, then put the new statement next to the last one and look for changed words.
- **Same moment:** Read the vote line for dissents.
- **At SEP meetings:** Compare the median dot for this year and next with what futures were pricing.
- **At 2:30 p.m.:** Listen for answers about the next meeting, and watch the 2-year yield as the chair speaks.
- **The next day:** See whether the first move held. Fed speeches resume once the blackout ends.
- **Three weeks later:** Read the minutes for words like "most," "many" and "a few" that show how broad the agreement was.
