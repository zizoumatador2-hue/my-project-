**The ISM Manufacturing PMI is a monthly survey-based index of U.S. factory activity from the Institute for Supply Management. It averages five measures: new orders, production, employment, supplier deliveries and inventories. A reading above 50 signals expansion, and below 50 signals contraction. In October 2026 it came in at 55.5.**

This is usually the first major U.S. economic report of any given month. It lands before the jobs report and well before the inflation data, so investors get an early read on demand, hiring and prices from the people who actually buy the materials. It fits into the broader set of data covered in our [guide to what moves the stock market](/), and it is one of several monthly releases we track in the [economic indicators hub](/economic-indicators/).

## What the ISM Manufacturing PMI measures

ISM asks purchasing and supply executives across the country a simple question about their U.S. operations: compared with last month, did this measure go up, go down or stay the same? The panel is grouped by industry and weighted by each industry's contribution to GDP, so a large sector counts for more than a small one.

There is no dollar figure anywhere in that question. A company that grew orders by 1 percent and a company that grew them by 40 percent both count as "higher." That is the main thing to understand about a PMI. It measures how widespread a change is, not how big it is.

Each answer feeds into a diffusion index, which equals the percentage of respondents reporting an increase plus half of those reporting no change.

Here is a hypothetical. Suppose 30 percent of purchasing managers say new orders rose, 50 percent say they held steady and 20 percent say they fell. The index is 30 + (50 ÷ 2), or 55. If everyone said "no change," the result would be exactly 50, which is why that number works as the dividing line.

ISM then seasonally adjusts new orders, production, employment and inventories to remove regular patterns tied to weather, holidays and institutional schedules. Supplier deliveries and prices are left unadjusted.

## The five parts of the PMI

The headline number is a simple equal-weighted average of five indexes. Nothing else goes into it. The report also includes five more indexes that sit outside the composite, and experienced readers often spend more time on those than on the headline.

| Index | October 2026 | September 2026 | In the PMI? | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| New Orders | 55.5 | 55.3 | Yes | Demand coming down the pipeline |
| Production | 59.0 | 56.7 | Yes | Current factory output |
| Employment | 52.4 | 52.7 | Yes | Whether factories are adding workers |
| Supplier Deliveries | 59.1 | 59.0 | Yes | Above 50 means deliveries are slower |
| Inventories | 51.4 | 48.6 | Yes | Whether factories hold more materials |
| Prices | 75.2 | 77.9 | No | Share of firms paying more for inputs |
| Backlog of Orders | 54.6 | 56.4 | No | Work waiting to be filled |
| New Export Orders | 51.2 | 50.9 | No | Demand from overseas |
| Imports | 52.9 | 51.0 | No | Materials arriving from abroad |
| Customers' Inventories | 41.6 | 41.6 | No | Below 50 means customers' stocks look too low |

*Source: [ISM, October 2026 Manufacturing PMI Report](https://www.ismworld.org/globalassets/pub/research-and-surveys/rob/pmi/r4in202610pmi.pdf).*

You can check the math yourself. Add the five October components and divide by five: (55.5 + 59.0 + 52.4 + 59.1 + 51.4) ÷ 5 comes to about 55.5.

Supplier deliveries is the odd one. Slower deliveries raise the index, on the logic that suppliers usually fall behind when they are busy. A shipping snarl or a parts shortage can push the reading up too, though, which has nothing to do with healthy demand. So a PMI held up by slow deliveries deserves a second look.

## Why 50 and 47.5 are different lines

Two thresholds appear in every report, and they answer different questions.

**50 is the line for manufacturing itself.** Above it, the factory sector is generally expanding. Below it, contracting.

**47.5 is the line for the whole economy.** ISM says a Manufacturing PMI above that level, over time, generally indicates that overall GDP is expanding. The gap exists because factories are a small slice of output. Manufacturing value added was about 9.7 percent of U.S. GDP in the second quarter of 2026, according to [Bureau of Economic Analysis data on FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/VAPGDPMA). Services can keep the economy growing while factories shrink, and that is a reasonable description of recent years. PNC economists noted that U.S. manufacturing [contracted for most of the three years before 2026](https://www.pnc.com/content/dam/pnc-com/pdf/aboutpnc/EconomicReports/EconomicUpdates/2026/PNC_Economics_Research_ISM_Mfg_1_October_2026.pdf).

A low reading therefore doesn't automatically mean recession. A PMI of 48 says factories are shrinking while the economy as a whole may be doing fine.

## What the October 2026 report showed

ISM published the October data on November 2, 2026. The Manufacturing PMI rose 1.0 point to 55.5, the 10th straight month of expansion after a 10-month contraction. Twelve manufacturing industries reported growth. Production reached 59.0, its highest reading since November 2021.

Read past the headline and the picture gets more mixed. Four details stand out:

- **Inventories did a lot of the lifting.** Each component carries one-fifth of the index, so inventories' 2.8-point jump added roughly 0.56 points to the PMI, and production's 2.3-point gain added about 0.46. New orders barely moved, up 0.2 point.
- **Backlogs eased.** The backlog index fell 1.8 points to 54.6. It is still growing, but more slowly.
- **Hiring didn't speed up.** Employment slipped to 52.4 while staying above 50 for a fourth month.
- **Prices stayed very high.** The Prices Index dropped 2.7 points yet still came in at 75.2, and it has been above 50 for 25 months. Because this is a diffusion index, 75.2 does not mean prices rose 75 percent. It means reports of higher prices far outnumbered reports of lower ones.

That combination, solid activity with widespread cost increases, is a difficult one for the Federal Reserve. The FOMC [held the federal funds rate at 3-3/4 to 4 percent](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/files/monetary20261028a1.pdf) at its October 27-28 meeting, with inflation still above its goal.

## Why the new orders index gets so much attention

Orders come before output. A factory has to book the work before it can build the product, so investors often treat the new orders index as the forward-looking piece of the report. A headline that rises while new orders fall can mean output is being sustained by old backlog rather than fresh demand.

Many analysts also compare new orders with inventories. The logic is mechanical: when orders grow faster than stockpiles, factories may need to step up production to catch up. When inventories build while orders flatten, the reverse risk appears. In September, new orders stood 6.7 points above inventories. In October, that gap narrowed to 4.1 points, even as the headline improved. One month doesn't make a trend, but it's a good example of how the composite can hide a shift underneath.

Customers' inventories add another angle. A reading of 41.6 means purchasing managers judge their customers' stock levels as too low, and it has stayed in that territory for 25 months. Thin customer inventories can support future orders, because those buyers eventually need to restock. They don't guarantee it, since customers may simply prefer to run lean.

## How the ISM report can move markets

The report touches stocks through a few different channels:

1. **Growth expectations.** A strong reading can lift earnings hopes for industrial, materials and other economically sensitive companies. A weak one can do the opposite.
2. **Interest rates.** Strong activity or a hot Prices Index can push Treasury yields higher if traders expect a tighter Fed. Weak data can pull yields down. Our article on [how interest rates affect stock valuations](/articles/how-interest-rates-affect-stock-valuations/) explains why that matters for share prices.
3. **Inflation signals.** The Prices Index arrives before the [Producer Price Index](/articles/producer-price-index-explained/) and the CPI, so it offers an early hint of cost pressure, though it says nothing about the actual size of price changes.
4. **The surprise.** As with most data, markets respond to the gap between the number and what was expected. The direction of the change alone tells you little.

Good news can also read as bad news. When inflation is the main worry, a stronger report may raise yields and weigh on stocks. When growth is the main worry, the same strength can be welcome.

## A real example: September 3, 2024

The [August 2024 ISM report](https://www.wardsauto.com/news/archive-auto-ism-pmi-manufacturing-production-interest-rates-august-2024/726263/) is a useful case because the headline actually rose. The PMI ticked up from 46.8 to 47.2. But new orders fell to 44.6, production dropped to 44.8 and employment came in at 46, all clearly in contraction, at a time when investors were nervous about growth.

On the day of the release, the [S&P 500 fell 2.1 percent, the Nasdaq lost 3.3 percent and the 10-year Treasury yield dropped 6.7 basis points to 3.844 percent](https://www.business-standard.com/amp/markets/capital-market-news/us-stocks-start-september-on-a-weak-note-124090400346_1.html). Falling stocks and falling yields together are what you would expect from a growth scare.

The ISM report wasn't the only cause, however. Nvidia dropped 9.5 percent that day and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index lost 7.8 percent, so a large part of the decline had a chip-sector flavor. Any single-day story deserves that kind of caution: one data point rarely explains an entire market move.

## Limits of the ISM Manufacturing PMI

The survey is fast and useful, but it comes with blind spots worth keeping in mind:

- **It measures breadth, not magnitude.** A high reading can coexist with modest actual growth.
- **Factories are a minority of the economy.** At under a tenth of GDP, manufacturing can diverge from everything else for long stretches.
- **It competes with other surveys.** S&P Global publishes its own U.S. manufacturing PMI, and the two can disagree. In February 2024, [ISM fell to 47.8 while S&P Global's measure rose to 52.2](https://abnamro.com/research/en/our-research/us-ism-and-manufacturing-pmi-divergence-what-does-it-mean), pointing in opposite directions on either side of 50.
- **Supplier deliveries can mislead.** As noted, a disruption can look like strength.
- **It is opinion data.** Hard data, such as the jobs report and factory output figures, may tell a different story later.

## How to read the report in five minutes

1. **Check the headline against the consensus.** Then note which side of 50 it fell on.
2. **Go to new orders and production.** These are the parts most closely tied to demand and output.
3. **Compare new orders with inventories.** Ask whether the gap is widening or narrowing.
4. **Read the Prices Index separately.** It isn't in the PMI, and it can matter more than the headline when inflation is the concern.
5. **Look at employment.** Then treat it as a rough preview, not a forecast, of factory jobs in the [monthly jobs report](/articles/how-the-jobs-report-moves-the-stock-market/).

For a faster-moving look at the labor market in the same week, pair it with [initial jobless claims](/articles/initial-jobless-claims-explained/).

## Where the ISM services PMI fits

Since services make up most of the economy, the companion report often gets just as much attention. The [ISM Services PMI](https://www.ismworld.org/globalassets/pub/research-and-surveys/rob/nmi/rain202609svcs.pdf) follows on the third business day of the month and averages four equal parts: business activity, new orders, employment and supplier deliveries. In September 2026 it read 54.9, its 27th straight month above 50. ISM says a reading above 48.1 over time generally indicates that GDP is expanding. Reading the two together tells you whether strength or weakness is confined to factories or spreading across the economy.
