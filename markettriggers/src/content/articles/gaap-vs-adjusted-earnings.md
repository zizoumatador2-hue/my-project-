**GAAP vs. adjusted earnings comes down to who sets the rules. GAAP earnings follow standard US accounting principles. Adjusted, or non-GAAP, earnings are management's version, with certain costs removed or added back. Adjusted EPS often drives the headline beat, but the GAAP figure and the reconciliation between the two show what actually happened.**

Open almost any large company's earnings release and you'll find two profit numbers. One sits in the financial statements at the back. The other, usually bigger, shows up in the first paragraph, the slide deck and the headlines. Knowing which is which, and why they differ, is one of the most useful habits an investor can build. It also explains a fair share of earnings-day surprises. This guide expands on the [earnings section of our guide to what moves the stock market](/#earnings-reports).

## Two numbers, one company

**GAAP** stands for generally accepted accounting principles, the rulebook US public companies use to prepare their audited annual and reviewed quarterly financial statements. GAAP net income, and GAAP earnings per share, are what a company's income statement says it earned.

**Adjusted earnings** go by many names: non-GAAP earnings, adjusted EPS, core earnings, operating earnings, pro forma earnings. The SEC's Regulation G defines a non-GAAP financial measure as a numerical measure of a company's historical or future financial performance, financial position or cash flows that excludes amounts included in the most directly comparable GAAP measure, or includes amounts that GAAP excludes. In plain terms: you start with the GAAP number and move some items in or out.

Here is how the two typically compare:

| | GAAP earnings | Adjusted (non-GAAP) earnings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who sets the rules | Accounting standard setters | Company management |
| Where it appears | Income statement, Form 10-Q and 10-K | Earnings release, slides, call, some filings |
| Consistent across companies? | Largely, within the same standards | No; each company picks its own adjustments |
| Includes stock-based compensation? | Yes | Often excluded |
| Includes restructuring and merger costs? | Yes | Often excluded |
| What the headline beat is often measured on | Sometimes | Very often |

## What usually gets adjusted

Each company writes its own list, but the same items appear again and again:

- **Stock-based compensation.** Shares and options paid to employees are a real cost under GAAP. Many companies remove them because they don't use cash at the time they're expensed.
- **Amortization of acquired intangibles.** After a purchase, the buyer records intangible assets like customer relationships and writes them down over time. Companies often exclude this amortization, arguing it reflects past deals rather than current operations.
- **Restructuring charges.** Severance, facility closures and similar costs tied to a reorganization.
- **Acquisition and integration costs.** Banker fees, legal bills and the expense of combining two businesses.
- **Impairments.** Write-downs when an asset, often goodwill from an old acquisition, is judged to be worth less than its carrying value.
- **Litigation settlements and gains or losses on investments.** Items management treats as outside the core business.
- **Tax effects of all of the above.** Removing a cost changes taxable income, so the adjustments ripple through to the tax line.

Some of these really are **one-time items**. A company that sells a division books a gain or loss once. Others recur every single quarter. Stock-based compensation is the clearest example: for many technology companies it shows up in every period, which is why some investors insist on looking at it as an ongoing cost no matter how it's labeled.

## Why companies report adjusted earnings at all

There's a reasonable case for the practice. Suppose a retailer closes 50 stores in one quarter and books a large charge. GAAP earnings for that quarter collapse. If you want to know how the remaining stores are doing, the adjusted number may tell you more. 
There's also a market reason. Many companies issue **guidance** on adjusted EPS, and analysts tend to build their consensus estimates on the same basis. When a headline says a company "beat by 6 cents," it's usually comparing adjusted EPS with an adjusted consensus. We cover how forecasts move stocks in [what earnings guidance is and why it matters](/articles/what-is-earnings-guidance/).

The case against is just as simple. Management decides what's excluded, and the exclusions rarely make results look worse. Two companies in the same industry can define "adjusted EPS" differently, so comparing them side by side may mean comparing two different yardsticks.

## The rules that keep non-GAAP honest

Non-GAAP numbers aren't a free-for-all. After the accounting scandals of the early 2000s, Section 401(b) of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act directed the SEC to write rules for them. The Commission adopted those rules in January 2003, and they took effect on March 28, 2003. Three pieces matter most to investors.

**Regulation G** applies whenever a public company discloses a non-GAAP measure, including in a press release or on a call. It requires a presentation of the most directly comparable GAAP measure and a reconciliation between the two (quantitative for historical figures). It also bars a company from presenting a non-GAAP measure that, together with its accompanying information, contains an untrue statement of a material fact or omits a material fact needed to keep it from being misleading.

**Item 10(e) of Regulation S-K** adds requirements for SEC filings. The GAAP measure must be presented "with equal or greater prominence," and the company must explain why management believes the non-GAAP measure is useful. The rule also prohibits adjusting a performance measure to eliminate or smooth items labeled non-recurring, infrequent or unusual when a similar charge or gain is reasonably likely to recur within two years, or when there was a similar one within the prior two years.

**SEC staff interpretations** fill in the details. The staff's Compliance and Disclosure Interpretations on non-GAAP measures, last updated in December 2022, give examples of adjustments that can be misleading even if no rule bans them outright:

| Staff interpretation | What it says, in short |
| --- | --- |
| Question 100.01 | Excluding normal, recurring cash operating expenses needed to run the business can be misleading. "Recurring" includes expenses that come back at irregular intervals. |
| Question 100.03 | Adjusting out non-recurring charges while leaving in non-recurring gains from the same period can be misleading. |
| Question 100.04 | "Individually tailored" measures that change GAAP's recognition rules, such as switching from accrual to cash accounting, may be misleading. |
| Question 100.06 | Some measures are so misleading that even extensive disclosure can't fix them. |
| Question 102.10 | Listing the non-GAAP number before the GAAP number, or calling a non-GAAP result a "record" without equal GAAP context, gives it more prominence than allowed. |
| Question 102.11 | Tax effects should be shown as their own adjustment rather than netting each item "net of tax." |

The SEC enforces these rules. In March 2023, it announced that DXC Technology agreed to pay an $8 million penalty, without admitting or denying the findings, after the agency found that from 2018 until early 2020 the company negligently classified tens of millions of dollars of expenses as transaction, separation and integration-related costs. Excluding those costs inflated non-GAAP net income, and the SEC's order found a material overstatement in three fiscal quarters. "Issuers that choose to report non-GAAP financial metrics must accurately describe those metrics in their public disclosures," Mark Cave, an associate director in the SEC's Division of Enforcement, said in the announcement.

## How to read the reconciliation in five minutes

The reconciliation table is usually near the end of the earnings release. It starts with GAAP net income (or GAAP EPS), lists each adjustment, and ends with the adjusted figure. Here's a quick way to work through it.

### 1. Measure the gap

Divide adjusted EPS by GAAP EPS. A gap of a few percent tells a different story than one where adjusted earnings are double the GAAP number. The bigger the gap, the more of the "profit" depends on management's choices.

### 2. Check whether the items repeat

Pull up the same table from the last four quarters or the annual report. If "restructuring" appears every quarter for three years, it's arguably part of the cost of running the business. That's the same logic the SEC applies with its two-year look-back.

### 3. Look for one-way adjustments

Did the company remove a loss on investments this quarter but keep a gain last quarter? Adjustments that only ever push earnings up deserve skepticism. The SEC staff flags exactly this pattern in Question 100.03.

### 4. Compare the definition over time

Companies sometimes add new exclusions. SEC staff guidance says a change in how a measure is calculated should be disclosed and explained, and prior periods may need to be recast. A new line in the reconciliation is worth a sentence of explanation from management. If you can't find one, ask why.

### 5. Read the cash flow statement

Neither earnings number is cash. Operating cash flow, and cash spent on capital projects, show whether reported profits are turning into money. ## A worked example (hypothetical)

The company and every figure below are invented for illustration.

Harbor Point Software reports its third quarter. The headline: adjusted EPS of $1.20, against an analyst consensus of $1.12. A clean beat.

The reconciliation at the back of the release tells a fuller story:

| Line item (per share) | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| GAAP EPS | $0.62 |
| Add: stock-based compensation | +$0.38 |
| Add: amortization of acquired intangibles | +$0.14 |
| Add: restructuring charges | +$0.12 |
| Less: tax effect of adjustments | -$0.06 |
| **Adjusted EPS** | **$1.20** |

Adjusted EPS is nearly double GAAP EPS. Stock-based compensation alone accounts for more than half of the difference, and a check of earlier releases (in our hypothetical) shows it in every quarter. Restructuring charges have appeared in five of the last six quarters.

None of this means the beat is fake. Adjusted EPS was computed the way analysts expected. But an investor who reads only the headline would miss that GAAP earnings tell a much more modest story, and that two of the "adjustments" look like ordinary costs of running this particular business. If the stock slips despite the beat, this is one of the places to look. We list other common culprits in [why stocks fall after beating earnings](/articles/why-do-stocks-fall-after-earnings/).

## How the market tends to react

Markets usually trade first on the adjusted beat or miss, because that's what the consensus measures. The GAAP picture tends to matter more over time and in specific situations:

- **When the gap widens suddenly.** A jump in adjustments can make investors question the quality of a beat.
- **When a new adjustment appears.** Changing the definition in a quarter that would otherwise have missed can draw attention.
- **When valuations get stretched.** A stock priced at a high multiple of adjusted earnings looks much more expensive on a GAAP basis, and that difference can matter when sentiment turns.
- **When GAAP results show a loss.** A small adjusted beat sitting on top of a GAAP loss can leave investors focused on the loss.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating adjusted EPS as "real" earnings and GAAP as noise.** Both are information. GAAP is the standardized baseline; adjusted shows management's view.
- **Assuming non-cash means not a cost.** Stock-based compensation dilutes existing shareholders even though no cash leaves the company.
- **Comparing adjusted EPS across companies without checking definitions.** Two "adjusted EPS" figures may exclude different things.
- **Ignoring the tax line.** Adjustments change taxes too, so look at the separate tax adjustment rather than adding up pre-tax items.
- **Reading one quarter in isolation.** The pattern across several quarters says far more than any single reconciliation.
