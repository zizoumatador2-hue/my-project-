**Stocks and bonds fall together when inflation, not weak growth, is the market's main fear. Rising inflation pushes interest rates up, which cuts bond prices and also shrinks what investors will pay for future corporate profits. That is why stocks and bonds fall together in some years, as in 2022, and move in opposite directions in others.**

Most investors learn a simple rule early on: when stocks have a bad day, bonds usually have a good one. For about two decades that rule held up well enough to build entire retirement strategies around it. Then 2022 arrived, and both sides of the portfolio lost money in the same year. It happened again on a smaller scale in March 2026, and the week of November 10, 2026, brought another taste. The relationship between the two asset classes isn't fixed. It depends on what kind of shock the economy is dealing with, and you can learn to tell which one is in charge. For the bigger picture on how rates, the dollar and stocks interact, see the [stocks, bonds and the dollar section of our guide to what moves the stock market](/#stocks-bonds-and-the-dollar-how-they-move-together).

## The usual story: bonds as a diversifier

Bonds earn their place in a portfolio for two reasons. They pay steady income, and they often hold up when stocks don't. The SEC's beginner guide to asset allocation puts it plainly: "Historically, the returns of the three major asset categories have not moved up and down at the same time." The three it means are stocks, bonds and cash.

The logic behind that pattern runs through the Federal Reserve. When the economy weakens, profits are expected to fall and stocks drop. At the same time, investors expect the Fed to cut interest rates to support growth. Expected rate cuts push Treasury yields down, and since bond prices move opposite to yields, existing bonds gain value. The bond side of the portfolio rises while the stock side falls. That offset is what people mean when they call bonds a diversifier.

The classic 60/40 portfolio, roughly 60 percent stocks and 40 percent bonds, is built on this idea. The stock portion supplies growth over time. The bond portion is supposed to soften the blow in a downturn and give you something to sell, or rebalance from, when stocks are cheap.

## Why stocks and bonds fall together: the inflation channel

The offset only works if a bad economy brings lower rates. Inflation breaks that link.

When prices are rising faster than the Fed wants, bad news for stocks doesn't automatically mean the Fed will come to the rescue. If anything, a jump in inflation tells markets the Fed may need to raise rates or keep them high longer. That hits both asset classes at once:

- **Bonds lose value directly.** A bond's coupon is fixed. When market rates rise, older bonds paying less become worth less. FINRA's rule of thumb is that a bond's price moves roughly its duration number, in percent, for each 1 percentage point change in rates. A bond with a duration of 7 would lose about 7 percent if rates rose a full point.
- **Stocks get hit through valuation.** A stock is worth the present value of its future profits. Higher rates mean those profits are discounted more heavily, so the same earnings are worth less today. We walk through that math in [how interest rates affect stock valuations](/articles/how-interest-rates-affect-stock-valuations/).
- **Growth expectations can fall too.** Tighter policy slows borrowing, hiring and spending, so investors may also trim their profit forecasts.

A 2026 analysis by Federal Reserve Board economists Canlin Li and Emre Yoldas sums up the split. Bonds tend to hedge stocks well when inflation is near the Fed's 2 percent target and expectations are anchored, because the central bank has room to cut after a shock. They hedge poorly when inflation is above target and supply shocks, such as an oil price spike, force the Fed to stay tight. In the authors' words, "inflationary dynamics matter greatly in determining whether bonds provide portfolio insurance."

A separate 2026 letter from the San Francisco Fed frames it as demand versus supply. When the main risk is a drop in demand, stocks and yields tend to fall together, so bonds cushion stock losses. When the main risk is a supply shock that raises prices while slowing growth, that cushion disappears.

| Type of shock | Typical example | Stocks | Treasury yields | Bond prices | Do bonds cushion stocks? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Demand shock | Recession fears, weak jobs data, financial panic | Down | Down | Up | Usually yes |
| Supply or inflation shock | Energy price spike, hot CPI report | Down | Up | Down | Usually no |
| Strong growth with tame inflation | Solid earnings, cooling prices | Up | Stable or up | Mixed | Not needed |

*Based on the framework in the Federal Reserve Board's October 2026 FEDS Note and the San Francisco Fed's Economic Letter 2026-21. These are tendencies, not rules.*

## What the stock-bond correlation history shows

Economists measure this relationship with a correlation, a number between minus 1 and plus 1. One common version, used by the Fed Board researchers, compares daily stock returns with daily changes in the 10-year Treasury yield over a rolling one-year window. The sign convention trips people up, so here it is in plain terms: when that number is positive, stocks and yields rise and fall together, which means stock prices and bond prices move in *opposite* directions. Positive is the good, diversifying state.

Here is what their data shows for the United States:

| Period | Average correlation, U.S. stock returns vs. 10-year yield changes | What it meant for investors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before 2000 | -0.26 | Bonds often fell when stocks fell |
| 2000 to 2021 | 0.40 | Bonds usually offset stock losses |
| After 2021 | -0.09 | The cushion largely disappeared |

*Source: Li and Yoldas, "Government Bonds' Safe-Haven Property and Inflationary Pressures," FEDS Notes, Federal Reserve Board, October 16, 2026. Sample runs from January 1990 through July 2026.*

That middle period matters because it shaped how a whole generation of investors thinks. If you started investing anytime between 2000 and 2021, bonds probably felt like reliable insurance. The longer record says that was one regime, not a law of nature. The San Francisco Fed's measure, built from monthly S&P 500 and 10-year yield data over five-year windows, tells the same story: positive from the early 2000s until the early 2020s, negative since. Its authors suggest that "a supply-driven economy may be the new normal for some time."

The Fed Board note also found that markets became far more sensitive to inflation news after 2021. In its event study, the U.S. 10-year yield reacted about three times as strongly to inflation surprises as it did in 2000 to 2019, and U.S. stocks fell significantly on those surprises. That is the falling-together pattern in miniature: a hot inflation number pushes yields up and stocks down on the same day. It's one reason [CPI release days](/articles/what-is-core-cpi/) have become such big events.

## 2022: the year both sides lost

The clearest modern example is 2022. Inflation had been building since 2021, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that consumer prices rose 9.1 percent over the 12 months ended June 2022, the largest such increase in 40 years. The Fed responded with a string of rate increases. It raised the federal funds target range from near zero to 1/4 to 1/2 percent in March 2022 and kept going, reaching 4-1/4 to 4-1/2 percent by its December 14 meeting.

Here is what that did to the two benchmarks:

| Measure | December 31, 2021 | December 30, 2022 | Change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| S&P 500 index (price) | 4,766.18 | 3,839.50 | Down 19.4% |
| 10-year Treasury yield | 1.52% | 3.88% | Up 2.36 percentage points |

*Source: FRED, Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, series [SP500](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/sp500) and [DGS10](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DGS10). The index figure excludes dividends.*

A rise of more than two percentage points in long-term yields is a severe hit for bondholders. Using FINRA's rule of thumb, a bond fund with a duration of 6 would have lost roughly 14 percent in price from a move that size, before counting interest income. That is a hypothetical illustration, not the return of any actual fund. Meanwhile, the stock market's close on October 12, 2022, at 3,577.03, was about 25 percent below its level at the end of 2021, which put it in bear market territory. (Our [correction vs. bear market explainer](/articles/stock-market-correction-vs-bear-market/) covers how those thresholds work.)

For a 60/40 investor, there was nowhere to hide. The bonds that were supposed to rise when stocks fell were falling for the same reason stocks were: inflation and the rate hikes meant to fight it.

## March 2026: a smaller replay

The pattern showed up again this year. The Fed Board note describes March 2026 this way: stock indexes fell sharply as energy prices rose during the Middle East conflict, and long-term government bond prices also fell. Here is the move in daily data:

| Date (2026) | S&P 500 close | 10-year Treasury yield |
| --- | --- | --- |
| February 27 | 6,878.88 | 3.97% |
| March 13 | 6,632.19 | 4.28% |
| March 27 | 6,368.85 | 4.44% |

*Source: FRED series [SP500](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/sp500) and [DGS10](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DGS10).*

Over those four weeks, the S&P 500 fell about 7.4 percent while the 10-year yield rose 47 basis points (a basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point). In a demand scare you'd expect yields to drop as investors bought Treasuries for safety. Instead, an oil-driven inflation worry pushed them higher. That is a textbook supply shock, and it's exactly the case where bonds don't cushion stocks.

The same pattern reappeared in mid-November. From November 10 to November 17, 2026, the S&P 500 slipped from 7,520.93 to 7,340.53, a drop of about 2.4 percent, while the 10-year yield climbed from 4.98 percent to 5.10 percent, according to FRED. Our explainer on [the 10-year Treasury yield](/articles/what-is-the-10-year-treasury-yield/) breaks down what drove that run-up.

## When bonds still do their job

None of this means bonds have stopped being useful. Treasuries can still rally hard when the threat is a sudden collapse in demand or a financial panic, because those are moments when investors expect the Fed to cut. The Fed Board note points out that during the 2008 global financial crisis and afterward, anchored inflation expectations and quantitative easing helped bonds rally when stocks fell.

The authors also argue the post-2021 shift reflects a change in the economic environment, "not necessarily a fundamental change in bond characteristics." They expect bonds' hedging ability to improve if inflation eases and adverse supply shocks become less frequent.

There is also a plainer benefit. Bonds pay interest, and with the 10-year yield above 5 percent in November 2026, new buyers are locking in more income than they could a few years ago. Higher starting yields give a bond some room to absorb price declines before its total return turns negative. Volatility still matters, and the [VIX](/articles/what-is-the-vix/) is one way to see how nervous stock investors are at any given moment.

## What this means for a 60/40 portfolio

This is education, not a recommendation, but a few practical points follow from the research:

1. **Know which regime you're in.** If inflation is running well above 2 percent and the Fed is leaning hawkish, assume stocks and bonds may move together for a while. If inflation is tame and the risk is a slowdown, bonds are more likely to act as a cushion.
2. **Duration is a choice.** Long-term bonds swing the most when rates move. Shorter-term bonds and Treasury bills lose less when yields rise, though they also gain less when yields fall.
3. **Diversification has limits.** The SEC's guide says the right mix "may" limit losses and reduce swings. It never promises that every asset will zig when another zags.
4. **Rebalancing still works the same way.** Investor.gov describes rebalancing as "bringing your portfolio back to your original asset allocation mix." When both sides fall, the mix may not drift much, but it can still shift.
5. **Watch inflation data, not just stock prices.** The releases most likely to move both markets at once are inflation reports and Fed decisions. Our guide to [how inflation affects stocks](/articles/how-does-inflation-affect-stocks/) covers the stock side of that link.
