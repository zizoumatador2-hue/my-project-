**Are stock buybacks good? They can be, but it depends on the price and the source of the cash. A share repurchase returns money to investors and shrinks the share count, which lifts earnings per share. Buybacks create value when a company buys at a sensible price with true excess cash. They destroy it when management overpays or borrows to do it.**

Buybacks are now one of the biggest ways American companies hand money back to shareholders, and one of the most argued about. Supporters see a tax-efficient, flexible alternative to dividends. Critics see a tool for flattering earnings per share and executive pay. Both camps are sometimes right, which is why it pays to judge each buyback on its own terms. This guide builds on the [earnings section of our guide to what moves the stock market](/#earnings-reports).

## What a share repurchase actually is

A share repurchase is simple: a company uses its own cash to buy its own stock, usually on the open market through a broker, and either cancels those shares or holds them as treasury stock. Either way, there are fewer shares in public hands, so each remaining share represents a slightly larger piece of the business.

Most buybacks start with a board authorization, often announced with a dollar figure ("up to $10 billion"). That number is a ceiling, not a commitment. A company can take years to use it, or never finish. What it actually spent shows up later in its quarterly and annual filings.

The scale is large. In a December 2025 release, S&P Dow Jones Indices reported that S&P 500 companies spent $249.0 billion on buybacks in the third quarter of 2025, and a record $1.020 trillion over the 12 months ending in September 2025. Dividends over the same 12 months were $664.9 billion. The spending is also concentrated: the top 20 companies accounted for 49.5% of third-quarter buybacks.

## How buybacks lift EPS (and why that's not the whole story)

Earnings per share is net income divided by shares outstanding. Cut the denominator and EPS rises even if profit doesn't move. That is the buyback EPS effect, and it is real arithmetic, not an accounting trick. It also has limits that are easy to miss.

**The cash isn't free.** Money spent on stock could have sat in Treasury bills earning interest or paid down debt. Lose that income, and net income falls a little, which eats into the EPS gain.

**Employees keep getting shares.** Many companies issue stock to employees every year through grants and options. If a company buys back 20 million shares while issuing 15 million, the share count barely moves. Some buybacks are mostly a way to keep dilution from building up.

**The EPS lift doesn't change the business.** Revenue, margins and cash flow are exactly what they were. A company growing EPS mainly through a shrinking share count is a different story from one growing its profits. S&P Dow Jones Indices tracks this directly: in the third quarter of 2025, 17.1% of S&P 500 companies had reduced the share count used for EPS by at least 4% from a year earlier. For those companies, a meaningful slice of EPS growth came from fewer shares rather than higher profit.

This is why it helps to check share count alongside EPS when you [read an earnings report](/articles/how-to-read-an-earnings-report/). If EPS rose 10% but net income rose 4%, the buyback did most of the work.

## A worked example (hypothetical)

The company and figures below are invented for illustration, and the math is simplified.

Ridgeline Tools earns $1 billion in net income (after stock-based compensation expense) and has 500 million shares outstanding, so EPS is $2.00. The stock trades at $40, or 20 times earnings. The board spends $1 billion, roughly a year of profit, buying back 25 million shares. Assume that cash had been earning 3% after tax, or $30 million a year.

| Scenario | Net income | Shares outstanding | EPS | Change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Before the buyback | $1,000M | 500M | $2.00 | n/a |
| Buyback, no new shares issued | $970M | 475M | $2.04 | +2.1% |
| Buyback, plus 10M shares issued to employees and 1% excise tax on the net | $964M | 485M | $1.99 | -0.6% |

In the clean version, EPS rises about 2%, because the earnings yield on the stock (5% at 20 times earnings) beats the 3% the cash was earning. In the more realistic version, Ridgeline also issues 10 million shares to employees worth $400 million. The net repurchase is $600 million, the 1% excise tax on that net is $6 million, and the share count falls only 15 million. EPS actually slips.

Two lessons come out of this. First, the price matters: if the stock had traded at 40 times earnings, the earnings yield would have been 2.5%, below what the cash was earning, and even the clean buyback would have reduced EPS. Second, the headline dollar figure says little until you net out shares issued.

## When buybacks create value, and when they don't

The simplest way to think about it: a buyback is an investment the company makes in itself. Like any investment, the return depends on what you pay. Buying back stock below what the business is worth transfers value to shareholders who stay. Buying it back above that value transfers value to the shareholders who sell.

| Signs a buyback is more likely to help | Signs to be skeptical |
| --- | --- |
| Funded from free cash flow left after investment needs | Funded mainly with new debt |
| Shares outstanding actually decline over several years | Share count flat or rising despite big buybacks |
| Valuation looks reasonable versus history and peers | Heavy buying after a big run-up, little after selloffs |
| Steady, consistent program | Spending stops abruptly when cash gets tight |
| Management explains why buybacks beat other uses of cash | Pay targets tied to EPS with no explanation of capital choices |

Timing is a common weakness. Many companies buy most when profits and cash are flush, which is often when their stock is expensive, and pull back in downturns when shares are cheap. S&P's data show the swing: buybacks hit a record $293.5 billion in the first quarter of 2025, then fell to $234.6 billion in the second quarter, which the release tied to uncertainty.

Leverage is the other concern. Borrowing to buy back stock can make sense when debt is cheap and the business is stable, but it leaves less room for error in a recession. Higher interest rates raise the bar, which connects buybacks to the broader story of [how interest rates affect stock valuations](/articles/how-interest-rates-affect-stock-valuations/).

## Buybacks vs. dividends

Both send cash to owners, but they work differently.

| | Dividends | Buybacks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who gets cash | Every shareholder, pro rata | Only shareholders who sell |
| Flexibility for the company | Low; cuts are seen as a bad sign | High; can be slowed or paused quietly |
| Tax timing for a taxable holder | Taxed when paid | Generally taxed only when you sell |
| Effect on share count | None | Reduces it, if not offset by issuance |
| Depends on stock price? | No | Yes; value depends on price paid |
| Corporate-level tax | None specific | 1% excise tax on net repurchases since 2023 |

The flexibility point cuts both ways. Companies like buybacks because they can turn them off without the market treating it as a crisis. For investors, that also means buyback "income" is far less dependable than a dividend that management has committed to defending. The tax-timing point applies only to taxable accounts and depends on your own situation; this isn't tax advice.

## The rules: Rule 10b-18, disclosure and the 1% tax

**Rule 10b-18.** Adopted by the SEC in 1982, this rule is a voluntary safe harbor. If a company's open-market repurchases meet four conditions on a given day, it is protected from manipulation claims for those purchases. The conditions cover manner (one broker or dealer per day), timing (no opening trade of the day, and no buying in the last 10 minutes before the close for larger, actively traded companies, or the last 30 minutes for others), price (no higher than the highest independent bid or last independent trade, whichever is higher) and volume (generally up to 25% of average daily trading volume). Missing any one condition removes all of that day's purchases from the safe harbor. The SEC notes the rule gives no protection to a company that buys while holding material, non-public information.

**Disclosure.** Under amendments the SEC adopted in November 2003, companies must disclose all repurchases, whether or not they used the safe harbor, in their 10-Q and 10-K filings. The table breaks purchases out by month and shows shares bought, the average price paid, and how much remains under announced programs. The SEC adopted a stricter rule in May 2023 that would have required more detail, but the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals vacated it on December 19, 2023. According to the SEC, the disclosure requirements reverted to those in effect before that rule.

**The excise tax.** The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 created a 1% excise tax on repurchases made after December 31, 2022. It applies to domestic corporations whose stock trades on an established securities market. The base is the fair market value of stock repurchased during the year, reduced by the fair market value of stock issued, including stock issued to employees. Companies that repurchase no more than $1 million in a year are exempt. Treasury and the IRS published final regulations on the calculation on November 24, 2025, and the tax is reported on Form 720.

How much does it matter? S&P Dow Jones Indices estimated the tax reduced S&P 500 operating earnings by 0.36% in the third quarter of 2025. Howard Silverblatt of S&P Dow Jones Indices called it "a manageable expense" that "has not impacted overall buybacks."

## How buyback news moves stocks

Buyback announcements often arrive with earnings, so they tend to be read alongside results and [earnings guidance](/articles/what-is-earnings-guidance/). A few patterns are worth knowing:

- **A new or bigger authorization** signals that management sees excess cash and, often, believes the stock is reasonably priced. Markets may treat it as a modest positive, but the authorization is not a promise to spend.
- **A paused or shrunken program** can be read as caution about cash needs, especially when paired with weaker guidance.
- **Large accelerated repurchases**, where a company buys a big block quickly through a bank, can draw attention because they show commitment, though the long-term value still depends on price.
- **Adjusted EPS beats built on a shrinking share count** may get a cooler reception once investors see net income growth was modest. If the company also reports adjusted figures, our [GAAP vs. adjusted earnings guide](/articles/gaap-vs-adjusted-earnings/) explains how to separate the pieces.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating an authorization as money spent.** Check the filings for what was actually repurchased.
- **Looking at gross buybacks only.** Net out shares issued to employees to see whether the share count is really falling.
- **Crediting EPS growth to the business.** Compare net income growth with EPS growth.
- **Ignoring price.** A buyback at a stretched valuation can be a poor use of cash even at a great company.
- **Ignoring the balance sheet.** Debt-funded buybacks look fine in good years and painful in bad ones.
