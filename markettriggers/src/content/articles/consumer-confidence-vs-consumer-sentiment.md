**Consumer confidence vs consumer sentiment comes down to two different surveys. The Conference Board's Consumer Confidence Index leans on how people see jobs and business conditions. The University of Michigan's Index of Consumer Sentiment leans on personal finances, buying conditions and inflation expectations. They often move together, but not always, and the gaps tell you something.**

Both surveys ask ordinary Americans how they feel about the economy, and both get headlines the morning they come out. That's where the similarity ends. The questions are different, the samples are different, the scales are different, and in late 2026 the two indexes are sitting at levels that look like they describe two separate countries. For how mood surveys fit next to hard spending data, see the [consumer spending and sentiment section of our guide to what moves the stock market](/#consumer-spending-and-sentiment).

## Consumer confidence vs consumer sentiment at a glance

Start with the basics. Here is how the two surveys line up, based on the publishers' own descriptions and a New York Fed comparison.

| | Conference Board Consumer Confidence Index | University of Michigan Index of Consumer Sentiment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Publisher | The Conference Board, a private research group | Surveys of Consumers, University of Michigan |
| Base period | 1985 = 100 | Scaled to a 1966 base period |
| Sub-indexes | Present Situation, Expectations | Current Economic Conditions, Consumer Expectations |
| What it emphasizes | Business conditions and job availability | Personal finances, business outlook, big-ticket buying |
| Expectations horizon | About six months ahead | One year and five years ahead |
| How it's collected | Online sample conducted by Toluna | Web survey using address-based sampling since 2024 |
| Release | Last Tuesday of the month, 10 a.m. ET | Preliminary early in the month, final later, 10 a.m. ET |

Sources: [The Conference Board](https://www.conference-board.org/topics/consumer-confidence/), [University of Michigan index calculations](https://data.sca.isr.umich.edu/fetchdoc.php?docid=75432) and [technical report](https://data.sca.isr.umich.edu/fetchdoc.php?docid=75437), [New York Fed](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/epr/98v04n2/9806bram.pdf).

## How the Conference Board index works

The Conference Board splits its index into two parts. The Present Situation Index is "based on consumers' assessment of current business and labor market conditions," according to the [Conference Board](https://www.conference-board.org/topics/consumer-confidence/). In plain terms, respondents say whether business conditions are good or bad and whether jobs are plentiful or hard to get. The Expectations Index covers consumers' "short-term outlook for income, business, and labor market conditions," with the survey section titled "Expectations Six Months Hence."

That job market tilt is the index's defining feature. One of the most quoted numbers in each release is the labor market differential: the share saying jobs are plentiful minus the share saying jobs are hard to get. Traders watch it as a quick read on the employment picture a couple of weeks before the official [jobs report](/articles/how-the-jobs-report-moves-the-stock-market/).

The survey also asks about prices, interest rates, stock prices and the chance of a recession. The Conference Board reports those answers, but they aren't part of the index calculation itself.

## How the Michigan sentiment index works

Michigan's index is built from five questions, according to the university's [index calculation notes](https://data.sca.isr.umich.edu/fetchdoc.php?docid=75432). Paraphrased, they ask:

1. Are you better or worse off financially than a year ago?
2. Will you be better or worse off a year from now?
3. Will the country have good or bad times financially over the next 12 months?
4. Over the next five years, will there be continuous good times or periods of widespread unemployment or depression?
5. Is now a good or bad time to buy major household items?

For each question, the share of unfavorable answers is subtracted from the share of favorable ones and 100 is added. The five scores are summed, divided by the 1966 base-period total and adjusted by a small constant.

Notice what's missing: there is no direct question about whether jobs are easy to find. Michigan's lens is the household budget. When prices for groceries, rent or gasoline climb, it tends to show up here quickly, because questions about being "better off" pick up the squeeze.

Michigan also publishes the survey's most closely followed numbers on inflation expectations: what consumers expect prices to do over the next year and over the next five to ten years. Economists and Fed officials pay attention to these because expectations can feed into actual wage and price decisions.

## The latest readings: two very different levels

Here's where things stood as of this writing.

| --- | --- | --- |
| Conference Board Consumer Confidence Index (October 2026) | 91.7 | 84.2 (September, revised) |
| Present Situation Index | 120.4 | up 8.6 points |
| Expectations Index | 72.6 | up 6.9 points |
| Michigan Index of Consumer Sentiment (preliminary November 2026) | 51.0 | 48.0 (October) |
| Current Economic Conditions | 51.0 | 46.7 |
| Index of Consumer Expectations | 50.9 | 48.8 |

*Sources: [Conference Board release, October 27, 2026](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/us-consumer-confidence-increased-in-october-302920731.html); [University of Michigan Surveys of Consumers, preliminary November 2026](https://www.sca.isr.umich.edu/index.html).*

The Conference Board said its October gain partly reversed three straight monthly declines in expectations. The labor market differential rose 2.4 percentage points to +5.3, and consumers' average 12-month inflation expectation eased to 5.8 percent. The cutoff for the preliminary results was October 21.

Michigan's preliminary November reading rose about 6 percent from October and matched its level of a year earlier. Year-ahead inflation expectations fell to 4.3 percent from 4.7 percent, and long-run expectations slipped to 3.4 percent from 3.5 percent. Survey director Joanne Hsu noted that "despite this month's improvement, sentiment is down 10% from February," and that AI worries "were dwarfed by cost of living concerns."

So both surveys improved, and both still point to strain from prices. The direction matched. The levels didn't.

## Why the numbers look so far apart

A reading of 91.7 next to a reading of 51.0 can look alarming, as if one survey sees a mild slowdown and the other sees a depression. Most of that gap is arithmetic.

**Different base years.** The Conference Board sets 1985 at 100. Michigan scales to 1966. The New York Fed's [comparison of the two indexes](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/epr/98v04n2/9806bram.pdf) notes that because of the different base periods, their levels "are not directly comparable." It also found that the Conference Board index swings more widely, estimating that a one-point move in Michigan's index is roughly comparable to a two-point move in the Conference Board's.

**Different questions.** Jobs drive the Conference Board index. Personal finances and buying conditions drive Michigan's. When the job market is holding up but prices are biting, you'd expect the Conference Board to look better, and that pattern has shown up repeatedly in recent years. A June 2026 [Chicago Fed Letter](https://www.chicagofed.org/publications/chicago-fed-letter/2026/521) observed that "the Conference Board index was much less pessimistic throughout the past ten or so years than the Michigan index."

**Different collection methods.** Michigan moved from telephone interviews to a web survey with address-based sampling in 2024, according to its [technical documentation](https://data.sca.isr.umich.edu/fetchdoc.php?docid=75437). The Chicago Fed authors found that shift lowered Michigan's readings, while the Conference Board's earlier move online had a much smaller effect. They went further and estimated recent Michigan readings are understated by 25 to 30 index points relative to their composite measure. That's one research team's estimate, not an official correction, but it's a good reason not to compare today's Michigan levels with readings from a decade ago without care.

The practical rule: compare each index to its own history and watch the direction of change. Comparing one index's level to the other's tells you almost nothing.

## Inflation expectations: the part bond traders watch

For markets, the most consequential numbers in these surveys are often the inflation expectations, not the headline mood.

Michigan's year-ahead and five-to-ten-year expectations matter because the Federal Reserve wants long-run expectations to stay anchored. If households start to believe high inflation is permanent, they may demand bigger raises and accept bigger price increases, which can make inflation harder to bring down. A jump in long-run expectations can push Treasury yields higher as traders bet the Fed will hold rates up longer. A drop can do the reverse. Our explainer on [how inflation affects stocks](/articles/how-does-inflation-affect-stocks/) walks through why that rate channel matters for valuations.

The Conference Board also reports 12-month inflation expectations. In October it was 5.8 percent, while Michigan's preliminary November year-ahead figure was 4.3 percent. Don't read that gap as a contradiction. The surveys ask about prices in different ways, at different times, of different samples. Each number is most useful compared with its own past readings.

Keep in mind that consumer expectations are only one input. Officials also look at market-based measures and at actual price data such as the [CPI and PCE](/articles/cpi-vs-pce/).

## Do these surveys actually predict spending?

This is the uncomfortable question for anyone who trades on survey headlines. The answer from Fed researchers: only somewhat, and less than it used to.

- The 1998 [New York Fed study](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/epr/98v04n2/9806bram.pdf) by Jason Bram and Sydney Ludvigson found the Conference Board index "generally serves as a better predictor of spending than the Michigan index," with the job availability questions doing much of the work. But it also found that predictive power diminished in the early 1990s, and cautioned that the results "do not prove that consumer attitudes cause changes in consumer spending."
- A May 2025 [Kansas City Fed bulletin](https://www.kansascityfed.org/research/economic-bulletin/forecasting-with-feelings-the-modest-link-between-consumer-sentiment-and-spending/) concluded that "consumer sentiment data have not substantially improved forecasts of consumer spending growth" over the past 30 years.
- The Chicago Fed's 2026 letter found the link between sentiment and spending weakened, especially since 2020, with the decline "most pronounced for the Michigan index."

There's a familiar pattern behind these findings. People can tell a pollster they feel terrible about the economy and still book the vacation, especially if they have a job and their pay is rising. What people do with their money usually beats what they say about it. That's why the [retail sales report](/articles/retail-sales-report-explained/), which measures actual spending, typically carries more weight with economists than either mood survey.

## How the surveys can move markets

On release mornings, the market reaction tends to be smaller than for the jobs report or CPI. The surveys come out at 10 a.m. Eastern, after the opening bell, and they're "soft" data: opinions rather than transactions. Still, a few situations make them matter.

**A big surprise in inflation expectations.** A sharp jump in Michigan's long-run expectations can move Treasury yields and rate-sensitive stocks, because it bears directly on the Fed.

**A turn in the job market questions.** If the share saying jobs are hard to get starts climbing, traders may read it as an early warning before layoffs show up in weekly [jobless claims](/articles/initial-jobless-claims-explained/).

**Confirmation of a story already in play.** When markets are already worried about a slowdown, a weak survey can reinforce the narrative. When the hard data is strong, the same weak survey is more likely to be shrugged off.

| If the surveys show... | Areas often in focus | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rising long-run inflation expectations | Treasuries, rate-sensitive stocks | Raises the odds the Fed keeps rates elevated |
| Falling expectations for jobs and income | Consumer discretionary, retailers | Hints at softer spending ahead |
| Better buying conditions for big-ticket items | Autos, home furnishings, appliances | Household willingness to make large purchases |
| Weak mood but strong spending data | Little reaction | Markets tend to trust spending over survey answers |

These are tendencies, not rules. What was expected, and what else came out that week, usually matters more than the survey itself.

## A practical example: reading both in the same month

Take October and early November 2026. The Conference Board index jumped 7.5 points, and Michigan's preliminary November reading rose 3 points. A quick reader might call that a huge improvement in one survey and a modest one in the other. Using the New York Fed's rough rule that one Michigan point is comparable to about two Conference Board points, the two gains look closer than the raw numbers suggest.

Then look under the hood. Both surveys showed inflation expectations easing. The Conference Board's labor differential improved. Michigan's director still pointed to cost of living as the dominant concern. Put together, the message is fairly consistent: consumers felt a bit better as some price pressure eased, but they weren't celebrating. That's a more useful conclusion than "confidence up, sentiment still at depressed levels."
