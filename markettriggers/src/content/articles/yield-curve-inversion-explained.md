**Yield curve inversion explained: an inverted yield curve happens when short-term Treasury yields rise above long-term yields, so lenders earn more for tying up money for months than for a decade. It has preceded every U.S. recession since the 1950s, which is why investors watch it, but the lag is long and false alarms happen.**

Most market signals are noisy. The yield curve is one of the few with a track record economists at several Federal Reserve banks have studied for decades, and it still gets misread all the time. Some headlines treat an inversion as a countdown clock for a crash. Others declare it broken the moment a recession fails to show up on schedule. The truth sits in between. For the bigger picture of how Treasury yields fit with the Fed, inflation and stock prices, see the [Treasury yields section of our guide to what moves the stock market](/#treasury-yields-and-the-yield-curve).

## What the yield curve is

The yield curve is a line connecting the yields on U.S. Treasury securities from the shortest maturities, such as 1-month and 3-month bills, out to the 10-year note and the 30-year bond. Because every point on the line carries the same credit risk (the U.S. government), the shape of the curve tells you mostly about two things: where investors expect short-term interest rates to go, and how much extra they demand for locking up money for a long time.

In normal times the curve slopes upward. If you lend for ten years, you are exposed to more inflation risk and more price swings than if you lend for three months, so you usually get paid more. When the curve flattens, the gap shrinks. When it inverts, the gap turns negative.

Rather than stare at the whole curve, most people track one spread between two points on it:

| Spread | How it's calculated | Who tends to use it | November 12, 2026 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2s10s | 10-year yield minus 2-year yield | Traders, financial media | +0.24 percentage point |
| 10-year minus 3-month | 10-year yield minus 3-month bill yield | New York Fed recession model, much academic research | +0.78 percentage point |
| 10-year minus 1-year | 10-year yield minus 1-year yield | San Francisco Fed research | Not shown |

*Sources: FRED, Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, series [DGS10](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DGS10), [DGS2](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DGS2) and [T10Y3M](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/T10Y3M), based on the Federal Reserve's H.15 data.*

So as of mid-November 2026, the curve is not inverted on either popular measure. Both spreads are positive, though the 2s10s gap is thin.

## Why short rates can climb above long rates

Short-term Treasury yields track the Federal Reserve's policy rate closely. When the Fed raises rates to fight inflation, 3-month bill yields follow almost immediately, and the 2-year yield moves with what traders expect the Fed to do over the next two years.

The 10-year yield answers a different question. It reflects the average short-term rate investors expect over a full decade, plus a cushion known as the term premium. If investors believe the Fed's current policy is tight enough to slow the economy, they also expect the Fed to cut rates eventually. Those expected future cuts pull the 10-year yield down, even as short yields stay high.

That is the core logic, and it was laid out in a 1996 New York Fed paper by Arturo Estrella and Frederic Mishkin. They wrote that monetary policy shapes the spread: raising short-term rates tends to flatten the curve and slow near-term growth. A 2018 Fed Board note by Peter Johansson and Andrew Meldrum put it in similar terms. When short rates are high relative to where they are expected to settle in the long run, policy tends to be restrictive, and restrictive policy is often followed by slower growth.

In plain English, an inverted curve is the bond market saying: rates are high now, and we think they will have to come down later because the economy will weaken.

## The track record of the inverted yield curve and recessions

This is where the yield curve earns its reputation. A few findings from Federal Reserve research:

- **San Francisco Fed (2018).** Michael Bauer and Thomas Mertens studied the 10-year minus 1-year spread from 1955 to early 2018. Every recession in that window was preceded by an inversion. A simple rule of thumb correctly signaled all nine recessions, with one false positive in the mid-1960s, when an inversion was followed by a slowdown but no official recession.
- **St. Louis Fed (2023).** Economist Christopher Neely wrote that an inverted curve has preceded each recession since the 1950s, while noting the nominal spread produced high recession probabilities in the late 1960s and late 1990s when none followed.
- **Fed Board (2018).** Johansson and Meldrum found the 10-year minus 3-month spread narrowed before each of the six most recent recessions and was inverted before five of them.

### How the New York Fed turns the spread into a probability

The New York Fed publishes a model that converts the 10-year minus 3-month spread into a probability of recession twelve months ahead. It uses monthly averages of daily rates, so a single wild trading session doesn't swing it much. The bank's own FAQ says a signal that lasts one day may be dismissed, while one that persists for a month or more deserves attention.

Estrella and Mishkin's original paper gave a feel for how the numbers map to risk in their four-quarter model:

| Average 10-year minus 3-month spread | Implied probability of recession four quarters later |
| --- | --- |
| +0.76 percentage point | 10% |
| -0.82 percentage point | 50% |
| -2.40 percentage points | 90% |

*Source: [Estrella and Mishkin, "The Yield Curve as a Predictor of U.S. Recessions," Federal Reserve Bank of New York, 1996](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/current_issues/ci2-7.html).*

Their most striking example came from the early 1980s. A spread of -2.18 points in the first quarter of 1981 implied an 86.5 percent chance of recession four quarters later. The National Bureau of Economic Research dates a recession from July 1981 to November 1982, so the economy was indeed contracting in early 1982.

One caution: those benchmarks come from a specific model and era. The New York Fed itself warns that parameter values may shift over time and that benchmarks for one spread don't transfer neatly to another.

## Why the signal is hard to trade

Even believers in the yield curve admit three problems.

**The lag is long and unpredictable.** Bauer and Mertens found that the time between the spread turning negative and a recession starting ranged from 6 to 24 months. Estrella and Mishkin said the 10-year minus 3-month spread worked best two to six quarters ahead. A two-year window is a long time for a portfolio.

**False alarms happen.** The New York Fed lists the 1967 credit crunch as a false signal. And the most recent episode is a big one. A Fed Board note published July 12, 2022, observed that the 10-year minus 2-year spread had recently turned negative. By the summer of 2023, the New York Fed's model put the odds of recession at about 66 percent using July 2023 data, according to the St. Louis Fed. Yet the NBER's chronology of business cycles lists no recession after the one that ended in April 2020.

**Different spreads can disagree.** The New York Fed notes that the 2s10s spread may invert earlier than the 10-year minus 3-month spread. A Fed Board team in 2022 went further, arguing that a "near-term forward spread," built from expected short-term rates over the next six quarters, captures the recession signal better than long-term spreads, partly because long yields also carry risk premiums that have nothing to do with the economy's next move.

Bauer and Mertens tested that risk-premium point and reached a different conclusion: splitting the spread into its expectations and term-premium parts didn't improve forecasts. The honest summary is that researchers agree the spread contains information and disagree about exactly why.

## What a yield curve inversion means for stocks

The yield curve is a recession signal first and a stock market signal second. Still, it touches stock prices through several channels.

**Earnings risk.** Recessions usually mean falling corporate profits. If the curve is right about a downturn, earnings estimates will eventually come down, and recessions have often overlapped with [bear markets](/articles/stock-market-correction-vs-bear-market/).

**Valuation pressure.** An inversion usually happens because short-term rates are high. High rates raise the discount rate investors apply to future profits, which tends to weigh most on companies whose earnings are far in the future. We walk through that math in [how interest rates affect stock valuations](/articles/how-interest-rates-affect-stock-valuations/).

**Bank margins.** Traditional banks borrow short (deposits) and lend long (mortgages, business loans). When the curve is flat or inverted, the spread they earn on new lending shrinks. That's why financial stocks often react to changes in the curve's shape, not just the level of rates.

**Timing.** Because the lag can stretch to two years, an inversion on its own says little about what stocks will do next week or next month. Stocks don't have to fall just because the curve inverted, and the signal says nothing about how far the market might run first.

The way the curve changes shape also matters. Analysts use four labels:

| Move | What happens | What it often suggests |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bear flattening | Short yields rise faster than long yields | Markets expect tighter Fed policy |
| Bull flattening | Long yields fall faster than short yields | Growth or inflation worries are pushing investors into long bonds |
| Bull steepening | Short yields fall faster than long yields | Markets expect Fed rate cuts, sometimes because the economy is weakening |
| Bear steepening | Long yields rise faster than short yields | Investors want more pay for inflation or long-term risk |

These are descriptions, not forecasts. One point that surprises many investors: the riskiest phase is not always the inversion itself. A curve that steepens back to positive because the Fed is rushing to cut rates in a weakening economy can be a worse sign for stocks than the inversion that came before. Our explainer on [what happens to stocks when the Fed cuts rates](/articles/what-happens-to-stocks-when-the-fed-cuts-rates/) covers the difference between "insurance" cuts and emergency cuts.

## A practical example: reading this week's move

Here is how the past week looked in the Federal Reserve's daily data:

| Date (2026) | 10-year yield | 2-year yield | 2s10s spread |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| November 6 | 4.86% | 4.51% | +0.35 |
| November 9 | 4.88% | 4.56% | +0.32 |
| November 10 | 4.98% | 4.73% | +0.25 |
| November 12 | 5.10% | 4.86% | +0.24 |

*Source: FRED series [DGS10](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DGS10), [DGS2](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DGS2) and [T10Y2Y](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/T10Y2Y). FRED shows no value for November 11, Veterans Day.*

Both yields jumped, but the 2-year rose about 35 basis points while the 10-year rose about 24. That is a textbook bear flattening: the front end, which is most sensitive to Fed expectations, moved more. The curve is still upward sloping, so this is not an inversion. But anyone watching the 2s10s spread would notice it has narrowed by about a third in less than a week.

How should you read that? Not as a recession call. A move like this tells you the bond market has repriced the Fed's path toward higher rates, and higher rates across the curve matter for stock valuations whether the curve inverts or not. For stock valuations, the 10-year climbing above 5 percent is arguably a bigger direct story than the thin positive spread.

## How to read the yield curve without overreacting

1. **Pick one spread and stick with it.** The 2s10s and 10-year minus 3-month spreads can give different answers. Know which one a headline is using.
2. **Look at monthly averages, not single days.** The New York Fed's own guidance is that a signal lasting a month or more deserves attention.
3. **Ask why the curve moved.** Did short rates rise on Fed expectations, or did long rates fall on growth fears? The table of four moves above helps.
4. **Check it against the real economy.** [Weekly jobless claims](/articles/initial-jobless-claims-explained/), payrolls and [GDP](/articles/what-is-gdp-and-why-it-matters/) tell you whether the slowdown the curve implies is actually showing up.
5. **Respect the lag.** Even a correct signal has historically come 6 to 24 months early.
6. **Watch what happens after an inversion ends.** A sharp steepening driven by expected rate cuts can be more telling than the inversion itself.
