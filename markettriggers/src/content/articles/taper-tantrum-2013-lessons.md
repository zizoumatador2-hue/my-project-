**The taper tantrum was the 2013 bond market selloff that followed Fed Chairman Ben Bernanke's hint that the Federal Reserve could soon slow its bond buying. The Fed hadn't changed policy yet, but the 10-year Treasury yield climbed roughly a full percentage point and mortgage rates jumped. The lesson: Fed guidance can move markets before any decision does.**

Most market shocks come from something that happened. The taper tantrum came from something that might happen. A few sentences in congressional testimony and a press conference were enough to reprice the entire bond market over a single summer. That makes it one of the cleanest case studies of how [the Federal Reserve moves the stock market](/#the-federal-reserve-the-most-powerful-market-trigger) through words alone, and it still shapes how the Fed talks to investors today.

## The setup: QE3 and $85 billion a month

By the spring of 2013, the Fed had held its policy rate near zero for years and was running its third round of quantitative easing. In his May 22, 2013 testimony to Congress's Joint Economic Committee, Bernanke described the program: the Fed had been buying longer-term Treasury securities "at a pace of $45 billion per month" and agency mortgage-backed securities "at a pace of $40 billion per month."

That's $85 billion of bond buying every month, with no fixed end date. The Fed said it would keep going "until the outlook for the labor market has improved substantially." Investors had grown used to the Fed acting as a steady, enormous buyer of long-term debt. That buyer helped hold down long-term yields and mortgage rates, and by extension it supported stock valuations.

The word "taper" simply meant slowing that pace. Not selling bonds, not shrinking the balance sheet, not raising rates. Just buying a little less each month. If you want the difference between buying less and actually shrinking the balance sheet, our [quantitative tightening explainer](/articles/what-is-quantitative-tightening/) walks through it.

## May 22, 2013: the remark that started it

Bernanke's prepared testimony that day was mostly a defense of the program. He warned that "a premature tightening of monetary policy could lead interest rates to rise temporarily" but would also carry "a substantial risk of slowing or ending the economic recovery." He said the Fed was "prepared to increase or reduce the pace of its asset purchases" as the outlook changed.

The market-moving line came in the question period. Rep. Kevin Brady, the committee's chairman, asked when the Fed expected to begin pulling back and what benchmarks it was watching. According to the official hearing transcript, Bernanke answered: "If we see continued improvement and we have confidence that that is going to be sustained, then we could in the next few meetings, take a step down in our pace of purchases."

He added a qualifier that got far less attention: "Again, if we do that, it would not mean that we are automatically aiming towards a complete wind down."

Read carefully, it was a conditional statement wrapped in caveats. Markets heard something simpler. The Fed's support had an expiration date, and it might be sooner than expected.

## June 19: the timeline gets specific

Four weeks later, after the June FOMC meeting, Bernanke laid out a rough schedule at his press conference. He recapped it in his July 17, 2013 testimony: the Committee anticipated "that it would be appropriate to begin to moderate the monthly pace of purchases later this year," then reduce purchases in measured steps through the first half of 2014 and end them around midyear. By then, he said, "unemployment would be in the vicinity of 7 percent."

He stressed that the plan depended on the data and that purchases "are by no means on a preset course." If conditions weakened or financial conditions got too tight, the pace could be held for longer, and the Fed was even prepared to increase purchases for a time.

That nuance didn't calm things down right away. Investors now had a calendar to trade against, and the selloff in bonds continued into the summer.

## What the taper tantrum did to markets

The Fed's own July 2013 Monetary Policy Report described the reaction plainly: "beginning in May, longer-term interest rates rose significantly and asset price volatility increased as investors responded to somewhat better-than-expected economic data as well as Federal Reserve communications about monetary policy."

Here's how large the moves were, using official and agency data:

| Measure | Before | After | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10-year Treasury yield | Around 2% (May 2013) | Around 3% (December 2013) | St. Louis Fed, FRED Blog |
| 10-year yield change during the episode | | +137 basis points | Federal Reserve, FEDS Notes (2023) |
| 30-year fixed mortgage rate | 3.35% (week ending May 3) | 4.51% (week ending July 12) | Fannie Mae, citing Freddie Mac PMMS |

| High-yield bond spread | | +16 basis points | Federal Reserve, FEDS Notes (2023) |

A few things stand out.

**Mortgages took the biggest hit in everyday terms.** Fannie Mae's economists calculated an increase of 116 basis points in nine weeks. By September 19, Freddie Mac noted that mortgage rates "have increased more than one percentage point since early May when speculation about Fed tapering began." For a household shopping for a home, that changed the monthly payment on the same loan by a meaningful amount within one season.

**The bond market moved far more than the stock market.** A 2023 Fed staff note looking back at the episode found that "the increases in the VIX index and high-yield spreads were modest during the taper tantrum." In other words, this was mainly a rates event. Stocks wobbled, but the main action was in Treasuries and anything priced off them.

**The reaction was out of proportion to the news.** The same Fed note made a striking point: the episode earned its name because the strong market reaction came in response to Fed communications "that were largely consistent with market analysts' expectations." Analysts broadly expected tapering at some point. What changed was the sense of timing and the realization that the exit had begun to take shape.

## Why a hint caused a selloff

If the Fed wasn't buying fewer bonds yet, why did yields jump so fast? Three mechanics help explain it.

First, markets price the future. A long-term bond's yield reflects where investors expect short-term rates to go over the life of the bond, plus extra compensation for uncertainty. When the Fed signals that bond buying will end sooner, investors also pull forward their guess about when rate hikes will start. Both pieces push long yields up immediately. We cover how that feeds into stock prices in [how interest rates affect stock valuations](/articles/how-interest-rates-affect-stock-valuations/).

Second, a lot of investors were positioned for low yields to last. When the outlook shifts, everyone tries to adjust at once, and prices move further than any single piece of news would justify.

Third, the 10-year Treasury yield is the benchmark for mortgages and much of corporate borrowing. When it moves, the effect spreads quickly. Our [10-year Treasury yield explainer](/articles/what-is-the-10-year-treasury-yield/) covers that chain in more detail.

## September's surprise and December's taper

By September 2013, many investors were watching for the first cut in purchases. The Fed held off. In his September 18 press conference, Bernanke said "the Committee decided to await more evidence that the recovery's progress will be sustained before adjusting the pace of asset purchases."

The reason was the tantrum itself. Bernanke said the Committee had "some concern that the rapid tightening of financial conditions in recent months could have the effect of slowing growth." Higher mortgage rates were exactly the kind of tightening the Fed had been trying to avoid. The market had, in effect, tightened policy on the Fed's behalf.

The actual taper came on December 18, 2013. Beginning in January 2014, the Fed would buy MBS "at a pace of $35 billion per month rather than $40 billion per month" and Treasuries "at a pace of $40 billion per month rather than $45 billion per month." It continued trimming through 2014, and on October 29, 2014, the FOMC "decided to conclude its asset purchase program this month," while continuing to reinvest principal from the bonds it already held.

| Date | Event |
| --- | --- |
| May 22, 2013 | Bernanke tells Congress the Fed could "take a step down" in purchases "in the next few meetings" |
| June 19, 2013 | Press conference outlines tapering later in 2013, ending purchases around mid-2014 |
| September 18, 2013 | Fed holds purchases at $85 billion a month |
| December 18, 2013 | Fed announces first $10 billion reduction, effective January 2014 |
| October 29, 2014 | Fed concludes the asset purchase program |

## 2021: how the Fed avoided a repeat

The clearest proof that the Fed learned from 2013 came eight years later. During the pandemic, the Fed again bought bonds in huge volume, and again it eventually needed to taper.

This time the market barely flinched. The St. Louis Fed's FRED Blog noted that "investors remained placid when the Fed hinted at tapering in July 2021," with the 10-year yield staying around 1.3 percent. Its explanation: "the announcement in 2021 was in line with market expectations and the announcement in 2013 came earlier than expected."

When the Fed made it official on November 3, 2021, it said it would begin reducing monthly net purchases "by $10 billion for Treasury securities and $5 billion for agency mortgage-backed securities," and that "similar reductions in the pace of net asset purchases will likely be appropriate each month." That was a mechanical, pre-announced path. No guessing about timing, no single remark for markets to seize on.

There's a modest tension in the record worth noting. The 2023 Fed note says the 2013 communications were broadly in line with analysts' expectations, while the FRED Blog describes the 2013 signal as earlier than expected. Both can be true: tapering was expected eventually, but the timing Bernanke implied came sooner than many investors had positioned for.

## Lessons for investors

**Guidance is policy.** The Fed doesn't have to change a number to change financial conditions. When it shifts the expected path of policy, markets reprice immediately. That's why traders parse every FOMC statement and why the [Fed's dot plot](/articles/what-is-the-fed-dot-plot/) can move markets on its own.

**Rates are the first transmission channel.** In 2013, Treasuries and mortgages took the hit while stock volatility stayed relatively contained. When the Fed signals a change, watch the bond market first. Rate-sensitive parts of the stock market, like homebuilders, utilities and long-duration growth companies, tend to feel it next.

**Conditional statements get stripped of their conditions.** Bernanke's May remark came with an "if" and a caveat. Markets traded the headline. If you read Fed communication, read the qualifiers, but expect the market to react to the simplest interpretation first.

**Markets can force the Fed's hand.** The September 2013 pause shows the feedback loop. A sharp rise in yields can tighten conditions enough that the Fed waits longer than it planned. That's useful context when yields spike after a Fed signal.

**Bonds and stocks can fall together.** A rate shock driven by policy expectations can hit both asset classes at once, which undercuts the usual diversification math. We explain when that happens in [why stocks and bonds sometimes fall together](/articles/stocks-and-bonds-falling-together/).
