**The main difference in a correction vs bear market is depth. A correction is generally a drop of 10% to 19.9% from a recent high, while a bear market is a drop of 20% or more. Both are conventions, not official rulings. Bear markets have historically gone much deeper and lasted far longer.**

Those two numbers get a lot of airtime whenever stocks slide, and they can make a decline sound more orderly than it is. Nobody rings a bell at 10% or 20%. Still, the labels are useful shorthand if you know how they're measured and what they leave out. They also connect to the bigger question in our [guide to what moves the stock market](/): what changed in investors' expectations for profits and interest rates? For more basics, see the [investor education hub](/investor-education/).

## Correction vs bear market: the definitions side by side

| | Correction | Bear market |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Usual threshold | Down 10% to 19.9% from a recent high | Down 20% or more from a recent high |
| Who defines it | Market convention | Market convention |
| S&P 500 declines, 1980 to October 2022 | 14 | 6 |
| Average decline in that period | About 15% | About 38% |
| Average length, peak to trough | About 88 days | About 414 days |
| Recent example | February to April 2025, about 18.9% | January to October 2022, 25.4% |

*Counts and averages are our calculations from [Yardeni Research's S&P 500 tables](https://archive.yardeni.com/pub/sp500corrbeartables.pdf), which run through October 2022 and count calendar days. The 2025 figure comes from [closing data reported by Dow Jones](https://itiger.com/news/2526280840).*

Sources vary at the edges. FINRA says a [correction means prices reverse by at least 10 percent](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/key-terms-tough-times-vocabulary-stressed-markets) before resuming their earlier trend, and that a 20 percent fall in a broad index is generally considered a bear market. The SEC's Investor.gov says a bear market generally occurs when a broad index [falls 20% or more over at least a two-month period](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/bear-market). Hartford Funds uses [closing prices and the most recent high](https://hartfordfunds.com/practice-management/client-conversations/managing-volatility/bear-markets.html) and doesn't include a time test.

That time test matters more than you might expect. The S&P 500 lost 33.9% between February 19 and March 23, 2020, which took just 33 calendar days. The Yardeni tables list it among the 20%-plus declines anyway, even though it wouldn't meet a strict two-month reading.

And "pullback"? It has no standard meaning. People generally use it for a drop too shallow to be called a correction. Treat it as a description, not a measurement.

## How the math works

Each decline is measured from the most recent high, usually on a closing basis, and the math is simple. Take a hypothetical index that peaks at 7,000:

- Down 10% puts it at 6,300, which is correction territory.
- Down 20% puts it at 5,600, which is bear market territory.
- If it climbs to a new high of 7,500 first, the lines move up to 6,750 and 6,000.

Three details trip people up.

**The index matters.** On March 27, 2026, [Reuters reported](https://new.streetinsider.com/Reuters/Dow+confirms+correction+as+traders+worry+about+war/26233957.html) that the Dow had confirmed a correction, down 10% from its February 10 record close. The Nasdaq Composite was already in one, measured from its October 29 record. The S&P 500 sat about 9% below its own January 27 record. Three indexes, three different peak dates and no single label that fit all of them. Something similar happened in April 2025, when [Fortune reported](https://fortune.com/2025/04/07/what-is-a-bear-market-trump-tariff-market-meltdown/) the Nasdaq was already in a bear market while the S&P 500 closed 17.6% below its high.

**Closing and intraday numbers differ.** An index can cross a threshold during the session and finish back above it. Most historical tables use the close, so a headline that says "briefly entered a bear market" isn't describing the same thing.

**Individual stocks run on their own clock.** A single company can fall 30% after a bad [earnings report](/articles/why-do-stocks-fall-after-earnings/) while the broad index barely moves. The two terms are mostly useful for broad indexes.

A separate set of numbers often gets mixed in with these. The market-wide [circuit breakers](https://www.sec.gov/rules/sro/nyse/2022/34-94441.pdf) trigger at 7%, 13% and 20%, but they compare the S&P 500 with the prior day's close and not with a peak. A Level 1 or Level 2 trigger before 3:25 p.m. Eastern halts trading for 15 minutes, and Level 3 ends trading for the day. Level 1 halts fired on four days in March 2020: the 9th, 12th, 16th and 18th. A 20% circuit breaker and a 20% bear market have nothing to do with each other.

## What history shows about depth and duration

Here are the S&P 500 declines of 10% or more since 2010 in the Yardeni tables, plus the 2025 episode.

| Peak | Trough | Decline | Calendar days | Label |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Apr 23, 2010 | Jul 2, 2010 | 16.0% | 70 | Correction |
| Apr 29, 2011 | Oct 3, 2011 | 19.4% | 157 | Correction |
| May 21, 2015 | Aug 25, 2015 | 12.4% | 96 | Correction |
| Nov 3, 2015 | Feb 11, 2016 | 13.3% | 100 | Correction |
| Jan 26, 2018 | Feb 8, 2018 | 10.2% | 13 | Correction |
| Sep 20, 2018 | Dec 24, 2018 | 19.8% | 95 | Correction |
| Feb 19, 2020 | Mar 23, 2020 | 33.9% | 33 | Bear market |
| Jan 3, 2022 | Oct 12, 2022 | 25.4% | 282 | Bear market |
| Feb 19, 2025 | Apr 8, 2025 | 18.9% | 48 | Correction |

*Through 2022: [Yardeni Research](https://archive.yardeni.com/pub/sp500corrbeartables.pdf). Those tables end in October 2022, so the list skips the [2023 correction](https://whec.com/?p=768211). 2025: closing levels from [Dow Jones](https://itiger.com/news/2526280840).*

The longer record points the same way. Hartford Funds, citing Ned Davis Research through March 2025, counts [27 bear markets in the S&P 500 since 1928](https://hartfordfunds.com/practice-management/client-conversations/managing-volatility/bear-markets.html), with an average loss of about 35% over an average of 289 days. Bull markets averaged a 112% gain over 988 days. Bear markets also came more often before 1945 than after: about one every 1.5 years from 1928 to 1945, then about one every 5.1 years.

Notice how much the averages hide. The 2020 bear market ended in about a month. The one that began in March 2000 took 929 days to hit bottom, and the S&P 500 lost 49.1% along the way. An "average bear market" isn't something you should expect to actually see.

## The near misses tell you a lot

Look at the Yardeni numbers since 1980 and one pattern jumps out. Four corrections stopped within about a point of the line: 19.9% in 1990, 19.3% in 1998, 19.4% in 2011 and 19.8% in 2018. The S&P 500's 18.9% drop in 2025 was another close call.

Had the index closed a little lower on any of those days, it would have been remembered as a bear market. Hartford notes that the 1990 drop is the reason the bull run from December 1987 to March 2000 is usually counted as one unbroken stretch. A difference of a tenth of a point changed the history books, though it probably didn't change much for anyone holding stocks at the time.

So don't read the 20% line as a change in the market's nature. An index down 19.5% and one down 20.5% are in about the same place. What separates a correction from a bear market tends to be what happens next, and in particular, whether the reasons behind the decline persist.

## What usually separates the two

No formula predicts which declines keep going. Still, a stock index is a claim on future profits, discounted back at some interest rate. That gives you two broad places to look.

**Earnings expectations.** A recession can pull profit estimates down, and that gives stocks a reason to keep falling even after a sharp drop. Recessions aren't required, though. Hartford counts 27 bear markets against 15 recessions over the same period, so plenty of bear markets came without one.

**Interest rates and valuations.** Higher rates can shrink what investors will pay for the same dollar of future earnings, as we explain in [how interest rates affect stock valuations](/articles/how-interest-rates-affect-stock-valuations/). That was central in 2022, when [high inflation and aggressive Fed hikes](/articles/how-does-inflation-affect-stocks/) hit stocks and bonds together.

**The reversal of the shock.** Some declines start with a specific event that later eases. The S&P 500 was [already down more than 8%](https://www.itiger.com/news/2531078557) before the April 2, 2025 tariff announcement and then fell further. The rebound that followed has been tied to a 90-day pause on part of those tariffs and to the start of trade talks.

Fed cuts on their own don't settle the question. Our piece on [what happens to stocks when the Fed cuts rates](/articles/what-happens-to-stocks-when-the-fed-cuts-rates/) looks at 2001 and 2007 to 2008, when stocks fell through a cutting cycle because earnings were weakening at the same time.

Sector behavior can offer a clue too. A decline led by economically sensitive groups such as industrials, materials and consumer discretionary suggests investors are worried about growth. When the steepest losses cluster in high-valuation growth stocks while yields climb, the concern may be more about rates. These are tendencies, not rules, and a sell-off can start in one place and spread.

## A real example: the 18.9% drop of 2025

The S&P 500 set a record close of 6,144.15 on February 19, 2025. By [April 8 it had fallen to 4,982.77](https://itiger.com/news/2526280840), a loss of 18.90%. By the standard definition, that was a correction, and a severe one. The Associated Press had already called the slide the index's [first correction since 2023](https://whec.com/?p=768211) when it first crossed 10%.

The way back was quick. The S&P 500 [closed at a record 6,173.07 on June 27, 2025](https://www.itiger.com/news/2546729797), about 24% above that April low. Dow Jones Market Data called it the fastest recovery on record into new-high territory after a decline of at least 15%.

Two lessons come out of that episode, and neither depends on the label. First, the drop felt like a bear market to many investors even though it technically never became one. Second, the sharpest gains arrived close to the worst of the losses. Hartford's data makes a broader version of that point: about 42% of the S&P 500's strongest days from 2003 through March 2025 fell during a bear market, and another 36% came in the first two months of a bull market. Big up days often cluster in rough stretches, which is one reason timing a decline is so hard.

## Why the recovery math is harsher than it looks

Losses and gains don't work symmetrically, because each gain starts from a smaller base.

| Decline | Gain needed to get back to even |
| --- | --- |
| 5% | 5.3% |
| 10% | 11.1% |
| 15% | 17.6% |
| 20% | 25.0% |
| 35% | 53.8% |
| 50% | 100.0% |

*Simple arithmetic; figures ignore dividends, fees and taxes.*

A correction's recovery math is manageable. An average-sized bear market needs a gain of more than 50%. That's part of why depth, not just the name, should drive how you think about a decline.

## How to read the headlines

When a "correction" or "bear market" alert hits your phone, a few quick questions will tell you more than the label:

1. **Which index, and from which peak?** The Dow, S&P 500 and Nasdaq can sit in different places.
2. **Close or intraday?** Check whether the threshold held at the end of the session.
3. **What's driving it?** Falling earnings estimates, rising yields and a one-off shock have different implications.
4. **What's the bond market doing?** Falling stocks with falling yields often reads as a growth scare. Falling stocks with rising yields points more toward inflation or rate worries.
5. **What does your own time horizon look like?** A plan built for decades and money needed next year face very different risks from the same decline. A licensed adviser can help with decisions specific to you.
