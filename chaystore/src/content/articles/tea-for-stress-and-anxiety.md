A warm cup of tea can be a comforting pause in a stressful day, and many people turn to caffeine-free herbal teas such as chamomile, lemon balm, lavender and rooibos for that purpose. However, tea is not a treatment for anxiety or any medical condition, research on herbal teas and stress is limited and mixed, and caffeine in some teas can make jitters worse. Use tea as a gentle ritual, and talk with a healthcare professional if anxiety is persistent.

This article is general information only and is not medical advice. If you feel unsafe or are in crisis, contact local emergency services or a crisis line right away.

## Why a cup of tea feels calming

Part of tea's calming effect is behavioral. Making tea gives you a few minutes to slow down: you boil water, wait, smell the steam and sip slowly. That pause, the warmth in your hands and the routine itself can lower the pace of your day. For some people, the ritual matters more than the specific herb.

For an overview of tea types and how they differ, start with our [complete guide to tea](/#tea-guide).

## Caffeine and anxious feelings

Caffeine is a stimulant. In some people, especially those who are sensitive or who drink a lot, it can cause jitteriness, a faster heartbeat, restlessness and trouble sleeping, which can feel like or worsen anxiety. Black tea, green tea, white tea, oolong, matcha and yerba mate all contain caffeine. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration says about 400 milligrams a day is not generally associated with negative effects for most healthy adults, in its consumer guide [Spilling the Beans: How Much Caffeine Is Too Much?](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much), but sensitivity varies widely.

If you notice that tea makes you jittery, try:

- Switching to caffeine-free herbal teas. See our [caffeine-free tea list](/guides/caffeine-free-tea-list/).
- Using less leaf, cooler water and shorter steeping for lower caffeine.
- Avoiding caffeine on an empty stomach or late in the day.
- Reducing gradually instead of stopping suddenly.

Our guide to [caffeine in tea vs coffee](/guides/how-much-caffeine-in-tea-vs-coffee/) gives typical amounts for different drinks.

### What about L-theanine?

L-theanine is an amino acid found naturally in tea leaves, particularly in shade-grown teas such as matcha and gyokuro. Some small studies have looked at whether L-theanine, alone or with caffeine, affects alertness and relaxation. Findings are limited, and the amounts in a cup of tea are modest. For general information about green tea and health, the [National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/green-tea) is a reliable source. Treat claims that any tea "cures" anxiety with caution.

## How to make a calming cup

1. **Choose a caffeine-free herbal tea** that you enjoy.
2. **Measure:** 1 to 2 teaspoons of dried herbs (or 1 tea bag) per 8 oz cup. See [how much tea per cup](/guides/how-much-tea-per-cup/).
3. **Heat water to a full boil** and pour it over the herbs.
4. **Cover and steep for 5 to 7 minutes**, which keeps the aromatic oils in the cup.
5. **Sit down and sip slowly,** without your phone if you can.

For more details on method, read [how to brew loose leaf tea](/guides/how-to-brew-loose-leaf-tea/).

## Build a small stress-relief routine around tea

Tea works best alongside other habits:

- **Breathing:** take a few slow breaths while the tea steeps.
- **Short breaks:** two or three minutes away from the screen.
- **Movement:** a short walk or stretching.
- **Sleep routine:** consistent bedtimes; see [best tea for sleep](/guides/best-tea-for-sleep/).
- **Limit stimulants:** watch caffeine and energy drinks.
- **Talk to someone:** a friend, family member or professional.

## Who should be careful?

Check with a healthcare professional about herbal teas if you:

- Are pregnant, trying to conceive or breastfeeding.
- Take prescription medication, especially sedatives, antidepressants, blood thinners or blood-pressure medicines. Some herbs can interact with medications.
- Have allergies, especially to ragweed or related plants.
- Have a medical condition.
- Plan to give tea to a child.

## When to seek professional help

Everyone feels stressed or anxious sometimes. But if your worry is persistent, hard to control, affects your sleep, work or relationships, or comes with physical symptoms such as chest pain or shortness of breath, please contact a healthcare professional. Effective help exists, including therapy and, when appropriate, medication. Tea is a pleasant addition to self-care, not a substitute for care.

## A five-minute tea pause you can use today

When the day feels heavy, a short, structured pause works better than drinking tea at your desk while you keep working. Try this: fill the kettle and, while it heats, stand up and stretch your shoulders and neck. Measure your tea slowly. Pour the water and set a timer for the steeping time. While it steeps, close your eyes for a minute and breathe in for four counts and out for six. Notice the smell of the tea. When the timer rings, carry the cup to a chair away from your screen and sip without multitasking. Many people find that even five minutes like this resets their pace. The tea matters less than the pause, so pick any caffeine-free blend you enjoy.

## Tea and caffeine timing

If you do drink caffeinated tea, timing helps. Many people find that having caffeine with food, rather than on an empty stomach, feels gentler. Others notice that caffeine after midday affects their sleep and next-day mood. A simple experiment is to keep a short note for a week with what you drank, when, and how you felt an hour later. If a pattern appears, such as jitters after matcha but not after a mild white tea, adjust accordingly. Our guides on [matcha vs green tea](/guides/matcha-vs-green-tea/) and [sencha vs matcha](/guides/sencha-vs-matcha/) explain why matcha tends to deliver more caffeine per serving.

## Summary

A warm, caffeine-free cup of tea can be a soothing pause. Chamomile, lemon balm, lavender and rooibos are popular choices, but none is a treatment for anxiety, and caffeine in other teas can make some people feel more on edge. Pay attention to how you feel, ask a professional about herbs if you take medicine or are pregnant, and seek help if anxiety is persistent.
