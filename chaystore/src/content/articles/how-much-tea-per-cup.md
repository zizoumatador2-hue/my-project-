For most teas, use about 1 level teaspoon of loose leaf (2 to 3 grams) per 8 oz (240 ml) cup of water. That is the simplest answer to "how much tea per cup," and it works well for black tea, green tea and many blends. Bulky leaves need more teaspoons to reach the same weight, and your own taste is the final judge.

This guide gives you the tea-to-water ratio, a teaspoon chart for common tea types, and the simple adjustments that fix a weak or bitter cup. It pairs with our step-by-step guide on [how to brew loose leaf tea](/guides/how-to-brew-loose-leaf-tea/).

## The basic tea-to-water ratio

The most useful way to think about tea strength is a ratio of leaf weight to water volume. A widely used starting point is:

- **2 to 3 grams of tea per 8 oz (240 ml) of water.**

Why weight instead of teaspoons? A teaspoon of tiny, dense leaves such as a rolled gunpowder green tea can weigh much more than a teaspoon of fluffy, open white tea leaves. If you drink loose leaf tea regularly, a small digital kitchen scale that measures in tenths of a gram is one of the cheapest upgrades you can make.

### Scaling the ratio

| Water amount | Tea (starting point) |
| --- | --- |
| 8 oz / 240 ml (1 cup) | 2–3 g |
| 16 oz / 475 ml (large mug) | 4–6 g |
| 24 oz / 710 ml (small pot) | 6–9 g |
| 32 oz / 950 ml (quart pot) | 8–12 g |
| 1 gallon / 3.8 L (iced tea batch) | See the iced tea section below |

## Teaspoons of loose tea per cup

If you do not have a scale, use measuring spoons and adjust. These amounts assume an 8 oz cup and a level (not heaping) teaspoon unless noted.

| Tea type | Teaspoons per 8 oz cup | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Black (broken leaf, such as many Assams) | 1 | Small pieces pack densely |
| Black (whole leaf) | 1 to 1½ | Larger leaves take more room |
| Green (rolled or small leaf) | 1 | Dense, expands a lot |
| Green (flat or large leaf) | 1½ to 2 | Light and bulky |
| White tea | 1½ to 2 | Fluffy buds and leaves |
| Oolong (rolled, ball-shaped) | 1 | Dense, unfurls fully |
| Oolong (open leaf) | 1½ to 2 | Bulkier |
| Herbal blends (flowers, herbs) | 1 to 2 | Varies widely |
| Matcha (powder) | ½ teaspoon (about 1 g) | See our guide on [how to make a matcha latte](/guides/how-to-make-matcha-latte/) |

Treat the table as a starting range. If the tea is too weak, move up by a quarter teaspoon. If it tastes harsh or bitter, scale back.

### What about "one for the pot"?

An old saying suggests one teaspoon per person and one more "for the pot." That rule of thumb dates from a time when tea was weaker. A ratio by weight scales more predictably, so use the table above for the number of cups you are making.

## A simple tea measuring chart for common cups and pots

Use this to picture quantities at a glance.

| Vessel | Typical size | Loose tea to start with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small teacup | 6 oz | ¾ to 1 teaspoon |
| Standard mug | 8–10 oz | 1 to 1¼ teaspoons |
| Large mug | 12–16 oz | 1½ to 2 teaspoons |
| Two-cup teapot | 16 oz | 2 teaspoons |
| Four-cup teapot | 32 oz | 4 teaspoons |

These numbers assume ordinary black or green leaf. For white tea and herbal blends, add a little.

## How strong should tea be?

Tea strength is personal. Some people like a delicate cup that shows off subtle flavor. Others want a bold, brisk cup that stands up to milk and sugar. A good method is to start with the standard ratio and then change one thing at a time:

1. **Too weak?** Add more leaf next time, about a quarter teaspoon more.
2. **Too bitter or drying?** Use less leaf, a slightly cooler water temperature, or a shorter steep.
3. **Flat or dull?** Check that the tea is fresh and the water is clean. Old leaf loses aroma, which is why our guide to [how to store loose leaf tea](/guides/how-to-store-loose-leaf-tea/) matters.

### More leaf versus longer steeping

When you want a stronger cup, increase the amount of tea rather than extending the steeping time. Longer steeping tends to pull out more of the compounds that taste astringent or bitter. A higher leaf-to-water ratio builds flavor and body while keeping the timing in the range the tea was meant for.

## Strength with milk and sugar

If you add milk, you dilute the flavor, so use a little more tea. For a robust black tea or a [masala chai](/guides/how-to-make-chai-at-home/), many people use closer to 1½ teaspoons per cup, or the tea is simmered with milk for several minutes. Read more about the difference in our guide to [masala chai vs chai latte](/guides/masala-chai-vs-chai-latte/).

## How much tea for iced tea?

Iced tea needs to taste strong when hot because the ice will dilute it. There are two common approaches:

- **Hot-brew and dilute.** Brew double-strength tea (about twice the usual leaf for the same amount of hot water), let it steep for the normal time, remove the leaves, then pour over ice or add cold water.
- **Cold brew.** Use the standard ratio, add cold water, and refrigerate for several hours. Cold brewing is gentle and often less bitter.

Our guide to [how to make iced tea](/guides/how-to-make-iced-tea/) walks through both methods with batch sizes.

## Tea bags versus loose leaf

A standard tea bag usually holds a pre-measured amount of fine tea, commonly around 2 grams, though the amount varies by brand and bag type. That is close to the loose leaf starting point, but the leaf size is different, which changes how it extracts. See [loose leaf tea vs tea bags](/guides/loose-leaf-tea-vs-tea-bags/) for the full comparison.

## Troubleshooting guide

| Problem | Likely cause | Quick fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Watery, thin | Too little leaf, or leaves cannot expand | Add leaf, use a larger infuser |
| Bitter, dry mouthfeel | Too much leaf, water too hot, steeped too long | Use less leaf or cooler water; shorten the time |
| Muddy or cloudy | Very fine leaf or too much agitation | Use a finer strainer and avoid squeezing the infuser |
| Bland | Stale tea | Replace tea; store airtight |

## Measuring without a scale or spoon

If you are camping or traveling, you can approximate with common objects. A heaping pinch between thumb and two fingers is often around a teaspoon of loose leaf. Use your best judgment and refine after the first cup. Over a few brews, you will learn what a "normal" amount looks like for your favorite tea.

## Calculating cost per cup

Once you know how much tea you use per cup, you can work out the real cost of any tea. Divide the price of the package by the number of grams, then multiply by the grams you use per cup. For example, if a 100 g bag of tea costs 15 dollars and you use 3 g per cup, each cup costs about 45 cents before re-steeping. If you get two infusions from the same leaves, the cost per cup drops to roughly 23 cents.

This is why many people find loose leaf tea better value than it first appears. The package price looks high, but the number of cups from a package can be large. Use the calculation to compare options fairly, including tea bags; a box of 20 bags at 2 grams each holds only 40 grams of tea.

## Adjusting for taste, strength and caffeine

If you want to reduce caffeine without giving up your tea, lowering the amount of leaf per cup is one of the simplest changes, along with shorter steeping and cooler water. If you want to increase body and richness, increase the leaf rather than the time, and consider a tea made for strength, such as a robust Assam. Our guide to [caffeine in tea vs coffee](/guides/how-much-caffeine-in-tea-vs-coffee/) explains how these variables interact.

## Use a scale for consistency

A kitchen scale that measures to 0.1 gram is inexpensive and gives you repeatable results. Weigh your usual teaspoon of a few different teas, and you will quickly see how much they differ: a level teaspoon of a dense rolled oolong can weigh nearly double a teaspoon of fluffy white tea. Once you know your own numbers, write them on the lid of each tin so you do not need to guess in the morning.

## Putting it into practice

1. Pick your tea and note the package's suggested amount. Producers often print an amount per cup.
2. Start with 1 teaspoon (2 to 3 g) per 8 oz.
3. Brew with water at the right temperature. Our guide on [water temperature for green tea](/guides/water-temperature-for-green-tea/) gives details for delicate teas.
4. Taste, then adjust the amount, not just the time.
5. Write down what you liked so you can repeat it.
