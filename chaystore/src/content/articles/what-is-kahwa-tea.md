Kahwa is a traditional Kashmiri tea made from green tea simmered with spices such as cardamom and cinnamon, flavored with saffron and often garnished with chopped almonds. It is usually served without milk, so it tastes lighter, more aromatic and more floral than masala chai. A cup of kahwa is warm, gently sweet and fragrant.

This guide explains what kahwa is, how to make a simple version at home, how it compares with chai, and variations to try.

## What is kahwa?

"Kahwa" (also spelled kehwa, qahwa or qehwa) is a word that is related to the Arabic word for coffee, and in different regions it can refer to different drinks. In Kashmir, kahwa is a green tea drink that is served in celebrations and everyday life. You can find a short background on the [Kahwa page on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kahwa).

A traditional recipe is cooked in a metal kettle called a samovar, with tea leaves, saffron, spices and sometimes dried fruits. It is served in small cups, often with almonds floating or placed on top. Recipes vary by household and season, and no single version is the only authentic one.

## What does kahwa taste like?

Kahwa is delicate and aromatic:

- **Saffron:** floral, honey-like and golden in color.
- **Cardamom:** sweet, citrusy and warm.
- **Cinnamon:** gentle warmth.
- **Green tea:** light and grassy, a clean base.
- **Almonds:** a subtle nutty richness.
- **Sweetener:** honey or sugar rounds it out.

Compared with chai, kahwa is lighter in body and more perfumed because there is no milk.

## Kahwa vs masala chai

| Feature | Kahwa | Masala chai |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tea base | Typically green tea | Typically strong black tea |
| Milk | Usually none | Usually yes |
| Signature spice | Saffron, cardamom, cinnamon | Cardamom, ginger, cloves, pepper, cinnamon |
| Body | Light | Rich and creamy |
| Preparation | Simmer spices, steep tea | Simmer tea, spices and milk together |
| Garnish | Chopped almonds | None |
| Flavor | Floral, delicate, sweet | Bold, spicy, malty |

## Simple kahwa recipe (serves 2)

### Ingredients

- 2 cups (480 ml) water
- 2 teaspoons loose green tea, or 2 green tea bags
- 1 cinnamon stick (about 2 inches)
- 3 green cardamom pods, lightly crushed
- A pinch of saffron threads (about 6 to 10 strands)
- 1 to 2 teaspoons honey or sugar, to taste
- A few chopped or sliced almonds
- Optional: 2 whole cloves, a small piece of ginger or a few dried rose petals

### Instructions

1. **Simmer the spices.** Combine water, cinnamon and cardamom (and cloves or ginger if using) in a saucepan. Bring to a boil, lower the heat and simmer for 5 minutes.
2. **Add saffron.** Crush the saffron between your fingers or steep it in a spoon of warm water first. Add it to the pan and let it infuse for 1 to 2 minutes.
3. **Steep the tea.** Take the pan off the heat and let the water cool for about a minute, so it does not scorch the tea. Add the green tea and steep for 2 minutes. This follows the general rule for green tea described in [water temperature for green tea](/guides/water-temperature-for-green-tea/).
4. **Strain and sweeten.** Strain into small cups and stir in honey or sugar.
5. **Garnish.** Top with chopped almonds.

For other measuring guidance, see [how much tea per cup](/guides/how-much-tea-per-cup/).

## Tips for better kahwa

- **Use real saffron.** A small pinch goes a long way. Store saffron in a dark, airtight container.
- **Do not boil the tea.** Boiling green tea can make it bitter. Add it after the spices have simmered.
- **Crush the spices lightly.** This releases oils without clouding the tea.
- **Use whole spices.** They are cleaner and more aromatic than ground spices.
- **Adjust sweetness.** Traditional kahwa is mildly sweet. Taste before adding more.

## Variations

- **Kahwa with milk or cream:** some people add a splash of milk for a softer taste.
- **Kahwa with dried fruit:** add a few dried apricots or raisins to the pan.
- **Kahwa with rose petals:** adds a floral touch.
- **Iced kahwa:** brew slightly stronger, chill and serve over ice. See [how to make iced tea](/guides/how-to-make-iced-tea/) for chilling methods.
- **Caffeine-free kahwa:** simmer the spices and saffron without tea leaves.

## Is kahwa caffeinated?

With green tea leaves in the recipe, kahwa contains caffeine. Green tea generally has less caffeine than brewed coffee, but the amount depends on how much tea you use and how long you steep it. For general comparisons, see [caffeine in tea vs coffee](/guides/how-much-caffeine-in-tea-vs-coffee/). If you prefer to avoid caffeine, make the spice-only version or try other [caffeine-free teas](/guides/caffeine-free-tea-list/).

## Is kahwa good for you?

Kahwa contains green tea and spices, which are commonly consumed in food and drinks. Beyond that, be careful with claims. Articles online sometimes promise that kahwa cures or prevents specific conditions, and these statements are not supported by strong evidence. Treat kahwa as a flavorful drink, not a treatment, and talk with a healthcare professional about your health.

## Where to buy kahwa

Kahwa is sold as ready-made blends of green tea with saffron, cardamom, cinnamon and almonds, usually in loose leaf or tea bags. When buying:

- Read the ingredients: you want tea, saffron and spices, not just flavoring.
- Check for freshness. Spices fade over time.
- Compare the price per serving, since saffron raises the cost.
- Store it airtight and away from light, as in our guide on [how to store loose leaf tea](/guides/how-to-store-loose-leaf-tea/).

## Serving suggestions

Serve kahwa in small cups after a meal, with dry fruits, almond biscuits, or light pastries. It also works as a warming drink on a cold day.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Better approach |
| --- | --- |
| Boiling the tea leaves | Add green tea off the heat |
| Using too much saffron | A pinch is enough |
| Using ground spices that are old | Use whole, fresh spices |
| Skipping the straining step | Strain for a clear cup |
| Expecting a milky chai | Kahwa is light and clear |

## Serving kahwa traditionally

Kahwa is often served in small cups, and the almonds are part of the presentation. A few chopped almonds float on top, and sometimes a pinch of saffron or a few dried rose petals sits in the cup. It is common to serve kahwa with light baked goods, nuts and dried fruit. It is sipped slowly, and refills are typical. If you want to serve it for guests, make a larger batch in a teapot and keep it warm, but do not keep the tea leaves steeping, since green tea turns bitter when it sits too long.

## Choosing your ingredients

The ingredients are simple, so quality matters. Choose a decent green tea, since the base shows through. A basic Chinese green tea such as gunpowder or a Japanese sencha both work, though they taste different. Use real saffron threads rather than coloring, and store them in a sealed container away from light. Buy whole cardamom pods and cinnamon sticks, and crush them as you need them. Using fresh spices makes a visible difference to aroma. If you cannot find all the ingredients, a pinch of saffron with cardamom and green tea already captures the spirit of the drink.

## Using a samovar or a saucepan

Traditionally, kahwa is prepared in a samovar, which keeps water hot for hours, and the spices and tea are placed in the central chamber. At home, a saucepan is perfectly fine. If you have a thermos, you can also steep spices and tea in a thermos for a short time, though be careful to strain the tea, since leaving green tea in hot water for long periods makes it bitter. For general advice on brewing temperature and timing, see [water temperature for green tea](/guides/water-temperature-for-green-tea/).
