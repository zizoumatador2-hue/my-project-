Herbal tea is one of the easiest drinks to make at home. There are no leaves to measure with precision, no delicate temperature range to protect, and very little to clean up afterward. You simply pour hot water over herbs, cover the cup and wait. The result can be floral, minty, citrusy or earthy, depending on what you choose.

This guide explains how to make herbal tea with dried or fresh herbs, how long to steep different ingredients, how to blend your own cups, and what to keep in mind for safety. It is general information, not medical advice.

## What counts as herbal tea?

Herbal tea, also called a tisane or herbal infusion, is made from plants other than the tea plant, Camellia sinensis. Common ingredients include flowers such as chamomile and hibiscus, leaves such as peppermint and lemon balm, fruits such as rosehips, and roots or barks such as ginger or cinnamon. Rooibos, which comes from a South African shrub, is also grouped with herbal teas even though it is not an herb in the same sense.

Because these plants do not contain the caffeine found in black and green tea, most herbal infusions are naturally caffeine-free. The exception is any blend that includes true tea. Always read the ingredient list.

## The basic method

You need only three things: dried or fresh herbs, boiling water and a covered cup or pot. Here is the basic method for one 8 oz (240 ml) cup:

1. Put one to two teaspoons of dried herbs in a mug, infuser or teapot.
2. Pour in water that has just boiled.
3. Cover the cup with a saucer or lid to keep the aroma in.
4. Steep for five to ten minutes, depending on the herb.
5. Strain the herbs out and drink while warm.

Covering matters more than people expect. Heat and aroma escape quickly from an open cup, and a covered steep gives a fuller flavor, especially with floral herbs.

## Dried or fresh?

Dried herbs are concentrated and keep for months, which makes them the easiest choice for regular brewing. Use about one to two teaspoons per cup. Some dried flowers, such as chamomile, look light and fluffy, so a heaping spoonful is normal.

Fresh herbs are milder and more fragrant, and they are best when you have a garden or a good market nearby. A rough guide is a small handful of leaves per cup, or about two to three times the amount of dried herb. Crush or tear the leaves lightly before steeping so they release their oils.

Taste as you go. A fresh herb can be very different from one harvest to the next, so trust your cup more than a fixed measurement.

## Steeping times by herb

Different herbs need different times. Start with the lower number and taste early:

- **Chamomile:** five to seven minutes. Longer steeping can make it taste bitter or grassy.
- **Peppermint:** five minutes. Steeping longer makes the flavor sharp and can overwhelm a cup.
- **Lemon balm:** five to seven minutes. It has a light citrus note that fades if you steep it too long.
- **Hibiscus:** five minutes for a tart, bright cup. Longer steeping makes it sour.
- **Rooibos:** five to ten minutes, with boiling water. It has a sweet, nutty flavor that deepens with time.
- **Ginger slices:** eight to ten minutes. Simmer slices in water for a few minutes first if you want a stronger, spicier cup.
- **Rosehips:** eight to ten minutes. They give a fruity, tangy base.

Roots, barks and seeds generally need longer steeping or a brief simmer. Flowers and soft leaves need less time. For a stronger cup, use more herb rather than steeping for much longer.

## Blending your own cups

Herbal tea is a fun place to experiment. Start with a base herb that you like, add one or two accents and balance the flavors. A common approach uses a base of mild chamomile or rooibos, a bright note such as lemon balm or peppermint, and a small amount of something warm, like cinnamon or ginger.

Keep the blend simple at first. Three ingredients is plenty. Measure the mix by volume, and write down what you used so you can repeat it. If one herb dominates, reduce it in the next batch.

For a calm evening cup, many people choose chamomile with a little lemon balm. Our guide on [the best tea for sleep](/guides/best-tea-for-sleep/) discusses common evening herbs and how to build a routine around them, but it does not promise any particular effect.

## Water and equipment

Use fresh, filtered water if your tap water has a strong taste, since the herbs are subtle and the water shows through. Use a kettle that has just reached a full boil. If you prefer a thermometer, herbal teas are usually poured at 212°F (100°C), but a few delicate flowers taste better slightly cooler.

An infuser or a teapot with a built-in filter keeps the herbs from ending up in your cup. Our guide on [how to brew loose leaf tea](/guides/how-to-brew-loose-leaf-tea/) explains the same ideas for true tea, and many of those tools work well with herbs.

## Storage

Herbs lose aroma when exposed to light, heat, air and moisture. Store dried herbs in airtight containers in a cool, dark cupboard. Label each jar with the name and date. Most dried herbs are best within about a year, while fresh herbs are best used within a few days or frozen.

If the herbs smell dull or musty, they are past their best and will make a flat cup. Replacing them is cheaper than drinking a disappointing tea.

## Safety and who should be careful

Herbal teas are not all equally suitable for everyone. Some herbs can cause allergic reactions, especially in people sensitive to plants in the daisy family, such as chamomile, or the mint family, such as peppermint and lemon balm. Some herbs can interact with medications, including sedatives and blood thinners. Some are not recommended during pregnancy or breastfeeding, and some are not suitable for young children.

If you are pregnant, nursing, taking prescription medication or managing a health condition, talk with a healthcare professional before drinking herbal tea regularly. Be careful with commercial blends that do not list their ingredients clearly. We do not make health claims about any herb in this guide. ## Common questions people ask

Many people wonder whether herbal tea can be strong. Yes, but strength comes from using more herb, not from steeping for much longer. Another common question is whether you can reuse the herbs. Some can be steeped a second time, usually with a little less flavor, while flowers and soft leaves often fade quickly.

People also ask whether they can sweeten herbal tea. You can, with honey or a small amount of sugar. Floral and mint teas often taste good without it, so taste first.

## A simple weekly routine

If you want to make herbal tea a habit, pick one or two blends and brew them at the same time each day. For example, drink a bright mint or hibiscus tea in the afternoon and a mild chamomile blend after dinner. Keep a few jars ready so you do not have to think about it.

Over time you will learn which herbs you enjoy and how strong you like them. That is the real skill of herbal tea: knowing your own taste.
