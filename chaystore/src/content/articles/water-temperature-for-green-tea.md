The best water temperature for green tea is about 160–185°F (70–85°C), which is noticeably cooler than boiling. Pouring boiling water directly on most green teas extracts too many bitter, astringent compounds too quickly, so the cup tastes harsh instead of sweet and grassy. Cooler water, a short steep and a modest amount of leaf are the three levers that fix most bad cups.

## Why green tea needs cooler water

Green tea is made from leaves that are heated soon after picking (steamed in Japan, often pan-fired in China) to stop oxidation. That keeps the leaf green and preserves its delicate flavor compounds. The same fresh, unoxidized character makes green tea sensitive to heat.

Hot water pulls out catechins and other compounds that taste bitter and astringent, and it does so faster than it extracts the sweeter amino acids. If you lower the water temperature a little, you extract the flavors you want first and avoid the harshness. A higher-quality, more delicate tea usually benefits more from careful temperature control than a sturdy, roasted one.

## Green tea temperature chart

Use this table as a starting point and check the package for the producer's advice.

| Green tea | Water temperature | Steeping time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gyokuro (shade-grown Japanese) | 140–160°F (60–70°C) | 1–2 minutes |
| Sencha (steamed Japanese) | 160–175°F (70–80°C) | 1–2 minutes |
| Kabusecha, shincha | 160–170°F (70–77°C) | 1–2 minutes |
| Genmaicha (with toasted rice) | 175–185°F (80–85°C) | 2–3 minutes |
| Hojicha (roasted) | 195–205°F (90–96°C) | 30–60 seconds up to 2 minutes |
| Chinese pan-fired (such as Dragon Well, or Longjing) | 170–180°F (77–82°C) | 2–3 minutes |
| Gunpowder | 175–185°F (80–85°C) | 2–3 minutes |
| Jasmine green | 175–185°F (80–85°C) | 2–3 minutes |

Matcha is a powdered green tea prepared differently; see our guide on [matcha vs green tea](/guides/matcha-vs-green-tea/). Roasted teas like hojicha tolerate hotter water because roasting changes their flavor structure.

## How to get the right temperature without a thermometer

You do not need special equipment to hit the target.

### Option 1: Let boiled water rest

After the water boils, take the lid off the kettle and wait. As a rough guide:

- About 1 minute: close to 200°F (93°C).
- About 2 to 3 minutes: close to 175–185°F (80–85°C).
- About 4 to 5 minutes: close to 160–170°F (70–77°C).

The exact cooling time depends on your kettle, the volume of water and the room temperature.

### Option 2: Pour between vessels

Pour boiled water into a second cup or pitcher and then onto the tea. Each transfer lowers the temperature by several degrees.

### Option 3: Add cold water

If you pour boiled water into a cup and add a splash of cool water, you bring it down fast. This is imprecise but practical.

### Option 4: Use a variable-temperature kettle

If you drink green tea or other delicate teas often, a kettle with temperature presets takes the guesswork away. We explain what to look for in our guide to the [best electric kettle for tea](/guides/best-electric-kettle-for-tea/).

## How long to steep green tea

Time works together with temperature. The warmer the water, the shorter the steep. A reliable pattern is:

1. Start with about 2 minutes at 175°F (80°C).
2. Taste. If the tea is too light, steep a little longer next time. If it is bitter or dry, cool the water or shorten the time.
3. For a second infusion, use a similar temperature and add about 30 seconds.

High-quality green tea can often be steeped two or three times. Small leaves release flavor quickly, so keep a timer nearby.

## Fixing bitter green tea

If your green tea tastes bitter, try the following in order:

| Problem | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| Water was boiling | Cool water to 160–185°F |
| Steeped over 3 minutes | Shorten to 1–2 minutes |
| Too many leaves | Use about 1 teaspoon per 8 oz cup |
| Tea is old | Replace it and store it airtight; see [how to store loose leaf tea](/guides/how-to-store-loose-leaf-tea/) |
| Cheap or very fine tea | Use cooler water and shorter time, or choose a better leaf |

## Does boiling water ruin green tea?

Boiling water will not harm you, and plenty of people drink green tea made with hot water. It simply tends to taste more bitter and less sweet. Some sturdy or roasted green teas, and many tea bags, are designed to take hotter water. If you enjoy the stronger taste, there is nothing wrong with it. If you find green tea unpleasant, cooler water is the first thing to try.

## Water quality matters too

Tea is mostly water, so the water you use affects the cup. Fresh, cold water that has not been boiled repeatedly tastes best because re-boiling reduces dissolved oxygen. If your tap water has a strong flavor from chlorine or minerals, filtered water usually gives a cleaner, brighter cup.

## Cold brewing green tea

If you want to skip temperature control entirely, cold brewing is forgiving. Combine about 1 teaspoon of leaf per 8 oz of cold water, refrigerate for 6 to 12 hours, then strain. Cold-brewed green tea tends to taste smoother and sweeter. We cover similar methods in our guide to [how to make iced tea](/guides/how-to-make-iced-tea/).

## Water temperature for other kinds of green tea products

### Green tea bags

Green tea bags usually contain small, broken leaf, which extracts quickly. Many bag makers recommend hot water, but the same bitterness rules apply: if your bagged green tea tastes harsh, let the water cool for a minute or two and steep for 1 to 2 minutes instead of 3 to 5. The leaf size difference is one reason the comparison in [loose leaf tea vs tea bags](/guides/loose-leaf-tea-vs-tea-bags/) matters.

### Matcha

Matcha is whisked rather than steeped, and the usual advice is about 175°F (80°C) water. Cooler water gives a sweeter, less bitter bowl, and hotter water brings out bitterness. If you are making a latte, see [how to make a matcha latte](/guides/how-to-make-matcha-latte/).

### Genmaicha, hojicha and roasted teas

Roasted green teas have a different flavor structure and can handle hotter water without turning harsh. Hojicha in particular is often brewed with water near boiling and tastes toasty and smooth.

## What temperature does for flavor: a simple experiment

If you want to taste the effect of temperature for yourself, try this experiment with a decent sencha or Chinese pan-fired green tea. Brew three small cups of the same tea with the same amount of leaf for the same time (1 minute), using water at about 160°F (70°C), 175°F (80°C) and 195°F (90°C). Taste them in order. The cooler cup is usually sweeter and more savory, the middle cup is balanced, and the hottest cup is bolder but more bitter and astringent. Note which you prefer and use that as your own default. Individual taste matters more than any chart.

## Altitude and your kettle

If you live at high altitude, water boils at a lower temperature than at sea level, so "boiling" water may only be about 200°F (93°C) or lower. This matters for black and herbal teas, which like hotter water, and means the rest time for green tea is shorter. A thermometer or a variable-temperature kettle, as discussed in our guide to the [best electric kettle for tea](/guides/best-electric-kettle-for-tea/), removes the guesswork.

## Quick checklist

- Start at 175°F (80°C) for most green teas.
- Steep 2 to 3 minutes and taste early.
- Use about 1 teaspoon per 8 oz.
- If it tastes bitter, lower the temperature first.
- Use fresh, filtered water and fresh tea.
