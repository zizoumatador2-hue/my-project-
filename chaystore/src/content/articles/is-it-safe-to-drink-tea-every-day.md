For most healthy adults, drinking tea every day is considered safe and can fit into a balanced diet. The main things to watch are your total caffeine from all sources, how your body responds, whether you take medications or have a medical condition, and how hot the tea is when you drink it. If you are pregnant or have a health concern, ask a healthcare professional what is right for you.

This article is general information only and is not medical advice.

## What "safe" depends on

Tea is a widely consumed drink around the world. Whether daily tea is right for you depends on several factors:

- The type of tea (caffeinated or herbal).
- How much you drink and how strong it is.
- Your sensitivity to caffeine.
- Your health, age and medications.
- When you drink it and how hot it is.

If you want an overview of tea types before reading more, see our [complete guide to tea](/#tea-guide).

## Caffeine: the main consideration

Black, green, white, oolong and matcha contain caffeine. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration notes that about 400 mg of caffeine a day is not generally associated with dangerous, negative effects in most healthy adults, as explained in its article [Spilling the Beans: How Much Caffeine Is Too Much?](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much). That total includes coffee, soda, energy drinks, chocolate and some medications, not just tea.

Because tea usually has less caffeine per cup than coffee, most people can drink several cups without reaching that level. Our guide to [caffeine in tea vs coffee](/guides/how-much-caffeine-in-tea-vs-coffee/) gives typical amounts.

But sensitivity differs. If tea makes you jittery, anxious or unable to sleep, you may want to reduce the amount, switch to lower-caffeine teas or move your tea earlier in the day. See [best tea for sleep](/guides/best-tea-for-sleep/) for evening alternatives.

## Possible downsides of daily tea

### Sleep disruption

Caffeine in the afternoon or evening can make it harder to fall asleep. Switching to a [caffeine-free tea](/guides/caffeine-free-tea-list/) later in the day is a simple fix.

### Stomach upset

Strong tea, especially on an empty stomach, can cause nausea or discomfort in some people. Try drinking it with food, steeping it for a shorter time, or choosing a gentler tea.

### Reduced iron absorption

Compounds in tea called tannins can bind to non-heme iron (from plants and supplements) and reduce how much your body absorbs. If you have low iron, are pregnant, follow a vegetarian or vegan diet, or are at risk of anemia, consider drinking tea between meals rather than with them, and ask a healthcare professional for advice. The effect depends on the person and the diet.

### Very hot drinks

Public health bodies have noted that drinking very hot beverages, around 150°F (65°C) or hotter, may irritate the throat and has been associated with higher risks of throat cancer in some studies. The simple precaution is to let your tea cool a little before sipping. It does not mean warm tea is harmful.

### Teeth

Tea can stain teeth over time. Rinsing your mouth with water after drinking and keeping up regular dental care can help. Sugar and sweet syrups in tea also affect dental health.

### Oxalates and kidney stones

Tea, especially black tea, contains oxalates. People who have had kidney stones, or who have been advised to limit oxalate, should ask their clinician how much tea is suitable.

### Medication interactions

Caffeine and plant compounds in tea can interact with some medications, including certain heart, blood-pressure, psychiatric and blood-thinning medicines, and some supplements. If you take prescription drugs, ask your pharmacist or doctor whether your tea habit is a concern.

## Herbal teas: do they have different considerations?

Herbal teas do not contain tea leaf or caffeine unless blended with it, but they are made from plants with active compounds. Some are generally mild, while others can have effects or interact with medications. Some herbs are not recommended in pregnancy or for certain conditions. Drinking one very large amount of a single herb every day for a long time may not be wise. Variety and moderation are sensible, and a professional can advise on specific herbs. Read more in our [caffeine-free tea list](/guides/caffeine-free-tea-list/) and [tea for digestion](/guides/tea-for-digestion/).

## Who should be extra careful?

Check with a healthcare professional about daily tea if you:

- Are pregnant, trying to become pregnant or breastfeeding.
- Have anxiety, heart rhythm problems, high blood pressure, reflux, insomnia or an iron deficiency.
- Have a history of kidney stones.
- Take prescription medications or supplements.
- Are giving tea to a child.
- Are drinking large amounts, such as many cups a day, or very strong tea.

## How to enjoy tea daily in a sensible way

1. **Pay attention to total caffeine** from tea, coffee, soda and other sources.
2. **Spread it out.** Have tea earlier in the day, and choose herbal in the evening.
3. **Drink between meals** if iron absorption is a concern.
4. **Let it cool slightly** before sipping.
5. **Use moderate strength.** Over-steeping increases tannins and bitterness. See [how to brew loose leaf tea](/guides/how-to-brew-loose-leaf-tea/).
6. **Watch added sugar.** Sweetened tea adds calories and affects teeth.
7. **Choose quality tea.** Reputable sources and proper storage reduce the chance of off flavors or contaminants. See [how to store loose leaf tea](/guides/how-to-store-loose-leaf-tea/).
8. **Drink water too.**
9. **Listen to your body.** If a tea consistently gives you symptoms, cut back.

## Is there a "best" tea to drink every day?

Not for everyone. A black tea may suit your morning, a green tea your afternoon, and an herbal tea your evening. Variety is fine. Let your preferences, tolerance and health guide you. If you want to explore, compare options in our guides on [matcha vs green tea](/guides/matcha-vs-green-tea/) and [Assam vs Darjeeling tea](/guides/assam-vs-darjeeling-tea/).

## What about health claims?

You will see claims online that tea prevents or cures specific diseases. Research on tea and health exists, but findings are often observational or mixed, and no tea is a substitute for medical care. Be skeptical of strong promises. For balanced information on green tea, see the [National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/green-tea).

## A balanced daily tea plan

Here is an example of a moderate daily plan for a healthy adult who enjoys tea. In the morning, a cup or two of black tea or chai with breakfast. Mid-morning or early afternoon, a cup of green tea or oolong, enjoyed between meals. In the late afternoon, a cup of white or herbal tea. In the evening, a caffeine-free herbal tea. That adds up to a few cups of caffeinated tea, which for most people is well within typical caffeine guidance, while leaving room for sleep and for iron-rich meals without tea alongside. Adjust the plan to your own sensitivity and health.

## Signs it is time to cut back

Pay attention to your body. Common signals that tea may not be agreeing with you include trouble falling asleep, a racing heart, restlessness, frequent headaches, stomach discomfort, or feeling dependent on tea to function. If you notice these, reduce your intake gradually, shift to lower-caffeine or herbal options, and consider speaking with a healthcare professional. Stopping suddenly after heavy daily caffeine use can cause withdrawal headaches, so taper over a week or two.

## Tea habits for specific groups

Older adults may be more sensitive to caffeine and may take medications that interact with it. People with reflux may do better with lower-acid or non-mint teas. Athletes may use tea for hydration and light caffeine, but should watch total intake. People who are trying to increase iron intake should keep tea between meals. Children and teens should limit caffeine, and herbal teas for children need professional guidance. These are general notes, and a clinician can tailor advice to your situation. ## Summary

A daily cup or several of tea is a normal, enjoyable habit for most healthy adults. Keep an eye on caffeine, iron needs, medications and the temperature of your drink, and ask a professional if you have a health condition or are pregnant.
