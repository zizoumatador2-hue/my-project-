Naturally caffeine-free teas include rooibos, chamomile, peppermint, hibiscus, ginger, lemon balm, fennel and many fruit blends. These are technically herbal infusions (also called tisanes) because they are not made from the tea plant. Real tea, meaning black, green, white, oolong and pu-erh, always contains some caffeine, even when decaffeinated.

This guide gives you a practical caffeine-free tea list, explains the decaf-versus-caffeine-free difference, and offers tips for choosing and brewing.

## True tea vs herbal tea

Tea in the strict sense comes from the leaves of *Camellia sinensis*. Black, green, white, oolong and dark teas all come from this plant, and all contain caffeine. You can learn more about tea types in our [complete guide to tea](/#tea-guide).

Herbal teas are infusions of other plants, such as dried flowers, herbs, spices, roots or fruit. The [herbal tea entry on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbal_tea) offers a helpful overview. Most are naturally caffeine-free, though there are a few exceptions, covered below.

## Decaf vs caffeine-free: what is the difference?

These terms are not interchangeable.

| Term | Meaning | Caffeine content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Caffeine-free | The plant used does not contain caffeine, such as chamomile or rooibos | None |
| Decaffeinated | Tea leaves from the tea plant that have been processed to remove most of the caffeine | Small amount remains |
| Regular tea | Black, green, white, oolong | Contains caffeine |

A decaf black or green tea typically still contains a small amount of caffeine, so if you are strictly avoiding it, choose a naturally caffeine-free herbal tea instead. For typical caffeine numbers in different drinks, see [caffeine in tea vs coffee](/guides/how-much-caffeine-in-tea-vs-coffee/).

## Watch out for hidden caffeine

Some herbal blends include ingredients that are not caffeine-free:

- **Yerba mate** (a South American holly plant) contains caffeine.
- **Guayusa** also contains caffeine.
- **Guarana** is a caffeine source sometimes added to "energy" blends.
- **Green or black tea** may be added to flavored herbal blends. Chai blends, for example, typically contain black tea unless labeled rooibos chai.
- **Kola nut** contains caffeine.

Read the ingredients list rather than relying on the product name. If the label lists "tea" or "green tea," it contains caffeine.

## How to brew caffeine-free teas

Herbal teas are forgiving because they do not turn bitter in the same way as black or green tea, but they still benefit from the right method.

- **Water temperature:** boiling, about 212°F (100°C).
- **Amount:** 1 to 2 teaspoons per 8 oz cup. Bulky flowers need more.
- **Steeping time:** 5 to 7 minutes. Cover the cup to keep volatile oils, especially in mint and chamomile, from escaping.
- **Re-steeping:** often possible, but flavor usually fades faster than with true tea.

For a full method, see [how to brew loose leaf tea](/guides/how-to-brew-loose-leaf-tea/). For measurements, read [how much tea per cup](/guides/how-much-tea-per-cup/).

## Iced herbal tea

Many caffeine-free teas make excellent iced tea. Hibiscus, mint, rooibos and fruit blends are especially popular. Brew double strength and chill, or cold brew for 6 to 12 hours. See [how to make iced tea](/guides/how-to-make-iced-tea/) for step-by-step methods.

## Chai without caffeine

You can make a chai-style drink with rooibos instead of black tea. Simmer rooibos with cardamom, cinnamon, ginger and cloves, and add milk if you like. Use the approach in our [chai recipe](/guides/how-to-make-chai-at-home/), swapping the tea.

## Safety and precautions

Caffeine-free does not always mean risk-free. Herbs are plants with active compounds, and some people should be careful with them.

- Some herbs may interact with medications.
- Certain herbs are not recommended during pregnancy or breastfeeding.
- People with allergies to related plants (for example, ragweed and chamomile, both in the daisy family) may react.
- Large amounts of some herbal teas are not advisable.

For general guidance on caffeine and health, see the [Mayo Clinic's caffeine overview](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/nutrition-and-healthy-eating/in-depth/caffeine/art-20045678). Always speak to a healthcare professional about herbal products if you have a medical condition, take medicines, are pregnant or are nursing.

## Buying and storing herbal tea

- Look for clearly labeled ingredients and, ideally, the plant parts (for example, "peppermint leaves").
- Choose a recent date, because dried herbs lose aroma.
- Store in an airtight, opaque container in a cool, dry place. See [how to store loose leaf tea](/guides/how-to-store-loose-leaf-tea/).
- If you prefer, buy whole herbs and blend your own.

## Quick answers

- **Best naturally sweet caffeine-free tea:** rooibos or honeybush.
- **Best for iced tea:** hibiscus or fruit blends.
- **Best mint:** peppermint (strong) or spearmint (gentle).
- **Best evening cup:** chamomile or lemon balm.

## Matching caffeine-free teas to moods and moments

If you are replacing your coffee or black tea habit, think about what you actually want from the cup. If it is warmth and ritual, any hot herbal infusion works. If it is a bold, comforting flavor, rooibos or a spiced rooibos chai gives body without caffeine. If it is something sweet, honeybush or fruit blends scratch the itch. If you want something refreshing for summer, cold-brewed hibiscus or mint is hard to beat. Making a short list of three or four favorites keeps your cupboard manageable.

## Making your own herbal blends

Blending is easier than it sounds. Start with a base herb that has body, such as rooibos, honeybush or lemon balm (about 50 percent), add a flavor herb such as peppermint, ginger or hibiscus (about 30 percent) and finish with a small accent such as lavender, rose petals or orange peel (about 20 percent). Mix a small batch, brew a cup, and adjust. Keep notes on ratios, and store blends in airtight tins. See [how to store loose leaf tea](/guides/how-to-store-loose-leaf-tea/) for storage advice that applies to herbs as well.

## Buying quality herbal tea

Quality herbal tea is made of recognizable pieces: whole flowers, leaves, seeds and chunks of root or peel. A heavy dusty powder in a tea bag is more likely to taste flat. Smell the dry herbs if you can. They should smell fresh, not musty. Check the ingredients for sweeteners or flavoring you do not want, and look for a date. If you drink herbal tea every day, vary your choices rather than relying on one herb in large amounts.
