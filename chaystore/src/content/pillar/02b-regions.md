## Tea Around the World: Major Regions and Their Signature Teas

Knowing where a tea comes from tells you a lot about what to expect in the cup. Here is a tour of the main tea regions and what each is known for. Treat these as tendencies rather than rules, because every garden, season and producer is different.

### China

China is the birthplace of tea culture and produces every category: green, white, yellow, oolong, black and dark teas. Regions are famous for particular styles.

- **Zhejiang:** home of Dragon Well (Longjing), a flat, pan-fired green tea with a toasty, chestnut sweetness.
- **Fujian:** known for white teas, jasmine-scented green teas and many oolongs, as well as smoky and sweet black teas.
- **Anhui:** produces Keemun, a smooth black tea sometimes described as winey or cocoa-like, as well as some well-known green teas.
- **Yunnan:** the source of pu-erh and rich, sweet black teas, grown in a region with ancient tea trees.
- **Guangdong:** known for roasted oolongs.

Chinese teas are often brewed gongfu-style, with a lot of leaf in a small vessel and many short infusions. Scented teas such as [jasmine green tea](/guides/jasmine-tea-vs-green-tea/) are a Chinese specialty.

### Japan

Japan is best known for green tea, mostly steamed rather than pan-fired, which gives it a fresh, vegetal, savory character.

- **Sencha:** the everyday green tea, sun-grown and bright.
- **Gyokuro:** shade-grown before harvest for a sweet, umami-rich cup.
- **Matcha:** shade-grown leaf ground into powder; see [matcha vs green tea](/guides/matcha-vs-green-tea/).
- **Genmaicha:** green tea blended with toasted rice.
- **Hojicha:** roasted green tea with a toasty, low-bitterness taste.
- **Kukicha:** made from stems and twigs, mild and slightly sweet.

Famous growing areas include Uji near Kyoto, Shizuoka and Kagoshima. Because Japanese greens are delicate, they benefit from cooler water, as explained in [water temperature for green tea](/guides/water-temperature-for-green-tea/).

### India

India is one of the world's largest tea producers, and its three most famous regions differ widely.

- **Assam:** warm, humid lowlands in the northeast produce bold, malty black teas. These form the backbone of many breakfast blends and masala chai.
- **Darjeeling:** high-altitude estates in West Bengal produce lighter, floral and muscatel-tinged teas, available in distinct seasonal flushes.
- **Nilgiri:** the Blue Mountains of the south yield bright, fragrant teas, well suited to iced tea.

For a direct comparison of the two best-known regions, see [Assam vs Darjeeling tea](/guides/assam-vs-darjeeling-tea/). India is also the home of masala chai culture; try [making chai at home](/guides/how-to-make-chai-at-home/). In the north, Kashmir has its own tradition of kahwa, covered in [what is kahwa tea](/guides/what-is-kahwa-tea/).

### Sri Lanka

Formerly Ceylon, Sri Lanka produces brisk, bright black teas, often with citrus or crisp notes. Regional differences by elevation are a big part of the story: high-grown teas from the central highlands tend to be lighter and more aromatic, while low-grown teas are darker and fuller. Ceylon tea is often used in blends and is a classic choice for iced tea.

### Taiwan

Taiwan is celebrated for oolong teas, including lightly oxidized, floral high-mountain oolongs and deeply roasted styles. Taiwanese tea culture also gave the world bubble tea. High-mountain teas are grown at high altitude and prized for their complex aroma.

### Kenya and East Africa

Kenya is one of the world's largest exporters of black tea, much of it processed as CTC for strong, quick-brewing cups. Many mainstream tea bag blends contain African teas.

### Turkey, Russia and Central Asia

Black tea is a daily staple here. Turkish tea (*çay*) is brewed strong in a double teapot and served in small glasses, while Russian-style tea is often brewed as a concentrate in a samovar and diluted with hot water. These traditions are a reminder that "chay" is a shared word and a shared habit.

### North Africa

Moroccan mint tea combines gunpowder green tea, fresh mint and plenty of sugar, poured from a height to create foam. It is a symbol of hospitality.

### The United States

The U.S. has a long tradition of iced tea, especially sweet tea in the South. There is also a growing specialty-tea scene, with small U.S. importers, cafés and a handful of domestic tea farms. If you enjoy cold tea, read [how to make iced tea](/guides/how-to-make-iced-tea/).

## What Seasonal Harvests Mean

Many teas are harvested in distinct seasons, called flushes. Spring harvests are often prized for tenderness and sweetness. Summer harvests tend to be stronger. Autumn teas are often mellow. In Darjeeling, "first flush" and "second flush" teas taste noticeably different. Japan's first-harvest teas, such as shincha, are celebrated for freshness. Because freshness matters, check harvest dates when buying, and store tea as described in [how to store loose leaf tea](/guides/how-to-store-loose-leaf-tea/).
