## The Types of Tea Explained

The traditional classification groups true tea into six families, based mainly on processing and degree of oxidation. This is the foundation for understanding any tea you meet.

| Type | Oxidation | Typical flavor | Typical water temperature | Caffeine tendency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| White | Minimal | Delicate, sweet, floral, hay-like | 175–185°F (80–85°C) | Lower to moderate |
| Green | None (stopped by heat) | Fresh, grassy, nutty, marine | 160–185°F (70–85°C) | Lower to moderate |
| Yellow | Slight, with a special "yellowing" step | Mellow, smooth, sweet | 175–185°F (80–85°C) | Lower to moderate |
| Oolong | Partial (roughly 10–80%) | Floral to roasted, creamy to toasty | 185–205°F (85–96°C) | Moderate |
| Black | Full | Malty, fruity, brisk, rich | 200–212°F (93–100°C) | Moderate to higher |
| Dark (post-fermented, such as pu-erh) | Microbial aging | Earthy, woody, smooth | 195–212°F (90–100°C) | Moderate |

These ranges are general guidelines. Producers know their tea best, so check the package.

### White tea

White tea is the least processed of the true teas. It is made from young buds and leaves that are withered and dried with little else done. The result is a pale, delicate cup with subtle sweetness and floral or hay-like notes. Because the leaves are fluffy, you often need more teaspoons per cup than for other teas; see [how much tea per cup](/guides/how-much-tea-per-cup/). White tea suits cooler water and short steeps, and it is a lovely tea for iced brewing.

### Green tea

Green tea is processed quickly after picking, using heat to stop oxidation. Japan typically steams its leaves, giving a bright, vegetal, savory flavor. China often pan-fires, giving toasty, nutty and sweet notes. Because green tea is delicate, it turns bitter easily if brewed with boiling water. Our guide to [water temperature for green tea](/guides/water-temperature-for-green-tea/) explains the details, and the family includes sencha, gyokuro, Dragon Well, gunpowder, genmaicha and many others.

**Matcha** is a special case: shade-grown green tea ground into a fine powder and consumed whole. It is richer, more intense and generally higher in caffeine per serving than steeped green tea. Read [matcha vs green tea](/guides/matcha-vs-green-tea/), then follow our recipe for a [matcha latte](/guides/how-to-make-matcha-latte/), and use [best matcha for beginners](/guides/best-matcha-for-beginners/) when you are ready to buy.

### Yellow tea

Yellow tea is rare. It resembles green tea but includes a slow, gentle "sealed yellowing" step that softens grassy notes and gives a mellow, smooth flavor. It is produced in small quantities, mostly in China, and can be hard to find outside specialty shops.

### Oolong tea

Oolong sits between green and black. Leaves are partially oxidized, often bruised, then rolled and dried. The range is wide: lightly oxidized oolongs taste floral and creamy and are often rolled into tight balls, while heavily oxidized and roasted oolongs taste toasty, fruity and rich. Taiwan and Fujian in China are famous for oolongs. Oolongs usually re-steep well, and many people find them rewarding for exploring flavor.

### Black tea

Black tea is fully oxidized, which darkens the leaf and produces deep, malty, fruity or brisk flavors. In China it is called "red tea," because of the color of the liquor. Black tea is the most consumed type in the West. Common examples include Assam (bold and malty), Darjeeling (light and floral), Ceylon (bright and brisk), Keemun (smooth, sometimes winey) and Yunnan (rich, sometimes peppery). Black tea is the base of English breakfast blends, flavored teas such as [Earl Grey](/guides/earl-grey-tea/), and spiced drinks such as masala chai. For a head-to-head look at two major Indian teas, see [Assam vs Darjeeling tea](/guides/assam-vs-darjeeling-tea/).

### Dark tea and pu-erh

Dark teas are post-fermented, meaning they undergo microbial aging after processing. The best-known is pu-erh from Yunnan, China, which comes as loose leaf or pressed cakes and can be aged for years. Flavors are earthy, woody and smooth. Pu-erh is a specialist category that rewards patient exploration, and storage matters a lot. Our guide to [how to store loose leaf tea](/guides/how-to-store-loose-leaf-tea/) explains general principles, but aged pu-erh has its own traditions.

## Flavored, Scented and Blended Teas

Many teas are modified after processing.

- **Scented teas** absorb the aroma of flowers. Jasmine tea is the classic example; see [jasmine tea vs green tea](/guides/jasmine-tea-vs-green-tea/).
- **Flavored teas** have oils, fruit, spices or flavorings added. Earl Grey is black tea with bergamot oil.
- **Blends** combine different teas for balance. English breakfast, for instance, is a blend of black teas.
- **Spiced teas** include masala chai and Kashmiri kahwa. The first is typically black tea simmered with milk and spices, and the second is typically green tea with saffron and spices. Compare them in [masala chai vs chai latte](/guides/masala-chai-vs-chai-latte/), try [how to make chai at home](/guides/how-to-make-chai-at-home/) and read [what is kahwa tea](/guides/what-is-kahwa-tea/).

When buying flavored tea, read the ingredients. Natural flavors and real botanicals usually produce a more nuanced cup than artificial flavoring.

## Herbal Teas (Tisanes)

Herbal teas are infusions of flowers, leaves, roots, seeds or fruit from plants other than *Camellia sinensis*. They are generally caffeine-free, with exceptions such as yerba mate and guayusa. Popular herbal infusions include:

| Herbal tea | Typical character |
| --- | --- |
| Chamomile | Gentle, floral, apple-like |
| Peppermint | Cool, refreshing |
| Rooibos | Sweet, nutty, earthy |
| Hibiscus | Tart, ruby red |
| Ginger | Warm, spicy |
| Lemon balm | Light, lemony |
| Fennel | Sweet, anise-like |

Herbal infusions are brewed with fully boiling water and often steeped for 5 to 7 minutes. Our [caffeine-free tea list](/guides/caffeine-free-tea-list/) covers many options and warns about hidden caffeine. If you are curious about evening choices, read [best tea for sleep](/guides/best-tea-for-sleep/), and for after-dinner choices, see [tea for digestion](/guides/tea-for-digestion/). Remember that herbs are plants with active compounds, so check with a healthcare professional if you are pregnant, taking medication or managing a health condition.

## Chai: One Word, Many Drinks

"Chai" simply means "tea" in many languages, which is why "chai tea" is a redundant phrase that has nevertheless become common in English. In English-speaking countries the word usually means one of three things:

1. **Masala chai:** a traditional spiced milk tea, simmered on the stove.
2. **Chai latte:** a café-style drink made from concentrate or syrup and steamed milk.
3. **Chai-spiced anything:** a flavor profile that has spread to cookies, granola and ice cream.

If you want to understand the difference in depth, [masala chai vs chai latte](/guides/masala-chai-vs-chai-latte/) explains it clearly, and [how to make chai at home](/guides/how-to-make-chai-at-home/) gives you a recipe.

## Quick Guide: Which Tea Should You Try First?

| If you like... | Try... |
| --- | --- |
| Strong, bold, milky drinks | Assam, English breakfast, masala chai |
| Light and floral | Darjeeling, white tea, jasmine green |
| Fresh and grassy | Sencha and other Japanese greens |
| Creamy and rich | Matcha latte, milk oolong |
| Toasty and nutty | Roasted oolong, hojicha, genmaicha |
| Citrusy and aromatic | Earl Grey |
| Sweet and caffeine-free | Rooibos, honeybush |
| Cooling and fresh | Peppermint, spearmint |
| Warm and spicy | Chai, ginger tea, kahwa |

A good plan for a beginner is to buy small amounts of four teas: one black, one green, one oolong and one herbal. Brew each carefully and compare.
