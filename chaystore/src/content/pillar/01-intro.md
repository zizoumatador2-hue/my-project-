## What Is Tea? A Plain-English Definition

Tea is a drink made by steeping the processed leaves of the *Camellia sinensis* plant in hot water. That single plant produces white, green, yellow, oolong, black and dark teas. The differences between them come from where the plant is grown, which part of the plant is picked, and above all how the leaves are processed after picking. For general background, [tea on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tea) and the entry for the plant, [Camellia sinensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camellia_sinensis), are good starting references.

Many drinks sold as "tea" do not come from this plant at all. Chamomile, peppermint, rooibos, hibiscus and ginger are herbal infusions, sometimes called tisanes. They can be delicious and are often caffeine-free, but they are a separate category from true tea. If you want to explore that world, start with our [caffeine-free tea list](/guides/caffeine-free-tea-list/).

## One Plant, Many Teas

It surprises many people that white tea, green tea, oolong and black tea all come from the same species. Two main varieties are widely cultivated. *Camellia sinensis var. sinensis* has smaller leaves and is typically grown in cooler, higher-altitude regions, such as parts of China, Japan and Darjeeling. *Camellia sinensis var. assamica* has larger leaves and thrives in warmer, humid lowlands, such as Assam in India. Thousands of cultivars have been developed from these two, each with its own flavor tendencies.

Growing conditions matter as much as variety. Soil, altitude, rainfall, mist, sunlight and season all affect the leaf. A tea from a high, cool garden grows slowly and often tastes more delicate and aromatic. A tea from a warm lowland garden grows faster and tends to taste bolder and maltier. This idea of place shaping flavor is called terroir, a word borrowed from wine, and it is why you will see teas named after regions: Darjeeling, Assam, Uji, Fujian, Yunnan and so on. For a close look at two famous regions, see [Assam vs Darjeeling tea](/guides/assam-vs-darjeeling-tea/).

### Where tea is grown

Tea is grown in many countries. China is the historic center and remains a leading producer, with a huge range of styles. India, Sri Lanka, Kenya, Japan, Taiwan, Vietnam, Turkey and Indonesia are also significant. Each region has its signature styles: Japan is known for steamed green teas and matcha, Taiwan for oolongs, India for Assam, Darjeeling and Nilgiri black teas, Sri Lanka for brisk Ceylon teas, and Kenya for strong black teas often used in blends.

## A Short History of Tea

Tea has a long and layered history. According to Chinese legend, an emperor discovered tea when leaves drifted into his boiling water, though historians treat the story as legend rather than fact. What is better documented is that tea drinking was well established in China more than a thousand years ago, and that it spread across Asia through trade, religion and culture.

Tea reached Europe in the seventeenth century through traders, and over time became a major commodity, shaping global trade, cuisine and social customs. In Britain, tea became deeply woven into daily life. In India, large-scale tea plantations were established, and a distinct tea culture grew up around milk, sugar and spices, giving rise to the drink many people now call chai. In Japan, tea developed its own refined traditions, including the ceremonial preparation of powdered green tea. In the United States, iced tea became popular in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries and remains a staple.

This history explains why tea has so many faces. A Russian *chay*, a Turkish *çay*, an Indian *chai*, a British "cuppa," a Japanese matcha ceremony and a Southern American sweet tea are all the same plant interpreted through different cultures. That is also why we named this site ChayStore: "chay" is a word for tea in many languages.

## How Tea Is Made: From Leaf to Cup

Understanding how tea is processed helps you understand its flavor. Almost all true teas go through some combination of the following steps.

1. **Plucking.** Leaves and buds are picked, often by hand for fine teas and by machine for large-scale production. Typically, the top two leaves and a bud are the prized part of the shoot.
2. **Withering.** Fresh leaves are spread out so that they lose moisture and become soft and pliable.
3. **Fixation (also called "kill-green").** For green teas, heat is applied by steaming or pan-firing to deactivate the enzymes that cause oxidation. This keeps the leaf green.
4. **Rolling or shaping.** Leaves are rolled, twisted or pressed. This breaks cell walls and shapes the leaf. The shape, from long twists to tight pearls, affects how the tea unfurls and how fast it infuses.
5. **Oxidation.** Oxidation is a chemical reaction that happens when the leaf cells are exposed to air, turning leaves darker and creating new flavors. Black tea is fully oxidized, oolong is partially oxidized, and green and white teas have little or none.
6. **Drying.** The leaves are dried to lock in flavor and make them shelf-stable.
7. **Sorting and grading.** Leaves are sorted by size and quality. Terms such as "whole leaf," "broken leaf," "fannings" and "dust" describe the size of the pieces. This is the reason for a notable difference between loose leaf tea and standard tea bags, which we explore in [loose leaf tea vs tea bags](/guides/loose-leaf-tea-vs-tea-bags/).

Two additional processes worth knowing: **shade-growing**, where tea bushes are covered before harvest to raise chlorophyll and amino acids (the technique behind matcha and gyokuro), and **post-fermentation**, where tea is aged with microbial activity (the process behind pu-erh).

### Oxidation versus fermentation

Tea people often use these words loosely. Technically, black tea is *oxidized*, not fermented. True fermentation, involving microbes, applies to dark teas such as pu-erh. When you read about "fermented tea," check which process is meant.

### What "orange pekoe" really means

"Orange pekoe" is a grading term for black tea of a certain leaf size, not a flavor or an orange ingredient. Grading terms vary by country, and they describe leaf appearance rather than taste or quality. Treat them as hints rather than guarantees.
