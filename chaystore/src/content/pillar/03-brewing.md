## How to Brew Tea: The Fundamentals

A great cup of tea depends on four variables: **leaf amount, water temperature, steeping time and water quality**. Master those and you can brew almost any tea well. The detailed walkthrough is in our guide to [how to brew loose leaf tea](/guides/how-to-brew-loose-leaf-tea/); this section gives you the essentials.

### 1. Leaf amount

A reliable starting ratio is about 2 to 3 grams of tea (roughly 1 teaspoon) per 8 oz (240 ml) of water. Light, bulky teas need more teaspoons for the same weight, and dense, rolled teas need fewer. A small digital scale makes measuring easy and consistent. Our dedicated guide, [how much tea per cup](/guides/how-much-tea-per-cup/), includes a teaspoon chart for common teas.

If your tea tastes weak, add leaf before you add time. If it tastes bitter or harsh, reduce the leaf, cool the water slightly or shorten the steep.

### 2. Water temperature

Water temperature changes flavor more than almost anything else. Hotter water extracts more, faster, which suits robust teas but makes delicate teas harsh.

| Tea | Temperature | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Green | 160–185°F (70–85°C) | 2–3 min |
| White | 175–185°F (80–85°C) | 2–4 min |
| Oolong | 185–205°F (85–96°C) | 3–5 min |
| Black | 200–212°F (93–100°C) | 3–5 min |
| Herbal | 212°F (100°C) | 5–7 min |
| Pu-erh | 195–212°F (90–100°C) | 3–5 min (after an optional rinse) |
| Matcha | 175°F (80°C) | Whisk, do not steep |

If you do not have a thermometer, boil the water and let it rest. After about one minute it is near 200°F (93°C), and after two to four minutes it is in the green tea range. Our guide to [water temperature for green tea](/guides/water-temperature-for-green-tea/) explains this in detail, including how to fix bitter green tea.

### 3. Steeping time

Start with the lower end of the range, taste, and adjust next time. Steeping is a one-way street: you can always steep longer, but you cannot undo an over-steeped cup. For most teas, a timer is your friend.

### 4. Water quality

Tea is more than 99% water, so water quality matters. Use fresh, cold water that has not been boiled repeatedly. If your tap water has a strong taste, use filtered water. Very hard water can dull flavor and cause cloudiness, while very soft or distilled water can taste flat.

### The step-by-step method

1. Measure the tea.
2. Heat water to the right temperature.
3. Pour over the leaves in an infuser, teapot or mug.
4. Steep for the recommended time, covered.
5. Strain promptly.
6. Taste, then adjust the next brew.
7. Re-steep good leaves for additional cups.

## Brewing Styles

### Western-style brewing

This is the common approach: more water, less leaf, a few minutes of steeping, and one or two infusions. It suits mugs and teapots and everyday use.

### Gongfu-style brewing

Used especially for oolongs, pu-erh and fine teas, gongfu brewing uses a lot of leaf in a small vessel (a gaiwan or small teapot), short infusions of seconds rather than minutes, and many steeps. It highlights how a tea evolves from steep to steep. It requires more attention but rewards curiosity.

### Cold brewing

Cold brewing steeps tea in cold water in the refrigerator for 6 to 12 hours. It extracts gently, producing a smooth, naturally sweet, less bitter tea. It is forgiving and ideal for green tea, white tea, jasmine and fruit blends.

### Simmering (for chai)

Masala chai and similar milk teas are simmered rather than steeped. Spices and tea are cooked with water and milk to build body and warmth. Learn it in [how to make chai at home](/guides/how-to-make-chai-at-home/).

## Iced Tea

Iced tea is one of America's favorite ways to drink tea. There are three popular methods:

- **Hot-brew and chill.** Brew at double strength, dilute and pour over ice.
- **Cold brew.** Steep in the refrigerator overnight.
- **Sweet tea.** Brew black tea, dissolve sugar while hot, dilute and chill.

Our guide to [how to make iced tea](/guides/how-to-make-iced-tea/) covers ratios, batch sizes, cloudiness and flavor ideas.

## Common Brewing Problems

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bitter, harsh | Water too hot, steeped too long, too much leaf | Cool the water, shorten the time, use less leaf |
| Weak, watery | Too little leaf, infuser too small | Add leaf, use a larger infuser |
| Flat, dull | Stale tea or poor water | Use fresh tea and filtered water |
| Cloudy iced tea | Rapid chilling or hard water | Cool gradually, use filtered water |
| Muddy, gritty | Fine particles slipping through | Use a finer strainer |
| Too astringent | Over-extraction | Shorten the steep |

## The Gear You Actually Need

You can make excellent tea with very little equipment. Here is what is useful, in order of importance:

1. **A kettle.** Any kettle works, but a [variable-temperature electric kettle](/guides/best-electric-kettle-for-tea/) makes green and white tea easier.
2. **A way to separate the leaves.** A good [tea infuser](/guides/best-tea-infusers/), a teapot with a filter, a French press or a simple strainer.
3. **A scale and a timer.** Cheap, and the best way to be consistent.
4. **A mug or teapot** that you enjoy using.
5. **Airtight storage.** Read [how to store loose leaf tea](/guides/how-to-store-loose-leaf-tea/).
6. **Optional:** a thermometer, a gaiwan, a matcha whisk (chasen) or a milk frother.

You do not need to buy everything at once. Start with a kettle, an infuser and a few teas, and add gear as you learn your preferences.

## Brewing With Milk, Sugar and Lemon

Milk, sugar, lemon and honey are personal choices, and there is no wrong way to enjoy your tea. Some guidance helps:

- **Milk** suits strong black teas, chai and breakfast blends. It softens tannins and adds body. It tends to mask the subtle flavors of delicate teas.
- **Lemon** works with lighter black teas and iced tea. Citrus brightens flavor. Do not add it to milk tea, since acid can make milk curdle.
- **Honey and sugar** soften bitterness. Add a little at a time.
- **Plain** is the best way to learn a tea's real flavor, especially when tasting.

## Loose Leaf or Tea Bags?

Both can make a pleasant cup. Loose leaf tea usually offers larger leaves, a wider selection, better control and the option to re-steep. Tea bags offer speed and convenience. Many people use both. Our comparison, [loose leaf tea vs tea bags](/guides/loose-leaf-tea-vs-tea-bags/), explains the trade-offs honestly so you can decide.
