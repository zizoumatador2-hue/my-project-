## How to Buy Good Tea

Buying tea can be intimidating, with thousands of options and plenty of marketing language. These principles will help you choose with confidence.

### Read the label

A good label tells you what you are buying:

- **Type and name** of the tea (for example, "Darjeeling first flush black tea" rather than just "premium tea").
- **Origin**: country, region, and ideally the estate or producer.
- **Harvest or packing date**, because freshness matters.
- **Ingredients**: for flavored teas, "black tea, natural bergamot oil" is more informative than "natural flavors."
- **Leaf grade or description**: "whole leaf," "full leaf," or similar.
- **Brewing guidance**: temperature, time and amount.

### Buy small and fresh

Tea fades. Buy an amount you can finish in a few months, especially for green tea, white tea, matcha and flavored teas. If you buy in bulk to save money, split it into airtight portions and open one at a time. Our guide on [how to store loose leaf tea](/guides/how-to-store-loose-leaf-tea/) explains the details.

### Look and smell

If you can see and smell the tea, use your senses. Fresh tea smells aromatic. Whole leaves with a consistent appearance are a good sign. Dust, stems and a flat, musty aroma suggest low quality or age.

### Choose a good seller

Reputable sellers describe the tea honestly, explain origin and harvest, accept questions and have a clear return policy. Be wary of exaggerated health claims and "miracle" tea marketing.

### Compare price per serving

A package price is not the real cost. Divide the price by the number of servings. Because loose tea can be re-steeped, it is often better value than it first seems. Use the amounts in [how much tea per cup](/guides/how-much-tea-per-cup/) to calculate.

### Matcha and special cases

Matcha has its own buying rules: bright green color, fine texture, named origin, a recent date and a small tin. Read [best matcha for beginners](/guides/best-matcha-for-beginners/). For spiced and scented teas, look for real ingredients; see [jasmine tea vs green tea](/guides/jasmine-tea-vs-green-tea/) and [what is kahwa tea](/guides/what-is-kahwa-tea/).

## How to Store Tea

Tea has five enemies: air, light, heat, moisture and strong odors. Store it in an airtight, opaque container in a cool, dry cupboard, and keep strongly scented teas apart. In general:

| Tea | Typical freshness window |
| --- | --- |
| Green and white | About 6–12 months |
| Black | About 1–2 years |
| Oolong | About 1–2 years |
| Herbal | About 1 year |
| Matcha | A few months after opening |
| Pu-erh | Can age on purpose |

These are general guidelines, not guarantees. Trust your nose: fresh tea smells aromatic. Discard any tea that smells musty or shows mold. Full instructions are in [how to store loose leaf tea](/guides/how-to-store-loose-leaf-tea/).

## How to Taste Tea Like a Beginner

Tasting is a skill you build by paying attention. A simple method:

1. **Look** at the dry leaves. Note color, size and shape.
2. **Smell** the dry leaves, then the wet leaves after steeping.
3. **Sip** slowly and let the tea move around your mouth.
4. **Notice** sweetness, bitterness, astringency (the drying feeling), body (thin or full) and aftertaste.
5. **Write** a few words. Over time, patterns emerge.
6. **Compare** two teas side by side to sharpen your perception.

A useful exercise is to brew the same tea with different water temperatures or steeping times and notice what changes. That is the fastest way to learn what you like.

## Caffeine in Tea

True tea contains caffeine, but generally less than coffee per cup. Typical estimates for an 8 oz cup are about 95 mg for brewed coffee, 40–50 mg for black tea and 25–35 mg for green tea, though amounts vary a lot with the tea, leaf amount, water temperature and steeping time. Matcha, which you consume as a whole-leaf powder, can contain more per serving than steeped tea. Herbal teas are usually caffeine-free, but not always, so read ingredient lists.

For the full breakdown, see [caffeine in tea vs coffee](/guides/how-much-caffeine-in-tea-vs-coffee/). The U.S. Food and Drug Administration's guidance, in [Spilling the Beans: How Much Caffeine Is Too Much?](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much), says that about 400 milligrams a day is not generally associated with negative effects for most healthy adults. Individual sensitivity varies, and some groups are advised to have less.

## Tea and Wellness: What We Can and Cannot Say

Tea is a popular drink with a rich cultural history, and people often ask whether it is "good for you." Here is a balanced answer.

- Tea is a flavorful, low-calorie beverage that can fit into a healthy diet.
- It contains natural plant compounds, such as polyphenols, and some teas contain L-theanine, an amino acid.
- Research on tea and health exists, but it is often observational or mixed, and no tea is a cure or treatment for any disease.
- Individual factors matter: caffeine sensitivity, pregnancy, medications, iron status and other conditions.

For neutral, evidence-based information on green tea, you can consult the [National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/green-tea). Our articles on wellness topics are general information only and not medical advice. See [is it safe to drink tea every day](/guides/is-it-safe-to-drink-tea-every-day/) for a careful overview of caffeine, iron absorption, medications and hot drinks.

### Evening and digestive teas

Many people reach for caffeine-free herbal teas in the evening or after meals. These can be pleasant parts of a routine, but evidence that they improve sleep or digestion is limited. Learn more in [best tea for sleep](/guides/best-tea-for-sleep/) and [tea for digestion](/guides/tea-for-digestion/), and speak with a healthcare professional about persistent symptoms.

## Building Your Own Tea Collection

You do not need a huge collection to enjoy tea. A thoughtful small selection is more useful than a cupboard full of stale tins.

**A starter set of five**

1. A bold black tea or breakfast blend for mornings (and milk, if you like).
2. A Japanese or Chinese green tea for lighter moments.
3. An oolong for exploration.
4. A caffeine-free herbal tea for evenings.
5. A flavored or spiced tea you love, such as Earl Grey or chai.

**Add over time**

- Matcha for lattes.
- A white tea for summer.
- A jasmine tea or a kahwa blend.
- A single-origin tea such as Darjeeling or Assam.
- Seasonal teas and samplers.

Keep notes on what you buy and what you enjoy. After a few months you will have a clear picture of your own taste.

## Tea Etiquette and Culture Around the World

Tea is also about people and places. A few examples:

- **United Kingdom:** black tea with milk, afternoon tea with scones and sandwiches.
- **India:** chai served from street stalls and at home, simmered with milk and spices.
- **Japan:** green tea at meals, and the formal tea ceremony with matcha.
- **China:** gongfu brewing, tea houses, and a vast range of regional teas.
- **Morocco:** sweet mint tea poured from height as a gesture of hospitality.
- **Russia and Central Asia:** strong tea from a samovar, often served with jam or sweets.
- **Turkey:** small tulip-shaped glasses of strong black tea (*çay*), served all day.
- **Kashmir:** kahwa, green tea with saffron and spices.
- **United States:** iced tea, sweet tea in the South, and growing interest in specialty loose leaf.

Whatever your tradition, the best way to drink tea is the way you enjoy it.

## Tea Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| *Camellia sinensis* | The plant that produces true tea |
| Tisane | An herbal infusion that does not contain tea leaf |
| Oxidation | A reaction that darkens tea leaves and develops flavor |
| Flush | A seasonal harvest, such as Darjeeling first flush |
| Astringency | The dry, puckering feeling that tea can create |
| Liquor | The brewed tea liquid |
| Infusion | The act of steeping, or one steeping |
| Gongfu | A style of brewing with many short infusions |
| Gaiwan | A lidded bowl used for brewing tea |
| Chasen | A bamboo whisk used for matcha |
| Tencha | Shade-grown leaf that is ground into matcha |
| CTC | "Crush, tear, curl," a method of producing small, strong black tea pellets |
| Fannings and dust | Small pieces of tea leaf, often used in tea bags |
| Terroir | The effect of place on flavor |
