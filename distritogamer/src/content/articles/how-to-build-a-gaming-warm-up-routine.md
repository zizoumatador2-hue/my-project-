Musicians warm up, athletes warm up, and competitive gamers who last for years usually do too. A warm-up is not magic. It is a short, repeatable routine that gets your hands moving, your eyes tracking and your attention switched on before the match that counts.

This guide shows you how to build a warm-up that takes about 15 minutes, how to adapt it to different games and how to avoid the mistakes that turn warm-ups into exhausting practice sessions. It fits into the wider approach in our [complete guide to gaming tips](/#pillar).

## Why warming up helps

Cold performance is a real thing. Your first few minutes of play are often your worst: hands feel slow, aim feels off and decisions are sloppy. A warm-up helps in three ways:

- **Physical:** gentle movement loosens muscles and tendons before sudden, precise actions. The general idea of warming up is covered in the [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warming_up).
- **Coordination:** repeating the same movement a few times lets your brain recalibrate to your current sensitivity, setup and day.
- **Mental:** a routine marks the shift from scrolling or working to focused play.

None of this requires special equipment, only a bit of consistency.

> **Note:** This guide offers general advice, not medical advice. If you experience pain, numbness or tingling in your hands, wrists or arms, stop and speak to a qualified professional. See [repetitive strain injury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repetitive_strain_injury) for background.

## The four-block structure

Most effective routines share the same structure, regardless of the game:

| Block | Time | Goal | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Loosen up | 1 minute | Prepare hands and shoulders | Gentle wrist circles, finger stretches |
| 2. Easy drills | 3 to 5 minutes | Find rhythm at a comfortable pace | Slow, accurate target practice |
| 3. Skill block | 5 to 8 minutes | Work one skill | Tracking, flicking, combos or movement |
| 4. Game-specific | 4 to 5 minutes | Rehearse real situations | Practice server, training mode, custom game |

## Block 1: Loosen up (about a minute)

Do these gently and never to the point of pain:

1. **Wrist circles.** Slowly rotate each wrist ten times in each direction.
2. **Finger spreads.** Open your hand wide, then close it into a loose fist, ten times.
3. **Forearm stretch.** Extend one arm, palm up, and gently pull the fingers back with the other hand for about ten seconds, then repeat with the other arm.
4. **Shoulder rolls.** Roll your shoulders backward ten times to release tension.

Keep your shoulders relaxed when you start playing. Tense shoulders cause tense forearms and tense aim.

## Block 2: Easy drills (3 to 5 minutes)

The goal here is rhythm, not performance. Choose drills that you can do accurately at a comfortable pace:

- **Shooters:** slow target practice with large targets, or slow-paced bot practice. Aim for clean hits rather than speed.
- **Fighting games:** repeat simple inputs such as a basic combo or a defensive option until it feels automatic.
- **Rhythm and platforming games:** play an easy level or song at normal speed.
- **Controller play:** make slow circles with each stick, then move between targets in the game with smooth movements.

If you miss a lot, slow down. The warm-up should never make you feel worse.

## Block 3: Train one skill (5 to 8 minutes)

Pick the skill that matters most for the day, and rotate through several across the week.

- **Tracking:** follow a moving target smoothly and aim for consistency.
- **Flicking:** quick moves to appearing targets, accuracy first and speed second.
- **Movement:** practice strafing, counter-strafing or dodging in a safe environment.
- **Execution:** repeat a combo, build order or route until it is clean.

We cover the aim side of this block in detail in [how to improve your aim in FPS games](/gaming-tips/how-to-improve-aim-in-fps-games/).

## Block 4: Game-specific practice (4 to 5 minutes)

Now practice what you will actually do in the match. In *Counter-Strike 2*, for example, you could load a practice server with bots, work on spraying with your favorite rifle and rehearse one grenade you use often. Our [Counter-Strike 2 practice guide](/game-guides/counter-strike-2-practice-guide/) shows how to set that up. In *Valorant*, the shooting range lets you practice with each weapon and your agent's abilities, as we mention in the [Valorant beginner guide](/game-guides/beginners-guide-to-valorant/).

> **Tip:** Finish your warm-up with the exact weapon, character or loadout you plan to use in your first match, so your hands start the match feeling familiar.

## Putting it together: a 15-minute template

1. **Minute 1:** Loosen up.
2. **Minutes 2 to 5:** Easy drills at a relaxed pace.
3. **Minutes 6 to 11:** One skill block, with a clear target such as "ten clean hits in a row".
4. **Minutes 12 to 15:** Game-specific practice with your actual loadout.
5. **Minute 16 onward:** Play, and pay attention to one cue, such as crosshair height.

## Adapting the routine

- **If you only have five minutes:** do blocks 1, 2 and 4 in a shortened form.
- **If you are tired or frustrated:** shorten the routine and consider a break instead.
- **If you play several games:** use the same structure, but swap the drills for each game.
- **If you are a beginner:** keep the drills very easy and focus on comfort, not scores.

## Common warm-up mistakes

- **Making the warm-up too hard.** If you are exhausted before the match begins, you have trained, not warmed up.
- **Changing the routine daily.** Consistency makes it a habit and makes your scores comparable.
- **Skipping the loosen-up block.** It takes one minute and sets the tone for relaxed hands.
- **Warming up with different settings.** Use the same sensitivity and controls as in the real game, or the warm-up teaches the wrong movement. See [best sensitivity settings for gaming](/game-settings/best-sensitivity-settings-for-gaming/).
- **Ignoring hardware.** A warm-up cannot compensate for inconsistent frame rates, so check [how to increase FPS on PC](/gaming-performance/how-to-increase-fps-on-pc/) if your performance fluctuates.

## Tracking whether it works

For two weeks, note how your first three matches feel on days when you warm up and days when you do not. Track one simple number, such as first-shot accuracy or deaths in the first few minutes. If you see a clear difference, keep the routine. If not, try shortening or adjusting it. Pair the habit with our weekly structure in [how to get better at gaming](/gaming-tips/how-to-get-better-at-gaming/).

## Warm-up examples for different kinds of players

The structure is the same for everyone, but the content should match your game. Here are three examples you can adapt.

### The shooter player (about 15 minutes)

1. **Minute 1:** wrist, finger and shoulder loosening.
2. **Minutes 2 to 5:** slow, accurate shots against large targets or slow bots.
3. **Minutes 6 to 10:** two scenarios, one flicking and one tracking, aiming for accuracy first.
4. **Minutes 11 to 15:** practice range with the weapons and abilities you plan to use, including a few counter-strafes and bursts.

### The controller action-game player (about 10 minutes)

1. **Minute 1:** thumb and wrist stretches.
2. **Minutes 2 to 4:** move through a familiar area with the camera, locking on and off targets, to settle your stick control.
3. **Minutes 5 to 8:** fight one or two easy enemies, focusing on timing your dodges and not on speed.
4. **Minutes 9 to 10:** check your equipment and healing items so that you start the main session prepared.

### The fighting game player (about 15 minutes)

1. **Minute 1:** finger and wrist loosening.
2. **Minutes 2 to 6:** repeat your simplest combos at a comfortable pace until they are clean.
3. **Minutes 7 to 11:** practice one defensive option, such as blocking and punishing a common move, against a recorded dummy.
4. **Minutes 12 to 15:** play a few relaxed matches with one cue to watch.

None of these is "the right" routine. They are templates, and the best version is the one you can repeat.

## Take notes and cool down

After you finish playing, a short cool-down helps you learn from the session.

- **Stretch briefly.** Gently stretch your hands, wrists and shoulders.
- **Write one line.** Note your warm-up score, one thing that went well and one thing to change.
- **Rest your eyes.** Look away from the screen for a minute.
- **Hydrate.**

The note takes less than a minute and gives you a record that makes patterns visible after a few weeks.

## Adjusting the warm-up over time

As you improve, your warm-up should change with you.

- **After a few weeks,** check whether the easy drills are still easy. If they are, make them slightly harder or shorter.
- **When you change games,** keep the structure but change the drills.
- **When your hands feel tired,** shorten the routine and add more rest days.
- **When you plateau,** change the skill block and see whether a different focus helps.

A routine that never changes can become automatic and stop teaching you. A routine that changes constantly never becomes a habit. Aim for a core that stays the same and a skill block that rotates.

## Key takeaways

- A warm-up takes 10 to 20 minutes: loosen up, easy drills, one skill block and game-specific practice.
- Keep it slightly easier than real play, and use the same settings as in the game.
- Consistency matters more than length, so choose a routine you will actually repeat.
- Stop if you feel pain, numbness or tension, and rest.
- Rotate the skill block over the week while keeping the rest of the structure the same.

A warm-up is not magic. It is a small habit that makes your first match of the day look more like your tenth.
