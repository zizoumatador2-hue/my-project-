Sensitivity decides how far your crosshair moves when you move your mouse. Get it roughly right and aiming feels natural. Get it wrong and you will either overshoot everything or run out of mouse pad in the middle of a turn.

Many players spend years trying settings copied from other people. A better approach is to understand what the numbers mean and pick a setting that fits **your** hand, **your** desk and **your** game. This guide shows you how, and it connects with the practice habits in our guide to [improving your aim in FPS games](/gaming-tips/how-to-improve-aim-in-fps-games/) and our [complete gaming tips guide](/#pillar).

## DPI, in-game sensitivity and eDPI

Three numbers matter:

- **DPI** (dots per inch, sometimes called CPI) is how many units your mouse reports per inch of movement. See [dots per inch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dots_per_inch) for the general idea.
- **In-game sensitivity** is the multiplier the game applies to those units.
- **eDPI** is the product of the two: DPI times in-game sensitivity.

For example, 800 DPI with a sensitivity of 0.5 gives an eDPI of 400. Another player with 400 DPI and a sensitivity of 1.0 has the same eDPI and the same effective speed in that game.

> **Important:** eDPI is only comparable within the same game, because different games convert sensitivity to rotation differently. A value that feels slow in one game can feel fast in another even with identical eDPI.

## A sensible DPI

Most modern mice track accurately across a wide range of DPI values. For gaming, most players use between 400 and 1600. There is no performance magic in high DPI numbers, and very high values can make small jitters in your hand more visible. A mid-range choice such as 800 is a sensible default, and you can adjust the in-game number to get the speed you want. Use the **same DPI in every game and in Windows** so your hand always sees the same behavior.

## Turn off mouse acceleration

Acceleration makes your cursor move farther when you move the mouse faster, which breaks consistency. For gaming, turn it off:

1. Open Windows mouse settings and uncheck **Enhance pointer precision**.
2. In each game, make sure any mouse acceleration or smoothing option is off.
3. If your mouse software has its own acceleration or angle snapping settings, disable them.

Learn how mice track and why acceleration changes the feel in the [computer mouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_mouse) article. With acceleration off, the same physical movement always produces the same turn, which is exactly what muscle memory needs.

## Find a starting point: the 180-degree test

1. **Set DPI** to something between 400 and 1600.
2. **Pick a middle in-game sensitivity** for your game.
3. **Center your mouse** on your pad.
4. **Turn 180 degrees** in the game with one natural sweep.
5. **Check where your hand ends up.** If you run off the pad, lower the sensitivity. If the turn takes a large arm movement and feels slow, raise it.

A common result is a full 180-degree turn that uses about a third to half of a typical mouse pad's width. That is a rough guide, not a rule, and your preference may differ.

## Low, medium or high?

| Style | Typical feel | Strengths | Trade-offs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lower sensitivity (arm-driven) | Large arm movements for turns | Precise small corrections, steady tracking | Needs a large mouse pad and desk space; slower turns |
| Medium sensitivity (mixed) | Wrist and forearm together | Balance of speed and precision | Needs consistent technique |
| Higher sensitivity (wrist-driven) | Small movements for turns | Fast turns, small desk footprint | Harder to make fine adjustments |

Many players land in the middle and shift slowly as they improve. Both ends can work at a high level. Pick the one you can keep consistent.

## Test precision, not just speed

After the 180-degree test, check small movements:

1. **Hover test.** Try placing your crosshair on a small target and keep it there. Do you overshoot or struggle to make tiny adjustments?
2. **Tracking test.** Follow a moving target for 30 seconds. Are you smooth or jerky?
3. **Flick test.** Flick between two targets. Do you consistently stop on target?

If small adjustments feel hard, lower sensitivity slightly. If you cannot turn quickly enough, raise it slightly. Change by small steps, about 5 to 10 percent, and play for several sessions before judging.

## Make your setup consistent

Sensitivity is part of a system:

- **Mouse pad:** a large, consistent surface supports low sensitivity. See our guide to [FPS gaming mice and pads](/gaming-gear/best-gaming-mouse-for-fps/).
- **Polling rate:** use at least 1000 Hz so your mouse updates often.
- **Frame rate:** unstable frame rates make a given sensitivity feel inconsistent. Check [how to increase FPS on PC](/gaming-performance/how-to-increase-fps-on-pc/).
- **Input lag:** delay can make aiming feel heavy, so see [how to reduce input lag](/gaming-performance/how-to-reduce-input-lag-on-pc/).
- **Posture:** a comfortable arm position helps consistency; see [how to set up a gaming desk](/gaming-gear/how-to-set-up-a-gaming-desk/).

## Zoom and scope sensitivity

Many games have separate sensitivity for scopes or zoom. You can often use a **zoom multiplier** so that moving your mouse the same distance covers a similar real-world distance at any zoom level. If your game has this option, experiment with it, because consistent feel across zoom levels makes scoped shots easier. If not, adjust scope sensitivity so it feels natural to you.

## Controllers are different

If you play with a controller, the equivalent settings are stick sensitivity, deadzones, response curves and aim assist. See our guide to [best controller settings for competitive gaming](/game-settings/best-controller-settings-for-competitive-gaming/), and our comparison of [controller and mouse-and-keyboard play](/game-comparisons/controller-vs-mouse-and-keyboard/) if you are deciding between them.

## Common sensitivity mistakes

- **Copying a professional's setting.** Their desk, hands and game may differ from yours.
- **Changing settings after every bad session.** This resets your muscle memory.
- **Using very high DPI as a shortcut.** It adds jitter without adding skill.
- **Leaving acceleration on.** It makes your aim inconsistent.
- **Using a pad that is too small.** Running out of room forces you to lift and reposition at bad moments.

## Keep it stable and reassess slowly

Once you find a comfortable setting, write down your DPI, in-game sensitivity and zoom values. Keep them for at least two to three weeks. If you still want to change, change by a small amount and give it another few weeks. Over months, your ideal setting may drift slightly as your hand control improves, and that is fine, as long as you change deliberately rather than reactively.

## A worked example of finding a sensitivity

Here is an invented example of the process, so that you can see how the steps connect.

A player uses 800 DPI and a medium-sized mouse pad. They start with an in-game sensitivity of 0.5 in a shooter, giving an eDPI of 400.

1. **180-degree test:** a full turn uses about half the pad, which feels comfortable.
2. **Hover test:** they find that placing the crosshair on a small, distant target takes a few corrections, which suggests the sensitivity is slightly high.
3. **Adjustment:** they lower it to 0.45 (an eDPI of 360), and the same turn now uses a little more of the pad.
4. **Re-test:** small corrections feel steadier, and the turn still fits on the pad.
5. **Lock in:** they write down 800 DPI and 0.45 and keep them for a month.

The numbers are only an example, and the right values for you depend on your hand, desk and game. The process is the same: a comfortable turn, a precise hover, small adjustments and a long commitment.

## Troubleshooting sensitivity problems

| Symptom | Possible cause | What to try |
|---|---|---|
| You constantly overshoot targets | Sensitivity too high, or a tense grip | Lower it slightly and relax your grip |
| You cannot turn quickly enough | Sensitivity too low, or a small pad | Raise it slightly or use a larger pad |
| Aim feels different from day to day | Changed settings, mouse acceleration or an inconsistent surface | Check acceleration and keep settings stable |
| Crosshair jitters at a standstill | Very high DPI or a dirty sensor | Lower DPI and clean the mouse and pad |
| Scoped shots feel off | Scope sensitivity not matched | Adjust scope or zoom sensitivity |
| Tracking feels jerky | Frame rate dips or input lag | Check performance and settings |

## Changing games without losing your feel

If you play several games, you will want your sensitivity to feel similar across them.

- Some tools and websites convert sensitivity between games so that a given hand movement turns the view by a similar amount. Search for a sensitivity converter for the games you play.
- Even with conversion, field of view and other settings can change the feel, so adjust slightly by hand.
- Keep a note of each game's values.

You do not need exact matches. Feeling similar is enough.

## Key takeaways

- There is no universal best sensitivity. Choose one that lets you turn comfortably and still make precise corrections.
- Use a moderate DPI, turn off mouse acceleration and use the same DPI in every game.
- Run the 180-degree test, then adjust in small steps of about 5 to 10 percent.
- Write your values down and keep them for at least two to three weeks before changing again.
- Support low sensitivity with a large, consistent mouse pad and good desk posture.

Consistency beats cleverness here. A good-enough sensitivity you keep will always outperform a perfect one you keep changing.
