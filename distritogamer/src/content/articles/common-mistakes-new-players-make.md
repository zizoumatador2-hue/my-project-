Everyone makes mistakes when they start a new game, and most of them are the same few mistakes. The good news is that they are easy to fix once you recognize them. This guide covers twelve of the most common ones, why they hold you back, and what to do instead. It pairs well with our [gaming tips for beginners](/beginner-guides/gaming-tips-for-beginners/) and our [complete gaming tips guide](/#pillar).

## 1. Changing settings every session

Tweaking your sensitivity, keybinds or graphics constantly feels like progress, but it resets your muscle memory every time. **Fix:** after you find something comfortable, keep it unchanged for at least a few weeks. Treat each change as an experiment and write down what you changed. See our guide to [the best sensitivity settings for gaming](/game-settings/best-sensitivity-settings-for-gaming/) for how to choose a starting point.

## 2. Skipping the tutorial and the settings menu

Tutorials are boring, but they teach the actions the game assumes you know. The settings menu often hides helpful options like control remapping, aim assist, auto-sprint or subtitles. **Fix:** do the tutorial once, and spend five minutes in the options before you start.

## 3. Ignoring the game's economy or resources

Almost every game has a resource system: money in a tactical shooter, stamina in an action game, mana, ammo, health items or cooldowns. Beginners spend them without thinking. **Fix:** learn what each resource does, how it refills, and what the standard spending pattern is. In team shooters, buying together and saving together is often more important than perfect aim, as we explain in our [Valorant beginner guide](/game-guides/beginners-guide-to-valorant/).

## 4. Playing while tilted

After a bad round or a loss, many players keep playing angry and lose more. **Fix:** decide in advance when you will stop, for example after two losses in a row, and take a short break. A calm brain makes better decisions.

## 5. Copying a professional's settings without understanding them

Pro settings are tuned to someone else's hands, hardware and habits. **Fix:** use them as a reference only. Try them for a while and keep what feels right, but expect to adjust.

## 6. Buying gear too early

New mice, keyboards, headsets and controllers can help, but not if your problem is a habit. **Fix:** make sure your settings, frame rate and fundamentals are right first. Then upgrade where you can name the limit, for example a mouse that feels too heavy for your grip. Our [FPS gaming mouse guide](/gaming-gear/best-gaming-mouse-for-fps/) explains what to look for.

## 7. Never reviewing losses

If you do not look back, you repeat the same mistakes. **Fix:** after a session, spend five minutes thinking about the moment you lost the most. In online games, watch a replay. Our guide to [reviewing your gameplay](/advanced-strategies/how-to-review-your-gameplay/) shows how.

## 8. Practicing the wrong thing

Many beginners practice what they are already good at because it feels satisfying. **Fix:** find the skill that costs you the most and give it a few minutes a day. A [simple warm-up routine](/gaming-tips/how-to-build-a-gaming-warm-up-routine/) keeps this habit easy.

## 9. Rushing instead of observing

In action and role-playing games, beginners often charge in and get punished. In *Elden Ring*, for example, many deaths come from over-committing to attacks rather than from low stats, as covered in our [Elden Ring beginner guide](/game-guides/beginners-guide-to-elden-ring/). **Fix:** watch enemy patterns for a few seconds before attacking, and attack only when you have a safe opening.

## 10. Ignoring performance problems

Low frame rate, stutter or lag make everything harder, and many players blame themselves. **Fix:** check performance first. Our guides on [increasing FPS on PC](/gaming-performance/how-to-increase-fps-on-pc/) and [fixing game lag](/troubleshooting/how-to-fix-game-lag/) can save you hours of frustration.

## 11. Blaming teammates or luck

Blame feels good and teaches nothing. **Fix:** ask what you could have done differently, even if the answer is small. This habit improves your play and your team's mood.

## 12. Comparing yourself to experts

Watching highly skilled players can make your own progress look tiny. **Fix:** compare yourself to your past self. Track one number, such as accuracy or deaths per match, and look for slow improvement over weeks.

## Why beginner mistakes are so hard to see

Most mistakes feel reasonable while you are making them. Changing your sensitivity feels like improvement. Charging into a fight feels brave. Blaming a teammate feels fair. The mistakes only become visible when you step back and compare what you did with what the game was asking of you. That is why the single most useful habit for any new player is a short, honest review after a session. Five minutes of asking "what cost me the most?" beats an extra hour of play.

A good test is to look for repeats. If the same kind of situation costs you again and again, such as dying in the same spot, running out of the same resource or losing the same type of duel, that is not bad luck. It is a pattern, and patterns can be fixed.

## Mistakes that depend on the genre

Some mistakes appear in almost every game, but others are specific to a type of game:

- **Shooters:** peeking without a plan, standing still while shooting, and not checking the minimap or radar. Focus on crosshair placement and moving before you fight. See [how to improve aim in FPS games](/gaming-tips/how-to-improve-aim-in-fps-games/).
- **Action and role-playing games:** over-leveling one stat, hoarding items you never use, and ignoring defensive options. Learn what each stat changes before you spend points.
- **Strategy and tactics games:** expanding too slowly, ignoring scouting and fighting even battles. Use the principles in our guide to [strategies that work in any game](/advanced-strategies/gaming-strategies-that-work-in-any-game/).
- **Sports and fighting games:** button-mashing instead of learning a few reliable moves. Pick two or three tools and master them in training mode first.

## A one-week plan to fix your biggest mistake

1. **Day 1:** Write down the mistake you recognize most in yourself. Be specific: "I change sensitivity after every loss" is better than "my aim is bad".
2. **Day 2:** Decide on a rule that prevents it, such as "no setting changes until Sunday".
3. **Days 3 to 5:** Follow the rule and keep a tally of how often you broke it.
4. **Day 6:** Watch or recall one moment where the rule helped.
5. **Day 7:** Decide whether to keep the rule, change it or move on to the next mistake.

Small rules like this work because they remove decisions that you would otherwise make while frustrated. They also build the habit of treating improvement as an experiment rather than a mood.

## What to do next

Pick the one mistake on this list that sounds most like you, and fix only that one for the next week. Then pick another. If you want a structured path, follow our [seven-step plan for getting better at gaming](/gaming-tips/how-to-get-better-at-gaming/).

## How to tell a mistake from bad luck

Not every loss is a mistake. Games have randomness, uneven teams and moments that nobody could have predicted. A useful test is to ask three questions after a bad moment:

1. **Could I have known?** If the information was available and I ignored it, it was a mistake. If nobody could have known, it was bad luck.
2. **Would I do it again?** If the same choice would be reasonable next time, the outcome was luck. If you would choose differently, you learned something.
3. **Does it repeat?** A single odd event is luck. A pattern is a habit.

Treating every loss as bad luck stops you from learning. Treating every loss as a mistake makes you anxious. The three questions keep you in the middle.

## A short checklist for your setup

Several beginner mistakes are about equipment and settings rather than play. Before you blame yourself, check:

- Is your monitor running at its highest refresh rate? See [monitor settings](/game-settings/best-gaming-monitor-settings/).
- Is your frame rate stable? See [how to increase FPS on PC](/gaming-performance/how-to-increase-fps-on-pc/).
- Is mouse acceleration off, and is your sensitivity comfortable?
- Do you have a wired connection if you play online?
- Is your volume comfortable, and can you hear important sounds?
- Are you sitting comfortably, with a screen at a good height?

Fixing these takes an afternoon and removes a lot of noise from your learning.

## One last thing: be patient with yourself

Everyone who is good at a game was once confused by it. The most common reason people stop improving is not lack of talent but frustration with how slowly progress feels. Keep your goals small, celebrate specific improvements and remember that one fixed mistake per week adds up to dozens in a year.

## Key takeaways

- Keep settings stable for weeks, and treat any change as an experiment.
- Do the tutorial and read the options menu once.
- Learn the economy or resource system before you try to play perfectly.
- Use a stop rule for tilt, and review one loss after each session.
- Spend money on gear only after you can name the problem it would solve.

Pick the single mistake that sounds most like you and fix only that one this week. Small, specific changes beat a long list of resolutions.
