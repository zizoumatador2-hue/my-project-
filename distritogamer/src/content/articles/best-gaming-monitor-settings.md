A good gaming monitor can look disappointing if it is set up wrong. A surprising number of 144 Hz and 240 Hz monitors run at 60 Hz for years because nobody changed one Windows setting. Others have overdrive set so aggressively that moving objects have bright halos, or processing modes enabled that add delay.

This guide walks through the display settings that matter, in the order that makes the biggest difference. It works with our [FPS settings guide](/game-settings/best-fps-settings-for-better-performance/) and our guide to [reducing input lag](/gaming-performance/how-to-reduce-input-lag-on-pc/). For the bigger picture, see our [complete guide to gaming tips](/#pillar).

## 1. Use the right cable and port

Your monitor's maximum refresh rate depends on the cable, the port on the monitor and the port on your graphics card.

- **DisplayPort** is the most common choice for high refresh rate PC monitors.
- **HDMI** can work well, but older HDMI versions limit resolution or refresh rate. Check your monitor's manual for which HDMI version it supports.
- **Plug into the graphics card,** not the motherboard's video output, or your PC may use the weaker integrated graphics.
- **Use the cable that came with the monitor** if you are not sure, or a certified cable of the right version.

## 2. Set the refresh rate in Windows

This is the most common mistake. Windows does not always select your monitor's highest refresh rate automatically.

1. Right-click the desktop and open **Display settings**.
2. Choose **Advanced display**.
3. Under **Choose a refresh rate**, select the highest value.
4. Confirm that your resolution is the monitor's **native resolution**.

Console players should check their console's video settings for the same option. Refresh rate is the number of times the display updates each second; see [refresh rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refresh_rate) for background. A higher refresh rate only helps if your game can produce enough frames, which is covered in [how to increase FPS on PC](/gaming-performance/how-to-increase-fps-on-pc/).

## 3. Enable adaptive sync

Adaptive sync (called FreeSync, G-Sync or G-Sync Compatible, depending on the manufacturer) adjusts the monitor's refresh rate to match your frame rate. It removes screen tearing without the latency penalty of traditional V-Sync. See [variable refresh rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variable_refresh_rate) for an overview.

1. Turn on adaptive sync in the **monitor's on-screen menu**.
2. Enable it in your **graphics driver control panel**.
3. In games, set your frame rate cap a few frames below the refresh rate so you stay inside the adaptive range.

If you see flicker in dark scenes or menus, try a different cable, lower the refresh rate by one step or check for firmware updates.

## 4. Choose the right overdrive setting

Pixels do not change color instantly. **Overdrive** (also called response time or trace-free in different menus) speeds up the change.

| Setting | What you see | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Too low | Dark trails behind moving objects | Blurry motion |
| Middle | Clean motion with little overshoot | Usually best |
| Too high | Bright or dark halos, "inverse ghosting" | Distracting artifacts |

To test, use a free online motion test or move a bright object across a dark background in a game. Pick the highest setting that does not create obvious halos. The best setting can differ at different refresh rates, so if your monitor has adaptive overdrive, enable it. Always judge with your own eyes, because manufacturer numbers are measured under ideal conditions.

## 5. Use a low-latency game mode

Many monitors have **game mode** or similar options that turn off extra image processing. Make sure features like dynamic contrast, noise reduction and motion smoothing are off. These can add milliseconds of delay or alter the image in ways that do not help gameplay. If your monitor offers a **low latency** or **fast** input mode, use it.

> **Tip:** TVs often have a separate "Game Mode" that disables heavy processing. If you play on a TV, turn it on and, for the best experience, set it per input.

## 6. Brightness, contrast and gamma

Good visibility matters more than a dramatic look.

- **Brightness:** match it to your room. Too bright causes eye fatigue, and too dark hides detail. A comfortable level for long sessions is better than the maximum.
- **Contrast:** keep it high enough to separate dark shapes from the background without crushing detail. Default is usually fine.
- **Gamma:** a gamma near 2.2 is the standard for most PC content. See [gamma correction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_correction). If dark areas are hard to see, raise brightness slightly or use the game's brightness option rather than setting an unusual gamma.
- **Color temperature:** a neutral setting such as 6500K looks accurate, while warmer settings can be more comfortable at night.

Some monitors have a "black equalizer" or shadow-boost feature that lightens dark areas. It can help visibility in some games but makes the image look flat, so test it and decide whether you want it for competitive play.

## 7. Sharpness and extras

- **Sharpness:** leave it at the default or neutral setting. Overdoing it creates halos around text and edges.
- **Aspect ratio:** use the native aspect ratio to avoid stretching. Stretched modes can distort enemies and make targets look different.
- **Backlight strobing (motion blur reduction):** features like ULMB can reduce blur but often cannot be combined with adaptive sync, and reduce brightness. Try them in competitive games if your frame rate is high and stable.
- **HDR:** only enable it for games and displays where it works well; otherwise turn it off.

## 8. Resolution, refresh rate and frame rate: finding a balance

Higher resolution looks sharper, but it requires much more GPU power. If you choose 1440p or 4K, check whether your PC can reach your monitor's refresh rate in the games you play. For fast competitive games, many players choose a lower resolution with a higher refresh rate. For visually rich single-player games, a higher resolution at a lower, stable refresh rate is often better.

## 9. Check for problems

- **Screen tearing:** enable adaptive sync or a frame cap.
- **Stuttering:** check frame rate stability first. See [fix game crashes and stuttering](/troubleshooting/fix-game-crashes-and-stuttering/).
- **Washed-out colors:** check HDR settings in both Windows and the monitor.
- **Flicker:** try another cable, port or refresh rate, and update drivers and monitor firmware.
- **Dead pixels or uneven backlight:** check your retailer's return policy and the manufacturer's warranty.

## 10. Posture and placement

A great display set up badly still causes problems. The top of the screen should be roughly at eye level, about an arm's length away, and not tilted so that you strain your neck. A monitor arm can help with height and desk space. See our guide to [how to set up a gaming desk](/gaming-gear/how-to-set-up-a-gaming-desk/).

## Quick checklist

1. Right cable and port
2. Highest refresh rate selected in Windows
3. Native resolution
4. Adaptive sync on, with a frame cap
5. Overdrive set to a clean middle value
6. Game mode on, extra processing off
7. Comfortable brightness, neutral gamma and color
8. HDR off unless it is working well

## Troubleshooting display problems

If something still looks wrong after the steps above, this table can help you narrow it down.

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to try |
|---|---|---|
| Image looks blurry | Non-native resolution or scaling | Set native resolution and 100 percent scaling, check sharpness |
| Trails behind moving objects | Overdrive too low | Raise overdrive one step |
| Bright halos around moving objects | Overdrive too high | Lower overdrive one step |
| Screen tears in fast motion | No adaptive sync or frame cap | Enable adaptive sync, set a cap below refresh rate |
| Flicker in menus or loading screens | Adaptive sync with variable frame rate | Update drivers and firmware, try another cable |
| Colors look washed out | HDR enabled incorrectly or wrong color range | Check HDR settings and RGB range in the driver |
| Game feels delayed | Processing modes on | Enable game mode and turn off extra processing |
| Refresh rate option missing | Cable, port or driver limit | Use a higher-rated cable, port or update drivers |

## Calibrating by eye

You do not need special equipment for a good result.

1. **Brightness:** display a dark scene in a game and raise brightness until shapes in the shadows are just visible.
2. **Contrast:** use a bright scene and lower contrast if bright areas lose detail.
3. **Check with a gradient image** from a trusted source. You should see smooth steps without banding.
4. **Compare at a different time of day,** since room light changes how a screen looks.
5. **Do not chase perfection.** A comfortable, clear image is the goal.

If color accuracy matters for other work, such as photo editing, a hardware calibration tool is worth considering, but it is not required for gaming.

## Revisit after hardware changes

Display settings are easy to forget, and they quietly change when you swap a cable, update a graphics driver or move to a new graphics card. After any hardware or driver change, spend two minutes checking that the refresh rate, adaptive sync and resolution are still set the way you left them. It is a small habit that prevents months of playing on the wrong settings without noticing.

## Key takeaways

- Set your monitor to its highest refresh rate in Windows, and use the native resolution.
- Enable adaptive sync and cap your frame rate a few frames below the refresh rate.
- Choose a middle overdrive setting that avoids both trails and bright halos.
- Turn on game mode and turn off extra processing such as motion smoothing and dynamic contrast.
- Aim for comfortable brightness, gamma near 2.2 and a neutral color temperature, and position the screen at eye level.

Most of the benefit comes from the first four steps. If your display still looks wrong after those, use the troubleshooting table above.
