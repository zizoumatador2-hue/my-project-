Good aim in a first-person shooter looks like talent from the outside, but it is mostly a collection of small, trainable habits. Players who "just have aim" usually share the same few things: a sensitivity they never change, a crosshair that is already where the enemy will be, and a lot of repetition that was deliberate rather than random.

This guide breaks aim into the parts you can actually work on. You will learn how to choose a sensitivity and keep it, how to fix **crosshair placement** (the most underrated skill in shooters), how to practice **tracking** and **flicking**, and how to make sure your hardware is not quietly working against you. If you want the wider picture of improving at games in general, our [complete guide to gaming tips](/#pillar) covers practice, mindset and review.

## What "good aim" really means

Aim is not one skill. It is four, and they fail in different ways:

- **Crosshair placement:** where your crosshair rests before a fight starts.
- **Flicking:** a fast, accurate move to a target that just appeared.
- **Tracking:** keeping the crosshair on a moving target while you shoot.
- **Recoil and spray control:** managing the weapon's pattern when firing continuously.

A useful way to think about it comes from a well-known model of pointing tasks called [Fitts's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitts%27s_law): the farther the target and the smaller it is, the longer an accurate movement takes. You cannot change how small enemy heads are, but you can shorten the distance your crosshair has to travel. That is exactly what crosshair placement does.

> **Tip:** Before you spend hours on aim trainers, record yourself playing a few matches. If most of your deaths happen while your crosshair is pointing at the floor or the wrong angle, your biggest gain is placement, not mechanics.

## Step 1: Settle on a sensitivity and keep it

Muscle memory is built by repeating the same movement with the same result. If you change sensitivity every other day, you reset that learning every time. Your goal is one sensitivity you can leave alone for months.

The simplest way to compare sensitivities across mice and games is **eDPI**, which is your mouse DPI multiplied by your in-game sensitivity. A mouse at 800 DPI with a sensitivity of 0.5 has an eDPI of 400. Different games convert sensitivity differently, so eDPI is only directly comparable inside the same game, but it is still a good way to track your own settings. We cover how to choose a starting point in our guide to [the best sensitivity settings for gaming](/game-settings/best-sensitivity-settings-for-gaming/).

A practical test for a good starting point:

1. Place your mouse in the middle of your mouse pad.
2. Move your hand to complete a full 180-degree turn in the game.
3. If you run out of mouse pad before you finish the turn, your sensitivity is too high for your pad. If the turn takes a large arm sweep, it may be too low.
4. Pick a value where the turn fits comfortably and small corrections still feel precise.

## Step 2: Fix crosshair placement

Crosshair placement means keeping your crosshair at the height where an enemy's head would be, and aimed at the angle where an enemy is most likely to appear. Players who do this need to move their mouse very little to land a shot.

- **Keep it at head height.** Most maps have flat ground. Walk with your crosshair at the level of an average player's head instead of looking at the floor.
- **Pre-aim common angles.** Before you swing around a corner or open a door, point at where an opponent would stand rather than at the middle of the doorway.
- **Clear angles one at a time.** Moving your view across a corner gradually shows you each position separately, so you only aim at one place at a time.
- **Reset after every fight.** After a kill, move your crosshair back to head height and toward the next angle before you do anything else.

Good placement is reviewed, not guessed. Watch a replay at normal speed, pause just before each fight, and look at where your crosshair is sitting. We explain a full review routine in [how to review your gameplay](/advanced-strategies/how-to-review-your-gameplay/).

## Step 3: Train flicking and tracking separately

Flicking and tracking use different movements, so they deserve different practice.

### Flicking

A flick is a single fast movement ending on a target. The common error is trying to move faster instead of trying to land more accurately. Start slow: aim for ten clean hits in a row before you increase speed. Many aim trainers have scenarios with static targets that appear one after another. Choose a scenario with medium-sized targets, aim for accuracy first and speed second, and stop when you get tired.

### Tracking

Tracking is steady, smooth movement while holding the crosshair on a moving target. It rewards a relaxed grip and consistent mouse control. Practice against strafing bots or aim-trainer scenarios where a target moves left and right, and watch for two common problems: **overcorrecting** (moving past the target and snapping back) and **lagging behind** (always a little late). Both improve when you slow down and focus on smooth, small movements.

| Skill | What it feels like | Typical mistake | Best fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Crosshair placement | Enemies appear almost on your crosshair | Looking at the floor or the middle of doorways | Review replays and pre-aim common angles |
| Flicking | One fast move, one clean shot | Moving too fast, overshooting | Slow down until accuracy is high, then speed up |
| Tracking | Crosshair glued to a strafing target | Overcorrecting or lagging | Lighter grip, smoother movement |
| Spray control | Bullets land in a tight group | Fighting the recoil with big movements | Learn each weapon's pattern with small pulls |

## Step 4: Build a short, repeatable routine

Short, repeated practice beats occasional long sessions. A good starting routine takes about 15 to 25 minutes:

1. **Warm up (3 to 5 minutes):** slow, easy target practice to get your hand moving.
2. **Flicking block (5 to 8 minutes):** one or two static-target scenarios.
3. **Tracking block (5 to 8 minutes):** one or two moving-target scenarios.
4. **Game-specific practice (5 minutes):** shooting range or practice server with the weapons you use.

We go deeper on this in our guide to [building a gaming warm-up routine](/gaming-tips/how-to-build-a-gaming-warm-up-routine/). Whichever games you play, you can also use their built-in practice tools: the shooting range in Valorant, or a local practice server in Counter-Strike 2, as described in our [Counter-Strike 2 practice guide](/game-guides/counter-strike-2-practice-guide/).

> **Important:** Stop a session when your accuracy drops sharply or your hand feels tense. Practicing tired builds sloppy habits and can lead to strain.

## Step 5: Make your hardware consistent

You cannot build reliable aim on an unreliable setup. If your frame rate swings between 60 and 140 FPS, your crosshair will feel different from one moment to the next. Check these basics:

- **Stable frame rate.** Aim for a steady frame rate rather than the highest possible number. Our guide to [increasing FPS on PC](/gaming-performance/how-to-increase-fps-on-pc/) shows how.
- **Low input lag.** Delay between your movement and what appears on screen makes tracking feel mushy. See [how to reduce input lag on PC](/gaming-performance/how-to-reduce-input-lag-on-pc/).
- **No mouse acceleration.** Turn off "Enhance pointer precision" in Windows, so the same hand movement always produces the same cursor movement.
- **A comfortable mouse and a good surface.** Your mouse should fit your hand and your pad should be consistent. Read our [buying guide for FPS gaming mice](/gaming-gear/best-gaming-mouse-for-fps/) to see which specifications matter.
- **A monitor that is not holding you back.** A higher refresh rate helps if your PC can reach it. See [best gaming monitor settings](/game-settings/best-gaming-monitor-settings/).

## Common aim mistakes to avoid

- **Changing sensitivity too often.** Treat a change as an experiment that lasts at least a few weeks.
- **Gripping the mouse too tightly.** A tense hand makes small, smooth corrections harder.
- **Only practicing what you are already good at.** Notice which scenario or situation feels worst and give it a few minutes a day.
- **Ignoring game sense.** Perfect aim cannot save you from taking a fight in the wrong position.
- **Practicing without a goal.** Write down one measurable target, such as "ten clean flicks in a row", and stop when you reach it.

## How to know you are improving

Progress in aim can feel invisible, so measure it. Keep a simple note after each session with the scenario, your score or accuracy, and how you felt. Every couple of weeks, compare. In games, track things like how often you win the first shot of a duel, or how often your crosshair is already at head height when a fight starts. The numbers will show gains long before they feel obvious.

## Key takeaways

- Keep one sensitivity long enough for muscle memory to form, and turn off mouse acceleration.
- Crosshair placement is the cheapest aim improvement: keep it at head height, aimed at the likely angle.
- Train flicking and tracking separately, accuracy first and speed second.
- Use a short routine most days, and stop when your accuracy or focus drops.
- Make your frame rate, input lag and mouse surface consistent so practice transfers.

If you do only one thing after reading this guide, record a match and check where your crosshair sits before each fight. It shows you where your next improvement is hiding.
