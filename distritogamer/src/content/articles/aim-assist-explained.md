If you have ever flicked a stick toward an enemy and felt the crosshair stick to them for a moment, you have met **aim assist**. It is one of the most discussed features in controller gaming, and one of the least understood. Players often assume it does all the aiming for them, or that it is unfair. In most games, the truth sits between those two views.

This guide explains what aim assist does, why developers include it, how it differs between games, and how to decide whether to keep it on, lower it or leave it alone. For the other controller options that matter, read our [best controller settings guide](/game-settings/best-controller-settings-for-competitive-gaming/). For the bigger question of which input suits your games, see [controller vs mouse and keyboard](/game-comparisons/controller-vs-mouse-and-keyboard/).

## What aim assist actually does

A thumbstick is a rate device. Pushing it further turns the view faster, and stopping it accurately takes practice. A mouse is a position device: the distance your hand moves is the distance the view turns. That difference is why controller aiming is harder for many players, especially on small or fast targets.

Aim assist helps close that gap, usually in one or more of these ways:

- **Slowdown:** the camera turns more slowly when the crosshair crosses a target, so it is easier to stop on it.
- **Magnetism or pull:** the crosshair is gently drawn toward a nearby target. This is the effect people notice most.
- **Tracking support:** the assist keeps the crosshair near a moving target while you hold fire, though the strength of this varies a lot.

None of these fire the weapon for you. You still choose the moment to shoot, and you still need to move the stick to bring the target into range. The assist only changes how forgiving the aiming feels.

The exact behaviour is set by each developer. Some games apply the assist only at certain distances, only when the target is not moving, or only for certain weapons. Others apply it differently for PC controllers and console controllers. The [Wikipedia article on auto-aim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auto-aim) gives a general overview of how the feature is used across games.

## Why developers include it

Aim assist exists for a practical reason. Mouse and keyboard players can make very precise aims that a stick cannot match, so developers add assistance to keep controller play competitive and enjoyable. It also helps players with limited dexterity, which is one reason many games offer it as an option rather than a hidden rule. Accessibility options like this are described in more detail in [video game accessibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_game_accessibility).

> **Note:** Because the design differs from game to game, the same setting can feel very different in two titles. Judge it inside the game you actually play.

## Is aim assist fair?

This is the question most people really want answered. A fair way to think about it:

- **Built-in aim assist is part of the game's design.** Matches with controller players are usually balanced around it, and turning it off in a game where everyone else keeps it on usually makes you weaker, not stronger.
- **Third-party devices or software that change aim assist are different.** They can break a game's rules, and they may lead to penalties. Stick to what the game provides.
- **Matchmaking rules vary.** Some games match players by input, some do not. Check the game's competitive information before assuming how your matches will be set up.

If you are unsure whether a method is allowed, check the game's official rules or support page before you use it.

## How to set it up

Most games with aim assist place it in the controls or accessibility menu. Look for options with names such as "aim assist", "targeting assist", "aim assist strength" or "aim assist mode". Not every game offers all of these.

1. **Start with the default.** Play a few practice sessions with the setting as the game ships it.
2. **Change only one thing at a time.** If there is a strength slider, move it in small steps.
3. **Test on the same drill.** Use a practice range or bot match, and track how often your shots land on the first try.
4. **Decide after a fair trial.** Give each setting several sessions before judging. Your first impression of a new setting is often biased by how unfamiliar it feels.
5. **Write the final value down.** This helps you restore it after an update or on a new console.

Adjusting strength is usually safer than switching it off completely. A lower strength keeps some help while letting you rely more on your own aim.

## When aim assist helps most

- **Against fast or small targets,** where the stick is hardest to control.
- **When tracking a steady target,** especially at range.
- **During fast turns,** when a stick can overshoot a target.

It helps less when you flick wildly across the map, or when an enemy stays behind cover. Aim assist does not replace good positioning. See our [strategy guide](/advanced-strategies/gaming-strategies-that-work-in-any-game/) for the decisions that matter more than any single aim tool.

## Common mistakes with aim assist

- **Turning it off to "prove" skill.** Most players end up slower and less accurate in matches where everyone else has it.
- **Relying on it to fix a bad setup.** A large deadzone or a sensitivity that is too high will cause problems that assist cannot solve. Check those first.
- **Judging it on one bad session.** Your aim changes from day to day, so compare over several sessions.
- **Using unofficial devices.** Check the rules before you buy anything that claims to change how aim assist works.

## Quick checklist

1. Find the aim assist option in the controls or accessibility menu.
2. Keep it on at the default where the game allows it.
3. Adjust strength only if the game lets you, in small steps.
4. Practise with the same setting you use in matches.
5. Record your final values and check after each update.

Aim assist is a tool for controller players, not a shortcut. Pair it with a comfortable sensitivity, a sensible deadzone and regular practice, and it will do its job without getting in the way.

## How aim assist interacts with sensitivity and deadzone

Aim assist does not replace your settings; it works on top of them. If your stick sensitivity is too high, the assist can make a target feel like it jumps. If your deadzone is too large, the assist may fire late because your input is not registering yet. Before you judge aim assist, check these basics:

- **Sensitivity:** choose a value where you can turn around quickly and still make small corrections. The assist works best when small movements are predictable.
- **Deadzone:** keep it as small as your stick allows without drift. A large deadzone delays every movement, which makes any assist feel weaker.
- **Response curve:** a gentler curve near the centre gives you finer control, which pairs well with assist when you are tracking a target.

Change one of these at a time. If you change sensitivity, deadzone and assist strength together, you will not know which change helped.

## A simple practice drill to test aim assist

A practice mode with bots or a target range is the best place to test aim assist. Use the same drill each time so the results can be compared:

1. **Warm up for about five minutes,** with slow, easy targets.
2. **Stand still and track a target that moves in a steady line.** Count how many seconds the crosshair stays on it.
3. **Flick between two targets at medium range.** Count how many first shots land.
4. **Repeat with a moving target at longer range.** Note whether the assist helps or whether you overcorrect.
5. **Switch the setting** (for example, lower the strength by one step) and repeat steps 2 to 4.
6. **Compare the numbers** after at least three sessions with each setting.

Write the results down, even in a simple table. Your memory of how a setting felt is less reliable than a count of hits.

> **Important:** Test in a mode that does not affect ranked standing. Changing settings in a live competitive match is risky, because you have no time to adjust and your results will be noisy.

## What to watch for in matches

Once you choose a setting, watch for signs that it is working well or badly:

- **Overshooting:** if the crosshair keeps flying past targets, the assist may be too strong for your sensitivity. Lower the sensitivity or the assist strength.
- **Late reactions:** if the crosshair does not respond until you have already moved past a target, check the deadzone and any input delay on your display. See [how to reduce input lag on PC](/gaming-performance/how-to-reduce-input-lag-on-pc/).
- **Fights that feel unfair:** if you are losing because the opponent seems to lock on, the problem is often positioning or information rather than the assist. Review the moment with our [review guide](/advanced-strategies/how-to-review-your-gameplay/).

## Aim assist and accessibility

Aim assist is also one of the options that makes controller play accessible to more people. Players with limited hand strength, tremors or reduced dexterity may depend on it, and it can be the difference between a game being playable and not. If you are choosing settings for someone else, or you have a condition that affects your hands, the assist strength and the layout options are worth exploring. A comfortable set-up is better than a competitive one you cannot sustain.

If discomfort, pain or numbness appears in your hands or wrists during play, stop and rest. This is general advice rather than medical advice; speak to a qualified professional if symptoms continue.
