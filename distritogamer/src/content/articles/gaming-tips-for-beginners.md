Starting a new hobby is easier when someone tells you what actually matters. Gaming has a surprising amount of unwritten knowledge: how to set up controls, what to ignore, how to practice, and how to avoid the habits that slow people down. These 15 tips are the ones we would give a friend on day one, whether you are on PC, PlayStation, Xbox or Nintendo.

If you want the wider overview afterwards, our [complete guide to gaming tips](/#pillar) goes deeper into skill, settings and mindset.

## 1. Choose one game and stay with it for a while

Hopping between games feels fun but slows learning, because each game has its own rules, controls and habits. Pick one that interests you and give it a few weeks. You will learn faster, enjoy the progress and find it easier to tell whether you truly like it.

## 2. Pick a game that suits how you like to play

Genres reward different strengths. A quick guide:

| If you like... | Try a game in this genre | What you will practice |
|---|---|---|
| Stories and exploration | Action-adventure or role-playing | Exploration, patience, resource choices |
| Teamwork | Cooperative or team-based games | Communication, roles, shared goals |
| Fast reactions | Shooters or fighting games | Aim, timing, positioning |
| Planning and puzzles | Strategy or puzzle games | Long-term decisions and planning |
| Relaxing creativity | Sandbox or simulation games | Building, experimenting, systems |

## 3. Learn the controls before the strategy

Open the controls menu and read it. Learn what each button does, and try the tutorial even if it feels slow. Many beginners lose time because they do not know basic actions like crouching, dodging or switching items quickly.

## 4. Adjust the settings that affect comfort

A few settings make a big difference for new players and cost nothing:

- **Sensitivity.** If the camera feels too fast or too slow, change it. See our guide to [best sensitivity settings for gaming](/game-settings/best-sensitivity-settings-for-gaming/).
- **Field of view (FOV).** A wider view helps in first-person games, though very high values can feel distorting.
- **Subtitles and text size.** Turn them on if they help you follow dialogue.
- **Motion blur and camera shake.** Many players turn these off because they reduce clarity or cause discomfort.
- **Accessibility options.** Many modern games offer control remapping, colorblind modes and other options. See [video game accessibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_game_accessibility) for an overview.

> **Tip:** Change one setting at a time and play for a few minutes before changing the next. That way you know what actually helped.

## 5. Keep your settings stable once they feel right

Muscle memory only forms if the controls feel the same from day to day. Once you find a sensitivity and layout that feel good, leave them alone for a few weeks.

## 6. Play at a difficulty that challenges you without crushing you

Too easy and you never learn; too hard and you quit. If a game offers difficulty settings, start on a middle level and adjust. There is no prize for suffering through a mode that is not fun.

## 7. Practice in short, focused sessions

Fifteen to thirty minutes of focused practice teaches more than hours of distracted play. Choose one thing to work on, such as hitting more shots or learning a boss's attack pattern, and stop when your focus fades. We explain this approach in [how to get better at gaming](/gaming-tips/how-to-get-better-at-gaming/).

## 8. Learn from losses

When you lose, ask one question: what is the one thing I would do differently? Writing that down, even in a sentence, makes the lesson stick. In online games, watching a replay of the moment you lost can teach more than ten more matches.

## 9. Use the practice tools the game gives you

Many games include a training mode, shooting range, offline mode with bots, or a safe area to experiment. These let you repeat the same thing until it clicks. Our guides to the [Valorant beginner experience](/game-guides/beginners-guide-to-valorant/) and [Counter-Strike 2 practice](/game-guides/counter-strike-2-practice-guide/) show how to use them.

## 10. Do not copy a pro's settings blindly

Professional players have different hardware, hands, habits and goals. Their settings are a starting point, not a recipe. Test them, but keep what feels good for you.

## 11. Avoid common beginner mistakes

Many new players change settings constantly, buy gear too early, skip tutorials or ignore how the game's economy works. We list the most frequent ones in [common mistakes new players make](/beginner-guides/common-mistakes-new-players-make/).

## 12. Be a good teammate and a good sport

Online play is more fun when people are decent to each other. Mute and report abuse, avoid blaming others, share information calmly and remember that everyone you play with was a beginner once. Good communication wins more games than frustration does.

## 13. Take care of your body and eyes

Long sessions can lead to eye strain, a stiff back and sore hands. Sit with your screen at a comfortable height, take short breaks regularly, stay hydrated and stop if anything hurts. Our [gaming desk setup guide](/gaming-gear/how-to-set-up-a-gaming-desk/) covers posture and layout. This is general advice; if pain persists, speak to a health professional.

## 14. Start with the hardware you have

You do not need an expensive setup to start. If a game runs poorly, our guides on [increasing FPS](/gaming-performance/how-to-increase-fps-on-pc/) and [fixing game lag](/troubleshooting/how-to-fix-game-lag/) show free ways to improve it. If you are deciding between platforms, read [PC vs console gaming](/game-comparisons/pc-vs-console-gaming/). If you play with a controller, check [best controller settings](/game-settings/best-controller-settings-for-competitive-gaming/).

## 15. Remember it is supposed to be fun

Improving is satisfying, but you do not owe anyone a rank. Set small goals, celebrate progress and play the games you enjoy. The players who improve most over the long run are usually the ones who keep playing because they like it.

## A simple first-week plan

1. **Day 1:** Pick a game, finish the tutorial, adjust basic settings.
2. **Days 2 to 3:** Play at a comfortable difficulty and focus on learning controls.
3. **Days 4 to 5:** Choose one skill to practice for 15 minutes before each session.
4. **Days 6 to 7:** Review one match or section you struggled with and write down one lesson.

By the end of the week you will know whether you enjoy the game and have started the habits that lead to improvement. When you are ready to go further, our [advanced strategies](/advanced-strategies/) and [game guides](/game-guides/) are the next step.

## What your first month might look like

Expectations shape whether you stick with a new game. Here is a realistic picture of a first month for a beginner who follows the tips above:

- **Week 1:** you learn the controls and the basic rules, and you probably lose a lot. That is normal. Your only goal is to understand what the game is asking of you.
- **Week 2:** you start to recognize patterns: enemy behaviors, common maps, typical mistakes. You begin to find a comfortable setup and keep it.
- **Week 3:** you pick one skill to practice and notice small gains. You might still feel clumsy, but you can describe what you are working on.
- **Week 4:** you have a routine, a stable setup and a clearer idea of what you enjoy. You can decide whether to go deeper into the game or try another.

Every player's pace is different, and nobody improves at the same speed. If you feel behind, compare yourself with your own first day, not with someone who has played for years.

## Setting up your first gaming space

You do not need a dedicated room or a pile of equipment. The basics are simple:

1. **A comfortable seat and a screen at a good height,** so that you are not hunching. See our [desk setup guide](/gaming-gear/how-to-set-up-a-gaming-desk/).
2. **A reliable internet connection** for online games. A wired connection is best if it is easy to arrange.
3. **Clear audio,** either from speakers or a comfortable headset.
4. **Enough space for your controller or mouse movements.**
5. **A habit of taking breaks.**

Spend money later, after you know what you enjoy and what limits you.

## Playing with others for the first time

Online multiplayer can be intimidating, but it is also where many players find the most fun.

- **Start in casual or unranked modes** where the pressure is lower.
- **Introduce yourself briefly,** and tell teammates you are new if you are comfortable doing so. Most players respond kindly.
- **Use pings and simple calls** if you do not want to use voice chat.
- **Mute anyone who is rude,** and report abuse through the game's tools. You do not owe anyone your attention.
- **Ask for feedback** from a friend or in a community, with a short clip and a specific question.

If you can find a friend who is also learning, practicing together is both more fun and more effective.

## Key takeaways

- Stay with one game for a few weeks, learn its controls and play the tutorial.
- Keep your sensitivity, layout and graphics settings stable once they feel comfortable.
- Practice in short, focused sessions and write down one lesson after each loss.
- Be a good teammate, take breaks and look after your eyes, hands and hearing.
- Start with the hardware you have and upgrade only when you can name a specific limit.

None of these tips needs money or talent. They are habits, and habits are the quickest route from feeling lost to feeling like you are getting somewhere.
