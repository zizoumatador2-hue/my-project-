If you only play, you improve slowly. If you play and review, you improve much faster. Reviewing your own gameplay is the closest thing to a shortcut that exists in competitive gaming, and it works in almost any game where you can record or replay your matches.

Most players skip it because it feels like homework. The method in this guide keeps it short, specific and useful. It builds on the improvement plan in our guide to [how to get better at gaming](/gaming-tips/how-to-get-better-at-gaming/) and the habits described in our [complete gaming tips guide](/#pillar).

## Why reviewing works

In the moment, you can only see what is on your screen and remember a fraction of what happened. In a replay, you can pause, rewind and look at the decision with calm eyes. That lets you spot patterns you would never notice while playing:

- You always peek the same corner the same way.
- Your crosshair sits too low when you move.
- You spend resources in the same wasteful moment.
- You take fights when your team is not ready.

Seeing the pattern is half the fix. The other half is deciding on one clear habit to change.

## What to record

Capture whatever your game and system allow:

- **Built-in replays:** many competitive games save recent matches that you can watch from any angle.
- **PC recording:** your graphics driver or operating system includes tools for recording the screen, and several free options exist. Check that recording does not hurt your frame rate; if it does, lower the recording quality.
- **Console capture:** most consoles let you save the last few minutes of gameplay.

If you cannot record at all, take notes right after a match while it is fresh: two or three lines about your biggest mistake is better than nothing.

## The five-step review method

### Step 1: Choose the moments, not the whole match

Do not watch everything. Skip to:

1. Every time you died or lost a round
2. Moments where you almost died
3. Moments where you had an opportunity and did not use it
4. Turning points where the match changed direction

Fifteen minutes on five moments is far more useful than an hour of passive watching.

### Step 2: Pause before the decision

Stop the replay a few seconds before the key choice and ask:

- What information did I have?
- What were my options?
- What did I choose, and why?

Then press play. Compare what you did with what you would now do. This is the heart of the exercise: **separating information from outcome**. A decision can be good and still fail, or bad and still work.

### Step 3: Write one line per mistake

Keep notes short so you will actually keep doing it. A simple format:

| Moment | What I did | What I should try next time |
|---|---|---|
| Round 4, lost duel | Peeked wide without information | Hold the angle and wait for a teammate |
| Round 7, no money | Bought alone | Buy with the team or save |
| Round 9, died in the open | Crossed without checking | Check, then cross with cover |

### Step 4: Look for patterns

After three to five reviews, scan your notes. Which mistakes appear most often? Count them. A pattern that shows up in every match deserves attention more than a one-off error.

### Step 5: Choose one habit to change

Pick the pattern that costs you the most and write a specific rule for it, for example:

- "I will not peek an angle without information from a teammate or utility."
- "I will move my crosshair back to head height after every fight."
- "I will not change my settings until the weekend."

Track the rule in your next session. This is how review becomes improvement instead of just awareness. Our guide to [aim improvement](/gaming-tips/how-to-improve-aim-in-fps-games/) uses the same technique for crosshair placement.

> **Tip:** Review right after a session, while the context is fresh. Reviewing a week later means you will remember less and miss more.

## What to look for in different games

The method is the same across games, but the signals differ:

- **Tactical shooters:** crosshair height, peeking habits, utility timing, positioning and economy decisions. See our guides to [Valorant for beginners](/game-guides/beginners-guide-to-valorant/) and [Counter-Strike 2 practice](/game-guides/counter-strike-2-practice-guide/).
- **Action and role-playing games:** how you approach enemies, how you use stamina and healing, and whether you rush. See [Elden Ring for beginners](/game-guides/beginners-guide-to-elden-ring/).
- **Strategy games:** build order timing, scouting, and when you expanded or attacked.
- **Fighting games:** what you did after blocking, which options the opponent used on you and which of your habits were predictable.

## Common review mistakes

- **Blaming others.** Reviewing teammates' mistakes does nothing for your own improvement. Focus on what you controlled.
- **Watching passively.** If you are not pausing and writing, you are mostly watching.
- **Trying to fix everything.** Pick one habit at a time.
- **Judging decisions only by outcome.** A good decision can still lose.
- **Skipping reviews when you are winning.** Winning streaks hide bad habits.

## Reviewing with a friend or coach

A second set of eyes catches things you will not notice. If you review with someone else, ask them for specific feedback on a limited question, such as "where was I out of position?", rather than "what do you think?". A friend who plays the same game is often enough, and many online communities offer replay-review discussions. Be open to hearing things you may not like.

## Building a simple review habit

1. After each session, tag your best and worst moment.
2. Once or twice a week, spend 15 to 20 minutes reviewing those moments.
3. At the end of the week, write down the one pattern you will focus on next.
4. Repeat for a month, and see how your notes change.

Pair this with a short pre-game routine such as the one in our guide to [building a gaming warm-up routine](/gaming-tips/how-to-build-a-gaming-warm-up-routine/). Together, a routine before and a review after create a loop of practice and feedback.

## A worked review example

Here is what a short review of a single lost round might look like, using the table from earlier.

| Moment | What happened | What I knew | Better choice |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:25 | I peeked the long corridor alone and died | A teammate had gone another way | Wait for a teammate or an ability first |
| 0:40 | I rushed to rejoin and was killed from behind | I heard footsteps but ignored them | Stop and check the sound |
| 1:10 | I used my last ability early | It was available | Save it for the final push |

Notice how each row separates what happened from what was known. The lesson is not "I should have aimed better," but "I peeked without information, and I ignored a sound." That is a habit you can change.

## Common patterns and what they mean

After a few reviews, you will begin to recognize patterns. Here are common ones and how to read them:

| Pattern | What it usually means | A first fix |
|---|---|---|
| You often die first | You are taking fights without information or support | Move with teammates and gather information before peeking |
| You lose fights you should win | Crosshair placement, positioning or setup | Review your crosshair height and check frame rate |
| You run out of resources at key moments | Spending patterns | Write a simple rule for when to save |
| You play worse after a loss | Tilt | Set a stop rule |
| You do well early and poorly late | Fatigue or overextending | Shorter sessions and a break between games |

These are starting points, not diagnoses. Use them to form a hypothesis, then test it.

## Reviewing team games versus solo games

In team games, it is easy to blame others. To keep your review useful, ask what **you** could have done to make the outcome better, even if your teammates also made mistakes. In solo games, such as action or role-playing games, review the moments where you took damage or died, and ask what pattern the enemy showed that you missed. In both cases, the question is the same: what information did I have, and what could I have done with it?

## Tools and habits that make reviewing easier

A few small habits make reviewing something you do rather than something you intend to do.

- **Name your clips.** A short note such as "lost round 7, peeked alone" saves time later.
- **Keep a single notes file** with dates, so that you can see patterns over weeks.
- **Review with a timer.** Fifteen minutes is enough, and a timer stops reviews from becoming long.
- **Review before you play,** not only after. A quick look at your last note reminds you of today's cue.

## Key takeaways

- Record or save your matches, and watch only the moments around deaths, lost rounds and turning points.
- Pause before each decision and write what you knew, what you did and what you will try next time.
- Look for patterns across three to five reviews instead of reacting to single events.
- Pick one habit to change, track it in your next session and review again.
- Review soon after playing, while the context is fresh, and keep the process short enough that you will actually repeat it.

A fifteen-minute review a couple of times a week will teach you more than extra hours of unreviewed play. The skill is not in watching footage but in asking honest questions about it.
