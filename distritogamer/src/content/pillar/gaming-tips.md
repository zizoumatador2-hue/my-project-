## What "getting better at gaming" actually means

Almost everyone who plays games wants to improve, but very few people can say what improvement is made of. They play more, they watch a streamer, they change a setting, and they hope something clicks. Sometimes it does. More often they stay on the same plateau for months.

The reason is that "skill" is not one thing. When you break it into parts, you can work on each part on purpose, and the whole thing improves much faster. In almost every competitive or skill-based game, ability comes from five layers that stack on top of each other:

1. **Fundamentals:** knowing the rules, controls, objectives and systems of the game.
2. **Mechanics:** the physical skills, such as aim, movement, timing and execution.
3. **Game sense:** reading the situation, predicting what will happen and choosing good actions.
4. **Mindset and habits:** staying calm, practicing well, reviewing honestly and playing consistently.
5. **Setup:** your settings, hardware and environment, which either support your skills or quietly work against them.

Most players who feel stuck are weak in only one of these layers, and they usually do not know which. A player with excellent aim but poor game sense will keep dying in the wrong places. A player with great game sense and a laggy setup will keep losing fights they should win. Fixing the weakest layer gives you more than polishing the strongest.

This guide walks through all five. It is a **pillar guide**: a broad map of everything that matters, with links to in-depth articles on each topic. You can read it from start to finish or jump to the section you need using the contents on this page. If you only have a few minutes, the 30-day plan near the end gives you a simple, ordered path.

> **Tip:** Skim the section headings first, then spend your time on the layer where you feel weakest. Reading about aim when your real problem is a 60 Hz monitor setting will not help you.

Plenty of gaming advice online is overconfident. It promises a secret setting or a single trick. We do not. ## Gaming tips for beginners: your first week

If you are new to a game, or to gaming itself, the biggest improvements come from simple habits rather than advanced techniques. Our full guide to [gaming tips for beginners](/beginner-guides/gaming-tips-for-beginners/) covers fifteen of them. Here are the ones that matter most.

**Choose one game and stay with it.** Moving between games every few days slows learning, because each game has its own controls, systems and habits. Give one game a few weeks, and you will understand far more than if you sample ten.

**Learn the controls and the menus.** Read the controls screen and play the tutorial, even if it feels slow. Many beginners lose hours because they do not know basic actions such as crouching, dodging, switching items quickly or pinging teammates. The settings menu often contains helpful options too, such as control remapping, subtitles, colorblind modes and sensitivity sliders. Our overview of [video game accessibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_game_accessibility) explains why these options exist and who they help.

**Play at a challenging but fair difficulty.** If a game offers difficulty settings, start at a middle level. Playing on an easy setting you never lose on teaches little, and playing on a setting you always fail on teaches frustration.

**Keep your settings stable.** Once your sensitivity, layout and graphics feel comfortable, leave them alone for a few weeks. Muscle memory only forms when the same movement produces the same result.

**Practice in short sessions.** Fifteen to thirty focused minutes teach more than hours of distracted play. Choose one thing to work on, such as landing more shots or learning one boss's attack pattern, and stop when your focus fades.

**Learn from losses.** After a loss, ask one question: what is the one thing I would do differently? Write it down. This takes under a minute and makes the lesson stick.

### The most common beginner mistakes

Beginners tend to make the same handful of mistakes, and fixing even one makes a visible difference. Our article on [common mistakes new players make](/beginner-guides/common-mistakes-new-players-make/) explains twelve, but these five cause most of the damage:

- Changing settings every session
- Skipping tutorials and the options menu
- Ignoring the game's economy or resource systems
- Playing while frustrated
- Buying expensive gear before fixing habits and settings

| Mistake | Why it hurts | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Changing settings constantly | Resets muscle memory every time | Keep them stable for weeks |
| Skipping tutorials | Misses core actions the game assumes you know | Do the tutorial once |
| Ignoring resources | Wastes money, health or cooldowns | Learn the standard spending pattern |
| Playing tilted | Leads to worse decisions and more losses | Set a stop rule and take a break |
| Buying gear early | Spends money without fixing the real problem | Fix settings and habits first |

If you are brand new, the best investment is simply time spent on fundamentals. Everything else on this page builds on them.

## How to improve your gaming skills with deliberate practice

Playing a lot does not guarantee improvement. Ordinary play mixes everything together: aim, positioning, decisions and communication all happen at once, so it is hard to tell which one cost you the round. Improvement speeds up when you practice **deliberately**: choosing a specific weakness, working on it with full attention, getting feedback and repeating.

The idea comes from research on expert performance, and you can read a summary of the concept in the Wikipedia article on [practice as a learning method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Practice_(learning_method)). In gaming, it looks like this:

1. **Pick one skill.** Look at your last few losses and choose the single thing that cost you most.
2. **Put a number on it.** "Win more duels" is vague. "Win the first shot of at least half my duels" is measurable.
3. **Practice it on its own.** Use a training mode, practice server, aim trainer or offline mode where you can repeat the skill many times.
4. **Review your play.** Watch your own replays to see what you actually did.
5. **Adjust your setup** so that practice transfers into real matches.
6. **Rest and manage tilt.** A tired, frustrated brain learns very little.
7. **Reassess monthly** and choose the next skill.

Our guide on [how to get better at gaming](/gaming-tips/how-to-get-better-at-gaming/) expands each step with examples, a sample week and answers to common questions.

### Build a warm-up routine

Musicians and athletes warm up, and so should you if your game demands precision. A warm-up does not need to be long. Ten to twenty minutes is enough to loosen your hands, find your rhythm, practice one skill and rehearse real situations. We describe a four-block structure, loosening up, easy drills, one skill block and game-specific practice, in our guide to [building a gaming warm-up routine](/gaming-tips/how-to-build-a-gaming-warm-up-routine/).

The key points are consistency and moderation. A short routine that you actually do every day beats a perfect one that you skip. Keep the warm-up slightly easier than real play, use the same settings as in the game, and stop before you feel tense or tired.

> **Note:** This guide offers general information, not medical advice. If you feel pain, numbness or tingling in your hands, wrists or arms, stop and speak to a qualified professional. See [repetitive strain injury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repetitive_strain_injury) for background.

### Review your own gameplay

Reviewing recordings of your play is the closest thing to a shortcut that exists. In the moment, you can only see what is on your screen and remember a fraction of what happened. In a replay, you can pause, rewind and look at each decision calmly. The method is short:

- Record or save your matches.
- Skip to deaths, lost rounds and turning points.
- Pause before each decision and ask what you knew and what your options were.
- Write one line per mistake.
- Look for patterns across several matches.
- Choose one habit to change.

We cover the process in detail, with a template for notes, in [how to review your gameplay](/advanced-strategies/how-to-review-your-gameplay/).

## Aim and mechanics: how to improve aim in FPS games

Aim is the mechanical skill people ask about most. In first-person shooters, it breaks into four parts that fail in different ways: crosshair placement, flicking, tracking and recoil control. Our complete guide on [how to improve your aim in FPS games](/gaming-tips/how-to-improve-aim-in-fps-games/) covers each in depth. Here is the overview.

**Crosshair placement** is where your crosshair rests before a fight starts. Keep it at head height and aimed at the angle where an enemy is most likely to appear. Players with good placement need very little mouse movement to land a shot, which is why it is often called the most underrated skill in shooters. A useful way to understand why comes from [Fitts's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitts%27s_law), which describes how the time to hit a target depends on its distance and size. You cannot change the size of an enemy's head, but you can reduce the distance your crosshair has to travel.

**Flicking** is a fast, accurate movement to a target that has just appeared. Beginners usually try to flick faster when they should try to flick more accurately. Practice slowly first, and speed will follow.

**Tracking** is keeping the crosshair on a moving target while you shoot. It rewards a relaxed grip and smooth, small movements. The most common errors are overcorrecting and lagging behind the target.

**Recoil and spray control** means managing a weapon's pattern while firing continuously. Each weapon in a game behaves differently, so learn one at a time.

### Sensitivity: choose one and keep it

The most important thing about sensitivity is not the number but the consistency. If you change it every other day, you reset your muscle memory. To compare settings within a game, use **eDPI**, which is your mouse DPI multiplied by in-game sensitivity. Our guide on [the best sensitivity settings for gaming](/game-settings/best-sensitivity-settings-for-gaming/) explains DPI, eDPI, the 180-degree turn test, zoom sensitivity and why you should turn off mouse acceleration. For background, see [dots per inch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dots_per_inch).

A short version:

1. Choose a DPI in the 400 to 1600 range and use the same in every game.
2. Turn off acceleration in Windows and in games.
3. Pick an in-game sensitivity that lets you complete a 180-degree turn with a comfortable arm movement while still making small corrections.
4. Write down the values and keep them for several weeks.

### Aiming with a controller

If you play with a controller, the equivalent settings are stick sensitivity, deadzones, response curves and aim assist. Start from defaults, change one thing at a time and use the **smallest deadzone that stops drift**. Keep aim assist on in games that allow it, because it is part of how controller play is balanced. Our guide to the [best controller settings for competitive gaming](/game-settings/best-controller-settings-for-competitive-gaming/) walks through each option, and our comparison of [controller versus mouse and keyboard](/game-comparisons/controller-vs-mouse-and-keyboard/) can help you choose between them.

## Game sense and strategy: playing smarter, not just faster

Mechanical skill gets you into fights. Strategy decides whether those fights are worth taking. Many players stuck at a rank have enough aim, but they keep making the same strategic mistakes: fighting without information, spending resources at the wrong time or repeating a plan the opponent has already worked out.

Eight ideas transfer across almost every genre. Our guide to [gaming strategies that work in any game](/advanced-strategies/gaming-strategies-that-work-in-any-game/) explains each one. In summary:

1. **Information before action.** Act on what you know, not on what you hope. Gather information cheaply before committing.
2. **Tempo.** Decide on purpose whether to speed up or slow down. Most weak play comes from accidental tempo.
3. **Resources.** Money, health, cooldowns and stamina are the game. The skill is spending them at the right moments.
4. **Position.** A good position gives you time, cover and options.
5. **Risk.** Take risks when the payoff is clear and a failure would not end the round.
6. **Adaptation.** Notice what opponents repeat and counter it, and vary your own pattern if you are predictable.
7. **Coordination.** In team games, simple coordination beats individual brilliance.
8. **Review.** Judge decisions by information, not by outcome.

> **Important:** A good decision can lose, and a bad one can win. Reviewing decisions rather than results is what builds real game sense.

### Learning a specific game quickly

General principles help, but every game also has its own systems. Here is a method for learning a new game quickly:

1. **Read or watch an overview** of the rules and objectives.
2. **Do the tutorial** and explore every menu once.
3. **Find the practice tools,** such as a range, training mode or offline mode.
4. **Learn the economy or resource system** before you try to optimize play.
5. **Choose one character, class or loadout** and stay with it for many matches.
6. **Review a few matches** to find your first repeating mistake.

## Best gaming settings: graphics, sensitivity, controller and display

Settings are the layer most players either ignore or obsess over. Neither extreme helps. The goal is a setup that is **clear, consistent and comfortable**, and then to leave it alone. Four areas matter.

### Graphics and FPS settings

There is no single best list for every game and PC, but there are principles. The goal in competitive games is a **high, steady frame rate and a clear picture**. In single-player games, you may prefer visual quality as long as the game runs smoothly. Our guide to the [best FPS settings for better performance](/game-settings/best-fps-settings-for-better-performance/) ranks graphics options by cost and usefulness. The headlines:

- Lower shadows, volumetric effects, reflections and ray tracing first, since they cost the most for the least visible gain.
- Keep texture quality high if you have enough video memory, because it uses memory rather than processing power.
- Turn off motion blur, film grain, chromatic aberration and depth of field.
- Use an upscaler such as DLSS, FSR or XeSS if your graphics card is the limit.
- Be careful with frame generation in fast competitive games, since it can add latency.
- Avoid traditional V-Sync in competitive play, or use adaptive sync with a frame cap slightly below your refresh rate.

### Monitor and display settings

A surprising number of high refresh rate monitors run at 60 Hz because nobody changed one Windows setting. Our guide to the [best gaming monitor settings](/game-settings/best-gaming-monitor-settings/) walks through the right cable and port, refresh rate selection, adaptive sync, overdrive, game mode, brightness, gamma and color. If you want background, see [refresh rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refresh_rate) and [variable refresh rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variable_refresh_rate).

| Setting | Recommended starting point |
|---|---|
| Refresh rate | Highest your monitor supports, set in Windows |
| Resolution | Native |
| Adaptive sync | On, with a frame cap a few frames below refresh rate |
| Overdrive | A clean middle setting without halos |
| Game mode | On, with extra processing off |
| HDR | Off unless the monitor and game handle it well |

### Controller settings

For controllers, the most important settings are deadzone, sensitivity, response curve and button layout, as described above. Check our [controller settings guide](/game-settings/best-controller-settings-for-competitive-gaming/) for a safe testing method.

### Do not copy a professional's settings blindly

Professional players have different hardware, hands, habits and goals. Their settings are a starting point, not a recipe. Test them, but keep what feels good for you.

## Understanding your hardware in plain English

You do not need to be a hardware expert to game well, but knowing what each part does makes every performance guide easier to follow and helps you avoid wasting money.

- **Graphics card (GPU):** draws the images you see. Resolution, graphics quality and effects such as ray tracing mostly stress the GPU. If your GPU is the limit, lowering those settings raises your frame rate.
- **Processor (CPU):** runs the game's logic: physics, artificial intelligence, the number of objects and players, and preparing each frame for the GPU. Competitive games running at very high frame rates, and large strategy or open-world games, often stress the CPU.
- **Memory (RAM):** holds the data the game and your operating system are using. Too little causes stutter and crashes, and running memory below its rated speed can cost performance in CPU-limited games.
- **Video memory (VRAM):** the graphics card's own memory, used mostly for textures. When you run out, stutter appears, and lowering texture quality one step fixes it.
- **Storage (SSD or hard drive):** affects load times and how smoothly open-world games stream data. An SSD is a worthwhile upgrade for the games you play most.
- **Monitor:** decides what you can actually see. A high refresh rate monitor shows the extra frames your PC makes, but only if it is set to that refresh rate.
- **Input devices:** your mouse, keyboard or controller turn your intentions into actions, and their quality and settings shape how consistent your play feels.
- **Network:** matters for online games. Stability and distance matter much more than raw speed.

The most important idea is that the slowest part sets the pace. That is why we say, in guide after guide, that you should find the bottleneck before buying anything. A faster graphics card will not help a CPU-limited game, and a faster monitor will not help a game that already runs below your current refresh rate.

### A quick way to find the bottleneck

1. Turn on an FPS counter and a usage display for your CPU and GPU.
2. Play a repeatable scene.
3. If GPU usage is near 100 percent, the GPU is the limit.
4. If GPU usage is well below 100 percent and a CPU core is busy, the CPU is the limit.
5. If both are low but the game still stutters, check memory, storage, temperatures and background tasks.

This takes five minutes and prevents most unnecessary upgrades. Our guide to [how to increase FPS on PC](/gaming-performance/how-to-increase-fps-on-pc/) goes through the rest.

## Gaming performance: increase FPS and reduce input lag

Performance problems make everything harder, and many players blame themselves for what is really a technical issue. Three topics cover most cases: frame rate, input lag and the operating system.

### How to increase FPS on PC

Low frame rates usually come from a short list of causes: the wrong settings, overheating, background programs, outdated drivers or one component holding the rest back. Our guide to [how to increase FPS on PC](/gaming-performance/how-to-increase-fps-on-pc/) gives twelve safe fixes in order. The first steps matter most:

1. **Measure** your frame rate in a repeatable scene.
2. **Find your bottleneck.** GPU usage near 100 percent means the graphics card is the limit. A low GPU usage with a busy CPU core means the processor is the limit.
3. **Update graphics drivers** and close background programs.
4. **Lower the most expensive settings.**
5. **Check temperatures.** Hot parts slow themselves down, called [thermal throttling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_frequency_scaling).
6. **Enable your memory's rated speed** (XMP or EXPO) and use an SSD for games.

### How to reduce input lag

Input lag is the delay between pressing a button and seeing the result on screen. The delay comes from a chain: your input device, the game and CPU, the render queue and graphics card, and finally the display. The biggest wins are usually:

- Raise and stabilize your frame rate.
- Cap your frame rate slightly below what your graphics card can sustain, so no queue builds up.
- Turn on a low-latency mode such as NVIDIA Reflex or AMD Anti-Lag when the game supports it.
- Avoid traditional V-Sync, or use adaptive sync with a cap.
- Use the monitor's highest refresh rate and its game mode.
- Use a wired or reliable low-latency connection for your mouse or controller.

Our guide on [how to reduce input lag on PC](/gaming-performance/how-to-reduce-input-lag-on-pc/) explains each in detail, including how to judge whether a change actually helped. For background, see [display lag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Display_lag).

### Optimize Windows safely

Most "Windows gaming tweaks" you find online are repeated, outdated or harmful. The safe changes are boring: create a restore point, keep Windows and drivers updated, turn on Game Mode, choose a performance power mode, remove unneeded startup apps, reduce background capture and overlays, schedule updates away from play time and keep storage healthy. Avoid registry cleaners and so-called FPS booster tools. Our guide to [how to optimize Windows for gaming](/gaming-performance/how-to-optimize-windows-for-gaming/) walks through the full list and explains what not to do.

## Troubleshooting: fix game lag, crashes and stutter

Few things are more frustrating than a game that misbehaves. The trick is to describe the problem precisely, because "lag" is used for at least four different problems with four different fixes.

| What you see | What it probably is | Where to start |
|---|---|---|
| Choppy visuals, even offline | Low frame rate or stutter | Settings, temperatures, background apps |
| Delayed actions online, smooth visuals | High ping | Network, server distance |
| Players teleporting or rubber-banding | Packet loss | Wi-Fi, router, provider |
| Controls feel heavy, even offline | Input lag | Frame rate cap, display settings |
| Sudden freezes then catch-up | Spikes or stutter | Test both network and PC |
| Crash at launch or at the same point | Corrupted files, drivers, mods | Verify files, update drivers |
| Random crashes after a while | Heat, unstable overclock, memory | Temperatures, defaults |

### Game lag online

For network lag, start with the cheapest, most reliable fix: connect with an Ethernet cable instead of Wi-Fi. Then pause other network use such as downloads and streams, restart your router, and choose the server region closest to you. If you still see problems, test for **packet loss**, because even small amounts cause rubber-banding. Our guide on [how to fix game lag](/troubleshooting/how-to-fix-game-lag/) walks through the whole process, including how to tell ping, packet loss and low FPS apart. For background, see [ping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ping_(networking_utility)) and [packet loss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Packet_loss).

> **Important:** Games use little bandwidth. A faster internet plan rarely fixes lag. Stability and distance matter much more than raw speed.

### Crashes and stuttering

For crashes and stutter, work in order: restart and update, verify the game's files, disable overlays and mods, check temperatures, return overclocks and memory profiles to defaults, check memory and storage, and read the crash information for patterns. Many modern games also compile shaders on first launch, so stutter in the first session is often normal. Our guide on [fixing game crashes and stuttering](/troubleshooting/fix-game-crashes-and-stuttering/) explains each step and the usual causes of each symptom.

Two habits will save you hours. First, change **one thing at a time**, so you know what worked. Second, write down what you changed. Both turn a vague, annoying problem into a specific, solvable one.

## Gaming gear: what actually matters

Gear can help, but it rarely fixes the problem you think it does. Before you spend money, ask whether your settings, frame rate and habits are in good shape. If they are, and you can name a specific limit, then a purchase might help.

**We have not hands-on tested individual products for these guides**, and we do not rank models or quote prices that may go out of date. Instead, our buying guides explain which specifications matter, what you can ignore and who each type of gear suits. Some pages include affiliate links, which we explain in our [affiliate disclosure](/affiliate-disclosure/). Affiliate links never decide what we recommend.

### Mouse

Shape and size matter more than anything on the spec sheet. A mouse that does not fit your hand causes tension, and tension hurts consistency. After shape come weight, grip style, sensor behavior, polling rate, switches and connection type. You can ignore headline DPI numbers, RGB lighting and extra buttons you will not use. Our guide to the [best gaming mouse for FPS](/gaming-gear/best-gaming-mouse-for-fps/) includes a grip-type table and a simple buying process. See also [computer mouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_mouse) for background.

### Headset

A headset has two jobs in a team game: helping you hear positional cues and letting your team hear you clearly. Comfort comes first, then sound clarity (not bass), microphone quality and connection type. Virtual surround sound is optional and does not help everyone. Please protect your hearing by keeping volume reasonable and taking breaks. See our [gaming headset guide](/gaming-gear/best-gaming-headset-guide/) for the full list of criteria.

### Monitor

A monitor with a high refresh rate and a fast panel can make motion clearer and more responsive, but only if your PC can produce enough frames and the monitor is set up correctly. Check your settings first with our [monitor settings guide](/game-settings/best-gaming-monitor-settings/).

### Desk, chair and posture

Your desk setup quietly shapes how long and how well you can play. A monitor that is too low strains your neck, and a keyboard too far away tires your shoulders. Most fixes are free: raise the monitor to eye level, keep elbows near 90 degrees, leave enough mouse room, light the room to reduce glare and take regular breaks. Our guide to [how to set up a gaming desk](/gaming-gear/how-to-set-up-a-gaming-desk/) covers it all, with references on [ergonomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_factors_and_ergonomics).

### A simple rule for buying gear

1. Fix settings and habits first.
2. Name the specific problem.
3. Find gear that solves that problem.
4. Check the return policy.
5. Give it two weeks before judging.

## PC vs console, and controller vs mouse and keyboard

Two debates dominate gaming forums: which platform to pick and which input to use. Neither has a single correct answer.

### PC or console

Consoles offer a simpler, fixed, usually cheaper way to play, with a curated library and a controller as standard. PCs offer flexibility, settings control, upgrades, input options, mods and a very large library, but they cost more to get right and require more tinkering. Our comparison of [PC vs console gaming](/game-comparisons/pc-vs-console-gaming/) goes through cost, games, performance, ease of use and online play. The short advice is to start with your list of must-play games, then check where each is available.

### Controller or mouse and keyboard

A mouse is a position device, a stick is a rate device, and that is why aiming feels so different. Mouse and keyboard usually offers faster, more precise aiming and many shortcuts, which suits shooters and strategy games. Controllers are comfortable and well suited to action, sports and racing games, and many games add aim assist to balance them. Both can reach a very high level. Read [controller vs mouse and keyboard](/game-comparisons/controller-vs-mouse-and-keyboard/) for a fuller comparison.

## Tips by genre

The principles above apply everywhere, but each genre rewards particular habits.

### Tactical and arena shooters

- Prioritize crosshair placement and counter-strafing, which means stopping before you shoot.
- Learn the economy and buy with your team.
- Use sound: footsteps and abilities tell you where enemies are.
- Learn a few reliable utility throws instead of dozens.
- Examples: our [Valorant](/game-guides/beginners-guide-to-valorant/) and [Counter-Strike 2](/game-guides/counter-strike-2-practice-guide/) guides.

### Action and role-playing games

- Learn enemy patterns before attacking, and strike after enemies finish an attack.
- Manage stamina, health items and equipment weight.
- Explore. Optional areas often give resources that make hard sections easier.
- Leave a boss that stops you, and return stronger.
- Example: our [Elden Ring](/game-guides/beginners-guide-to-elden-ring/) guide.

### Strategy and simulation games

- Scout before you commit, and expand your economy steadily.
- Avoid even fights, and look for favorable positions.
- Learn the interface and hotkeys, since speed of execution matters.

### Sports and fighting games

- Master two or three reliable tools instead of mashing buttons.
- Use training mode to repeat sequences until they are automatic.
- Review which of your habits were predictable.

### Battle royale and open-world multiplayer

- Plan where you land or start and what you want to do with the first minutes.
- Avoid unnecessary fights early on, and take fights where you have a positional edge.
- Manage resources and rotations deliberately.

## Transferable skills: what carries from game to game

One of the best reasons to learn the fundamentals is that a surprising amount carries over. When you move to a new game, you do not start from zero. Here is what transfers well and what does not.

| Transfers well | Transfers partly | Starts again |
|---|---|---|
| Practice habits and review | Aim and movement feel | Specific maps and characters |
| Information gathering | Economy and resource thinking | Weapon and ability details |
| Staying calm after mistakes | Positioning ideas | Menus and controls |
| Settings discipline | Team communication style | Meta and patch knowledge |
| Setup knowledge | Reading opponents | Exact timings and numbers |

This is why we spend so much of this guide on habits rather than game-specific tricks. A player who knows how to practice, review and stay consistent will improve faster in any game than a player who memorized one game's tricks.

### Reading patch notes without getting lost

Online games change regularly. Patch notes can look overwhelming, but you only need a simple process:

1. **Read the headings,** and skip sections about modes or characters you do not use.
2. **Look for changes to what you play,** such as your weapon, character or map.
3. **Note economy or system changes,** which affect how you play every round.
4. **Test the change** in a practice mode before ranked play.
5. **Do not overreact.** Balance changes are often small, and fundamentals matter more than the newest meta.

## How to practice when you have limited time

Many people have an hour or two to play, not a whole evening. You can still improve, and in fact short practice is often more effective.

- **Ten minutes:** a quick warm-up focused on one skill, then play.
- **Twenty minutes:** a warm-up plus a review of one moment from your last session.
- **Forty minutes:** a warm-up, a short play session with one cue to watch and a two-minute written note afterward.
- **One hour or more:** add a proper review of one match each week.

The key is to protect the pattern: warm up, play with a goal, note one lesson. Even a single sentence written after each session builds a record you can use later. Over a month, those notes show your habits more clearly than any statistic.

## Choosing games that fit you

Not every game suits every person, and forcing yourself to play a game you do not enjoy slows improvement. Think about what you like:

- **Do you enjoy fast reactions or slower planning?** Shooters and fighting games reward the first, strategy and simulation games reward the second.
- **Do you prefer teamwork or solo play?** Team games require communication, and single-player games let you set your own pace.
- **How much time can you commit?** Some games, such as long open-world role-playing games, ask for many hours, while others work in short matches.
- **What platform and input do you have?** Check that the games you like work well with a controller or a mouse and keyboard, as discussed in our comparison of [controller and mouse and keyboard play](/game-comparisons/controller-vs-mouse-and-keyboard/).

If you are unsure, try free games first, and use our [game profiles](/games/) to see which games we cover in depth.

## Accessibility and comfort options

Games are for everyone, and accessibility options are not just for people with specific needs. They can make a game more comfortable for anyone.

- **Control remapping** lets you move actions to buttons you can reach easily.
- **Toggle instead of hold** reduces strain for actions like crouching or aiming.
- **Subtitles and text size** help you follow dialogue and information.
- **Colorblind modes** make important visual cues easier to distinguish.
- **Camera and motion options,** such as reduced camera shake, field of view sliders and motion blur settings, help if some games cause discomfort.
- **Difficulty and assist options** let you enjoy a game at your own pace.

Check the options menu before you play. If a game has few options, you can often compensate with your platform's accessibility settings. For a broader overview of this area, see [video game accessibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_game_accessibility).

## Avoiding burnout and keeping gaming fun

The players who improve most over years are usually the ones who keep playing because they like it. Burnout is common when every session becomes a test. A few habits help:

- **Alternate focused sessions with relaxed ones.** Not every match needs a goal.
- **Take breaks from a game** if you notice you are dreading it.
- **Set goals based on skills, not rank.** A rank is a number that moves with your teammates and luck, while skills are yours.
- **Play with friends** when you can. Social play makes improvement more enjoyable.
- **Try other games.** Skills from one game often help in another.

If gaming stops being fun, that is a signal worth listening to. Improvement should serve enjoyment, not replace it.

## Mindset, tilt and sustainable play

Skill is built by sessions, and sessions depend on how you feel. Mindset is not a soft extra; it often decides whether practice happens at all.

**Tilt** is the frustration that makes you play worse after a mistake or a loss. A few habits help:

- **Set a stop rule.** For example, take a ten-minute break after two losses in a row.
- **Name the mistake, not the person.** Blame feels good briefly and teaches nothing.
- **Compare yourself to your past self.** Watching experts can make your progress look small, so track one number over weeks.
- **Keep some games just for fun.** Not every session needs a goal.

### Communication and etiquette

Online play is more fun when people are decent to each other. Share information briefly and calmly, avoid blaming teammates, mute and report abuse, and remember that everyone you play with was a beginner once. Good communication wins more games than frustration does.

### Take care of your body

Long sessions can lead to eye strain, a stiff back and sore hands. A commonly suggested habit for your eyes is the 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet away for about 20 seconds. See [computer vision syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_vision_syndrome) for background. Stand and stretch between matches, keep water nearby, protect your hearing and sleep well, because a tired brain reacts slower and learns less.

> **Note:** This is general advice, not medical advice. If you have recurring pain or strain, speak to a qualified professional.

## Common gaming myths

Plenty of confident advice does not hold up. These are some of the most common myths we see.

| Myth | Reality |
|---|---|
| "You need expensive gear to improve." | Habits, settings and practice matter far more. Upgrade when you can name a specific limit. |
| "Higher DPI means better aim." | Most players use a moderate DPI. Very high numbers add jitter, not skill. |
| "More FPS always means better play." | Frame rate above your monitor's refresh rate has limited use, and stability matters more than peaks. |
| "A faster internet plan fixes lag." | Games use little bandwidth. Stability, distance and Wi-Fi matter more. |
| "Pro settings will make me play like a pro." | Their hardware, hands and habits are different from yours. |
| "Aim assist is cheating." | In games that offer it, it is part of how controller play is balanced. |
| "PC is always better than console." | It depends on the games, budget and how much you like tinkering. |
| "Playing more is the best way to improve." | Deliberate practice and review beat sheer hours. |

## Measuring progress honestly

Progress in games can feel invisible, because ranks and win rates move with your teammates, your opponents and luck. If you rely only on rank, you can improve for weeks without seeing it, or get lucky and think you improved when you did not. A better approach is to measure the **inputs** you control and a few **outcomes** that reflect your skill.

| What to track | Example | Why it helps |
|---|---|---|
| A habit | Percentage of fights where your crosshair was at head height | Shows whether practice is changing behavior |
| A mechanical score | Accuracy or score in the same practice drill each week | Shows whether mechanics are improving |
| A decision rule | How often you followed your stop rule or buy rule | Shows whether strategy is sticking |
| A setup check | Average frame rate and 1 percent lows in a test scene | Shows whether technical problems are fixed |
| An outcome | First-shot win rate in duels, deaths while alone | Reflects real performance more closely than rank |

Keep it simple. A short note after each session, with a date, one number and one sentence, is enough. Review the notes monthly. You will often notice progress long before it shows up in your rank, and you will spot habits that no statistic reveals.

## Learning from others without copying them

Coaches, guides, streamers and communities can speed up your learning, as long as you use them well.

- **Watch with a question.** Before you watch a high-level player, decide what you want to learn, such as how they position on one map or when they spend money. Passive watching teaches far less than watching with a purpose.
- **Pick sources that explain why.** A guide that shows a trick without a reason makes it hard to adapt when circumstances change.
- **Check the date.** Game advice ages quickly when a patch changes things. Look for the published and updated dates, which is why we show them on every guide.
- **Ask specific questions in communities.** "Why did I lose this duel?" with a clip gets better answers than "how do I get better?".
- **Try one idea at a time.** Adding five new habits at once makes it impossible to know which one helped.
- **Be careful with paid products** that promise guaranteed rank gains. No one can promise that.

A coach or friend who reviews your gameplay can be valuable because they see things you cannot, but even then, the best advice is the advice you apply consistently.

## Practice plans by type of game

The structure of a good practice plan is the same everywhere, but the content changes. Use these as starting points and adjust them to your game.

| Game type | Warm-up focus | Skill block ideas | Review focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tactical shooter | Slow target practice, counter-strafing | Flicking, tracking, one grenade lineup | Crosshair height, peeking, economy decisions |
| Battle royale | Movement and landing routes | Aim under movement, looting routes | Landing choices, rotations, fights taken |
| Action role-playing | A familiar area to settle your hands | One enemy type or boss pattern | Stamina use, rolling habits, healing timing |
| Strategy | Building or economy routine | One build order or scouting pattern | Timing of expansions and attacks |
| Fighting game | Basic inputs in training mode | One combo or defensive option | Predictable habits after blocking |
| Sports and racing | A short, familiar session | One corner, move or set play | Decision timing and consistency |

If your game is not on the list, ask three questions: what does my hand have to do, what does my brain have to decide and what does my team need from me? Then design a warm-up for the first, a skill block for the second and a review for the third.

## A 30-day improvement plan

If you want structure, use this four-week plan. It works for most competitive or skill-based games.

**Week 1: Foundations**

1. Choose one game and finish its tutorial.
2. Check your settings: refresh rate, sensitivity, controls and audio.
3. Turn off mouse acceleration, and aim for a stable frame rate. See [how to increase FPS on PC](/gaming-performance/how-to-increase-fps-on-pc/).
4. Pick a sensitivity or controller setup and write the values down.

**Week 2: Habits**

1. Add a 15-minute warm-up before each session. See [the warm-up guide](/gaming-tips/how-to-build-a-gaming-warm-up-routine/).
2. Choose one skill and set a measurable target.
3. Set a stop rule for tilt.

**Week 3: Review and strategy**

1. Record or save three matches and review the moments around your deaths.
2. Write down your single most common mistake.
3. Apply one strategic idea per session: information, tempo or resources. See [strategies that work in any game](/advanced-strategies/gaming-strategies-that-work-in-any-game/).

**Week 4: Refine**

1. Compare your numbers with Week 1.
2. Pick the next skill to work on.
3. Review your gear and desk only if you can name a specific problem. See our [gear guides](/gaming-gear/).
4. Take at least one full rest day.

After thirty days, you will have a stable setup, a routine and a way to keep improving.

## Glossary of gaming terms

New terms can slow down learning. These definitions are short and practical, and many link to deeper explanations on this site or on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_video_game_terms).

**Aim assist:** a feature that helps controller players aim by slowing or following targets. See our [controller settings guide](/game-settings/best-controller-settings-for-competitive-gaming/).

**Bottleneck:** the component limiting your PC's performance, usually the graphics card or the processor.

**Counter-strafing:** tapping the opposite movement key to stop quickly before shooting, so that shots are accurate.

**Crosshair placement:** where your crosshair rests before a fight starts, ideally at head height and at the likely angle.

**Deadzone:** the area around a stick's center where movement is ignored, which prevents drift.

**DPI:** dots per inch, how many units your mouse reports per inch of movement. See [dots per inch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dots_per_inch).

**eDPI:** effective DPI, your mouse DPI multiplied by in-game sensitivity.

**FPS:** frames per second, how many images your PC produces each second. Also used for first-person shooters, so context matters. See [frame rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_rate).

**Input lag:** the delay between your action and the result on screen.

**Packet loss:** network data that never arrives, causing rubber-banding.

**Ping:** the time a message takes to reach a game server and return, measured in milliseconds.

**Refresh rate:** how many times per second your monitor can show a new image, measured in hertz. See [refresh rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refresh_rate).

**Shader compilation:** a process in which a game prepares graphics programs, which can cause stutter on first launch.

**Thermal throttling:** a component slowing itself down because it is too hot.

**Tilt:** frustration that makes you play worse.

**V-Sync:** a setting that syncs frames to your monitor's refresh to prevent tearing, but can add input lag.

**VRR (variable refresh rate):** monitor technology, called G-Sync or FreeSync, that adjusts refresh rate to match your frame rate. See [variable refresh rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variable_refresh_rate).

## Ten rules to remember

If you forget everything else on this page, remember these ten rules. They fit on a sticky note, and they cover most of what separates players who steadily improve from players who stay stuck.

1. **Fix your setup before you blame your skill.** A stable frame rate, correct refresh rate and consistent controls remove excuses and noise.
2. **Choose one sensitivity or controller layout and keep it.** Muscle memory is built by repetition, not by constant tweaking.
3. **Work on one skill at a time.** Trying to fix everything fixes nothing.
4. **Put a number on your goal.** If you cannot measure it, you cannot tell whether it is working.
5. **Practice short and often.** Twenty focused minutes a day beats one long weekly marathon.
6. **Review your own play.** Watching what you actually did is the fastest way to find repeating mistakes.
7. **Judge decisions by information, not results.** A good decision can lose, and a bad one can win.
8. **Learn the economy and the systems.** Resources are the game in almost every genre.
9. **Stay calm and rest.** Tilt, fatigue and pain all make you worse and make learning slower.
10. **Keep it fun.** The best long-term improvement comes from playing because you enjoy it.

These ideas are simple, but they are not easy. The difference is made by doing them consistently. Choose the one that you are worst at today, and start there.

## Final thoughts

Getting better at games is less mysterious than it seems. It comes from a short list of habits: a stable setup, one skill at a time, short and deliberate practice, honest review of your own play, calm decisions and enough rest to learn from all of it. None of that requires expensive gear or special talent.

Start with the layer where you feel weakest. If you are new, begin with [gaming tips for beginners](/beginner-guides/gaming-tips-for-beginners/). If your game runs poorly, start with [how to increase FPS on PC](/gaming-performance/how-to-increase-fps-on-pc/).
