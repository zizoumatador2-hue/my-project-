An individual retirement account (IRA) is one of the simplest ways to save for retirement with a tax break, whether or not you have a 401(k) at work. The big decision is **which kind**: a **traditional IRA** gives you a possible tax break today, while a **Roth IRA** gives you tax-free money later. This guide is part of our [personal finance hub](/personal-finance/) and fits into the bigger picture in [financial planning for beginners](/guides/financial-planning-for-beginners/).

*Figures below are for tax year 2026. Tax rules change, so confirm current numbers on IRS.gov or with a tax professional.*

## The core difference: pay tax now or later

| | Traditional IRA | Roth IRA |
|---|---|---|
| **Contributions** | May be tax-deductible | Made with after-tax dollars (no deduction) |
| **Growth** | Tax-deferred | Tax-free |
| **Withdrawals in retirement** | Taxed as ordinary income | Tax-free if qualified |
| **Early withdrawal of contributions** | Taxable, plus 10% penalty before 59½ (with exceptions) | Contributions can be withdrawn anytime, tax- and penalty-free |
| **Required minimum distributions** | Yes, starting at 73 (75 if born 1960 or later) | None for the original owner |
| **Income limit to contribute** | None | Yes (phases out at higher incomes) |

## 2026 contribution limits

For 2026, you can contribute up to **$7,500** in total across all of your traditional and Roth IRAs. If you're **50 or older**, you can add a **$1,100 catch-up contribution**, for **$8,600** in total. You also can't contribute more than your taxable compensation for the year.

A spouse without earned income can still contribute to a **spousal IRA** if you file jointly and the working spouse has enough compensation to cover both contributions.

## Roth IRA income limits for 2026

The amount you can put in a Roth IRA shrinks, then disappears, as your **modified adjusted gross income (MAGI)** rises:

| Filing status | Full contribution if MAGI is under | No contribution allowed at or above |
|---|---|---|
| Single or head of household | $153,000 | $168,000 |
| Married filing jointly | $242,000 | $252,000 |

If your income falls inside the range, you can make a reduced contribution. Higher earners sometimes use a "backdoor" Roth conversion strategy, which has tax rules worth reviewing with a professional.

## When a traditional IRA contribution is deductible

Anyone with earned income can contribute to a traditional IRA, but the **deduction** can phase out if you or your spouse are covered by a retirement plan at work. For 2026, if you're covered by a workplace plan:

| Filing status | Full deduction if MAGI is under | No deduction at or above |
|---|---|---|
| Single or head of household | $81,000 | $91,000 |
| Married filing jointly (contributor covered) | $129,000 | $149,000 |

If neither you nor your spouse has a workplace plan, your traditional IRA contribution is fully deductible regardless of income.

## Roth vs. traditional: a side-by-side example

Say you contribute **$7,500 a year for 30 years** and earn an average of **7% a year**. Either account grows to roughly **$708,000**. What differs is the tax:

- **Traditional IRA:** If you're in the **22% bracket** today, the deduction saves you about **$1,650 a year**. In retirement, every withdrawal is taxed. At a 22% rate, about $156,000 of that balance goes to taxes.
- **Roth IRA:** No deduction today, so it costs you the full $7,500 each year. In retirement, the whole $708,000 is yours tax-free if withdrawals are qualified.

If your tax rate is the **same** now and in retirement, the two come out about even (provided you invest the traditional IRA's tax savings). The deciding factor is whether you expect your rate to be **higher or lower** later. ## Which one should you choose?

### A Roth IRA often makes sense if you:

- Are early in your career and expect to earn more later
- Are in a low tax bracket (10% or 12%) today
- Want flexibility to access your contributions in an emergency
- Want to avoid required minimum distributions and leave tax-free money to heirs

### A traditional IRA often makes sense if you:

- Are in your peak earning years and a high bracket now
- Expect a lower income in retirement
- Need the deduction to lower your taxable income this year
- Earn too much to contribute to a Roth directly

### Not sure? Split it

Many people contribute to both to diversify their future tax exposure. You can also pair a Roth IRA with a traditional 401(k) at work, or vice versa.

## Withdrawal rules to know

- **Roth contributions** come out first and can be withdrawn at any time without tax or penalty.
- **Roth earnings** are tax-free and penalty-free in a **qualified distribution**: generally after age **59½** and at least **five years** after your first Roth contribution.
- **Traditional IRA withdrawals** before 59½ are usually taxed and hit with a **10% additional tax**, unless an exception applies, such as disability, certain medical expenses, qualified higher education costs or up to **$10,000** for a first-time home purchase.

## How to open an IRA

1. **Choose a provider.** Brokerages, robo-advisors and many banks offer IRAs. Compare account fees, fund expense ratios and minimums.
2. **Pick the account type**: Roth, traditional or one of each.
3. **Fund it** by transfer from your bank, ideally with an automatic monthly contribution.
4. **Invest the money.** Cash sitting in an IRA doesn't grow much. A low-cost target-date fund or broad index fund is a common starting point for beginners.
5. **Track the deadline.** Contributions for 2026 are allowed until the 2027 tax filing deadline, normally April 15.

## Before you invest: the order of operations

An IRA works best once you have the basics covered. A common order is: build a starter [emergency fund](/guides/emergency-fund-guide/), capture any **employer 401(k) match** (it's free money), pay off high-interest debt such as credit cards, then fund an IRA.

## The bottom line

Both accounts are powerful. The traditional IRA rewards you now and the Roth IRA rewards you later. If you're young or in a low bracket, a Roth is usually hard to beat; if you're in a high bracket now, a deductible traditional IRA can make more sense. Either way, starting early matters more than picking perfectly.
