Most budgets don't fail because of the rent. They fail because of the bills that show up once or twice a year — the car insurance premium, the holiday season, the vet visit, the new set of tires. A **sinking fund** turns those budget-breakers into small, predictable monthly amounts. This guide explains how sinking funds work, how they differ from an emergency fund and how to set them up in under an hour. It complements our guides to the [50/30/20 rule](/guides/50-30-20-rule/) and [building a monthly budget](/guides/how-to-build-a-budget/).

## What is a sinking fund?

A **sinking fund** is money you save gradually for a specific, expected expense. The term comes from corporate finance, where companies set aside money over time to repay a bond when it matures. For households, the idea is the same: rather than paying a $1,200 bill all at once, you put aside $100 a month for a year.

Three things define a sinking fund:

1. **A specific purpose** — "car insurance," not "general savings."
2. **A target amount** — what you expect the expense to cost.
3. **A deadline** — when the money will be needed.

## Sinking fund vs. emergency fund

Both live in savings accounts, but they do different jobs:

| | Sinking fund | Emergency fund |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Planned, predictable expenses | Unexpected events |
| **Examples** | Insurance premiums, holidays, car registration, school costs | Job loss, urgent medical bill, sudden major repair |
| **Timing** | Known or estimable | Unknown |
| **Target** | The cost of the item | 3–6 months of essential expenses |
| **Spending it** | Expected and planned | Only in a true emergency |

Keeping them separate matters. If you pay for the holidays out of your emergency fund every December, you don't really have an emergency fund. Our [emergency fund guide](/guides/emergency-fund-guide/) covers how big that safety net should be.

## Common sinking fund categories

Look through the last 12 months of bank and credit card statements and you'll likely find several of these:

- **Insurance premiums** paid annually or every six months (auto, renters, homeowners, life).
- **Car costs:** registration, inspection, tires, brakes, routine maintenance.
- **Holidays and gifts:** birthdays, weddings, end-of-year holidays.
- **Travel:** a planned vacation or a trip home.
- **Home maintenance:** a common rule of thumb is to budget about 1% of a home's value per year for upkeep.
- **Medical:** deductibles, dental work, glasses and contacts.
- **Annual subscriptions and memberships:** warehouse clubs, software, professional dues.
- **Kids and school:** back-to-school supplies, sports fees, camps.
- **Pets:** annual vet visits and vaccinations.
- **Big purchases:** a laptop, phone, furniture or the next car.
- **Taxes:** if you are self-employed, quarterly estimated taxes are one of the most important sinking funds you can have.

## How much to save: the sinking fund formula

The calculation is simple:

**Monthly amount = total cost ÷ number of months until it is due**

| Expense | Cost | Due in | Monthly amount |
|---|---|---|---|
| Car insurance (6-month premium) | $900 | 6 months | $150 |
| Holiday gifts | $1,200 | 12 months | $100 |
| Car registration | $240 | 12 months | $20 |
| New tires | $800 | 16 months | $50 |
| Annual vet visit | $360 | 12 months | $30 |
| **Total** | | | **$350 a month** |

If an expense is due soon and you're starting late, the monthly amount will be higher the first time around. Once the cycle resets, it drops to the steady annual rate.

For costs that recur but vary — like car repairs — use your average over the past two or three years. If you have no history, a modest starting amount (say $50 a month) is better than nothing.

## How to set up sinking funds

1. **List your irregular expenses** from the past year, plus anything you know is coming.
2. **Estimate the cost and due date** of each one.
3. **Calculate the monthly amount** with the formula above.
4. **Choose where to keep the money.** A separate, insured savings account works well. Many online banks let you open multiple savings "buckets" or nickname sub-accounts, so each fund has its own balance. Because the money may sit for months, a [high-yield savings account](/guides/what-is-a-high-yield-savings-account/) earns noticeably more than a typical savings account. Compare options on our [high-yield savings accounts page](/best/high-yield-savings-accounts/).
5. **Automate the transfers** for payday. Our free [budget calculator](/calculators/budget/) helps you see where the monthly total fits.
6. **Pay the bill from the fund** when it arrives — and start saving for the next one.

### One account or several?

- **Separate accounts or sub-accounts** make it obvious how much is available for each purpose.
- **One account with a spreadsheet** works too: keep a simple tracker with the target and current balance for each fund.

Either approach is fine. The key is that sinking-fund money is not mixed with your checking account, where it is easy to spend by accident.

## Fitting sinking funds into your budget

Sinking funds don't add a new expense — they spread costs you already have more evenly. In a [50/30/20 budget](/guides/50-30-20-rule/), the monthly amounts for needs (insurance, registration) sit in the 50% needs bucket, wants (vacations, gifts) in the 30% bucket, and longer-term goals in the 20% savings bucket.

If the total feels too large right now, start with the two or three biggest irregular bills, especially those that have pushed you onto a credit card in the past. Avoiding even one surprise card balance can save real money: carrying a balance at a typical card APR quickly costs more than the interest a savings account earns, as our guide to [how credit card interest works](/guides/how-credit-card-interest-works/) shows.

## Tips for making sinking funds work

- **Name the accounts clearly** ("Car insurance – due March") so you know exactly what each balance is for.
- **Round up.** Saving a little more than the estimate builds a cushion for price increases.
- **Review twice a year.** Update costs when premiums change or a goal is added.
- **Don't raid one fund for another** unless you rebuild it right away.
- **Celebrate the payoff.** Paying a big bill in full, with no stress and no card balance, is the point of the system.

Sinking funds are one of the simplest habits in personal finance, and they make every other part of your budget more reliable. Pair them with an emergency fund and a basic monthly plan, and irregular expenses stop being emergencies.
