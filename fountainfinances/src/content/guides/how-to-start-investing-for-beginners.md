Many people wait to invest until they feel ready, which can mean waiting for years. The good news is that starting is simpler than it looks. A small, steady plan built on a few low-cost funds will serve most beginners well. This guide walks through the order to follow, the accounts to consider and the mistakes to avoid. It is part of our [personal finance hub](/personal-finance/) and fits into the bigger picture in [financial planning for beginners](/guides/financial-planning-for-beginners/).

## Step 1: Build a cash cushion first

Before you buy anything, keep a basic emergency fund in a savings account. Three to six months of essential expenses is a common target, and a [high-yield savings account](/guides/what-is-a-high-yield-savings-account/) keeps that money easy to reach.

The reason is timing. If your car breaks down in the middle of a market drop, a cushion lets you pay the bill without selling your investments at a loss. Our [emergency fund guide](/guides/emergency-fund-guide/) explains how to size it.

## Step 2: Take the employer 401(k) match

If your job offers a 401(k) with a matching contribution, contribute at least enough to get the full match. A typical match might be 50 cents for every dollar you contribute, up to 6% of your pay. That is an immediate return on your money before any market gains.

Check your plan's summary for the match formula and any vesting schedule, which determines when employer money becomes fully yours.

## Step 3: Open a tax-advantaged IRA

An individual retirement account (IRA) lets your investments grow with a tax benefit. For 2026, you can contribute up to $7,500 across all your IRAs, or $8,600 if you are 50 or older, as long as your earnings cover the amount. A Roth IRA is often a good fit for beginners who expect higher income later. Compare the two types in our [Roth vs. traditional IRA guide](/guides/roth-ira-vs-traditional-ira/).

Once the IRA is full, a regular taxable brokerage account is the next step. It has no contribution limit and no withdrawal restrictions, but you pay tax on investment gains and dividends each year.

## Step 4: Choose simple, low-cost investments

Most beginners do not need to pick individual stocks. A few broad funds cover thousands of companies at once.

| Option | What it is | Why beginners use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Total market index fund** | Holds a large share of U.S. stocks | Broad diversification and typically very low fees |
| **S&P 500 index fund** | Holds 500 large U.S. companies | Simple, widely available, well-known benchmark |
| **Target-date fund** | Automatically mixes stocks and bonds and gets more conservative as a chosen year approaches | One fund, rebalanced for you |
| **Individual stocks** | Shares of a single company | Higher risk; one company's problems can hurt badly |

Look at the **expense ratio**, the annual fee expressed as a percentage of your balance. Many broad index funds charge well under 0.20% a year. Over decades, the difference between a 0.05% fee and a 1% fee is large.

## Step 5: Invest on a schedule

Set up an automatic transfer on payday and buy your chosen funds on the same day each month. This approach, often called dollar-cost averaging, means you buy more shares when prices are low and fewer when they are high. It also removes the need to guess when the market will rise.

## What your money could become

Here is a hypothetical example. Suppose you invest **$200 a month** for **30 years** and earn an average of **7% a year**, a rate used for illustration only and not a guarantee.

- Total contributions: **$72,000**
- Estimated balance at the end: about **$244,000**
- Most of the difference is growth from [compound interest](/guides/how-compound-interest-works/).

Try your own numbers with our [investment calculator](/calculators/investment/). Real returns change from year to year, and some years are negative. Treat the result as a planning estimate, not a forecast.

## Mistakes beginners make

- **Chasing hot stocks or trends.** By the time a stock is in the news, much of the gain may already have happened.
- **Paying high fees.** An actively managed fund with a 1% fee can take a large share of your long-term growth.
- **Trading too often.** Frequent buying and selling adds costs and often leads to worse results.
- **Selling in a panic.** Prices fall in most long periods. Selling after a drop turns a temporary loss into a permanent one.
- **Investing money you need soon.** Money for a home down payment in two years belongs in savings, not in stocks.

## A simple starter plan

1. Save a cash cushion of three to six months of essential expenses.
2. Contribute enough to your 401(k) to get the full employer match.
3. Pay off credit card debt with high interest.
4. Open an IRA and contribute monthly.
5. Put the money into one broad index fund or one target-date fund.
6. Review once a year, rebalance if needed and avoid reacting to news.

Your plan will change as your income, goals and tax situation change. Our [budget calculator](/calculators/budget/) can help you find the monthly amount to invest, and the [how to build a budget](/guides/how-to-build-a-budget/) guide walks through the steps.

## The bottom line

You do not need expert knowledge to start investing. You need a cash cushion, the employer match, a tax-advantaged account, a few low-cost funds and the patience to leave them alone. Start with what you can afford today, and increase it as your income grows.
