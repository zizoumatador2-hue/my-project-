Life insurance protects the people who depend on you if you die. The biggest decision when you buy it is the type: **term** or **whole life**. They solve different problems and cost very different amounts. This guide explains how each works, compares them side by side and shows how to estimate how much coverage you need. It's part of our [insurance hub](/insurance/); for an overview of every major policy type, see [types of insurance you need](/guides/types-of-insurance/).

## How term life insurance works

**Term life insurance** covers you for a fixed period — commonly 10, 15, 20 or 30 years. If you die during the term, your beneficiaries receive the **death benefit**, usually tax-free. If you outlive the term, coverage ends and no benefit is paid.

Key features:

- **Level premiums** for the whole term on most policies.
- **No cash value** — you're paying only for protection.
- **Lower cost**, especially when you're young and healthy.
- **Renewal and conversion options** on many policies, which let you extend coverage or switch to permanent insurance without a new medical exam, within limits.

## How whole life insurance works

**Whole life insurance** is a type of **permanent** life insurance. It covers you for your entire life as long as premiums are paid.

Key features:

- **Lifetime coverage** and a guaranteed death benefit.
- **Fixed premiums** that are much higher than term premiums for the same death benefit.
- **Cash value** that grows tax-deferred at a guaranteed rate, and possibly dividends with participating policies from mutual insurers.
- **Policy loans and surrenders** let you access cash value, with trade-offs: unpaid loans and interest reduce the death benefit, and surrendering in the early years often returns less than you paid because of fees and surrender charges.

Other permanent types include **universal life** (flexible premiums and death benefit) and **variable life** (cash value invested in subaccounts with market risk; regulated as a security).

## Term vs. whole life at a glance

| | Term life | Whole life |
|---|---|---|
| **Coverage length** | Fixed term (e.g., 20 or 30 years) | Lifetime |
| **Cost for the same death benefit** | Lowest | Many times higher |
| **Cash value** | None | Yes, grows tax-deferred |
| **Premiums** | Level during the term; rise sharply on renewal | Level for life |
| **Complexity** | Simple | More complex (dividends, loans, surrender charges) |
| **Best for** | Income replacement during working and child-raising years | Permanent needs and certain estate-planning goals |

## Why most families start with term

Most people need life insurance for a specific period — while children are growing up, while a mortgage is outstanding, or until retirement savings can support a surviving spouse. Term coverage matches that need at a price that lets a family buy **enough** coverage.

Because whole life costs much more per dollar of death benefit, families on a budget sometimes buy too little whole life coverage when they could have afforded a much larger term policy. Being underinsured is the bigger risk.

Some people pair term insurance with investing the premium difference in retirement accounts. That can work well, but only if the difference is actually invested consistently — and investments carry market risk that whole life's guarantees don't.

## When whole life can make sense

Permanent coverage can be appropriate if you:

- **Have a lifelong dependent**, such as a child with special needs.
- **Expect estate taxes or estate-equalization needs** and are working with an estate attorney.
- **Want guaranteed lifetime coverage** for final expenses or to leave a legacy, regardless of health later in life.
- **Have already maxed out** tax-advantaged retirement accounts and value guaranteed, conservative cash value growth.

If you're considering whole life, compare illustrations from several insurers, focus on the **guaranteed** values (not just projected dividends), and make sure you can keep paying the premium for decades. Lapsing early is the most expensive outcome.

## How much life insurance do you need?

A practical estimate adds up what your family would need and subtracts what's already available:

1. **Debts to pay off:** mortgage, car loans, credit cards, private student loans.
2. **Income replacement:** annual take-home income × the number of years your family would rely on it.
3. **Future costs:** college, child care, a spouse's retirement savings.
4. **Final expenses:** funeral and settling the estate.
5. **Subtract** savings, investments and existing life insurance (including coverage through work).

**Example:** a parent with a $250,000 mortgage, $70,000 of take-home income, two young children and $40,000 in savings might estimate $250,000 + ($70,000 × 15 years = $1,050,000) + $150,000 for college + $15,000 final expenses − $40,000 savings ≈ **$1.4 million**. A 20- or 30-year term policy is usually the affordable way to buy that much protection.

## Tips for buying life insurance

- **Buy while you're young and healthy** — premiums are based largely on age and health at application.
- **Match the term to your longest need**, such as when your youngest child will be financially independent or your mortgage will be paid off.
- **Compare quotes** from several insurers and check the insurer's financial strength ratings.
- **Look for conversion rights** on term policies in case your needs change.
- **Name and update beneficiaries**, and tell them the policy exists.
- **Don't rely only on workplace coverage**, which may end if you change jobs.

## The bottom line

For most households, life insurance is about replacing income during the years others depend on it, and **term life** does that job at the lowest cost. **Whole life** offers lifetime coverage and cash value, which can fit specific long-term needs if the premiums are comfortably affordable.
