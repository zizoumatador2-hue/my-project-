If you bought a home with less than 20% down on a conventional loan, you're probably paying **private mortgage insurance (PMI)** — often tens or even hundreds of dollars a month that protect the lender, not you. The good news: PMI isn't permanent. This guide explains exactly when PMI can be removed, how to get there faster and how FHA mortgage insurance differs. It's part of our [mortgage hub](/mortgage/) and builds on [how mortgage payments work](/guides/how-mortgage-payments-work/).

## What PMI is and why you pay it

Lenders generally require **private mortgage insurance** on conventional loans when the down payment is below 20% of the home's value. PMI protects the lender if you stop paying and the home is sold for less than the loan balance. The premium is usually added to your monthly mortgage payment, and its cost depends on your loan-to-value ratio, credit score and loan type.

## The three ways PMI ends on a conventional loan

The federal **Homeowners Protection Act** sets three milestones for most conventional loans made on primary residences since July 1999. All of them use the home's **original value** — generally the lower of the purchase price or the appraised value at closing.

| Milestone | What happens | Your action |
|---|---|---|
| **80% of original value** | You can **request** cancellation | Ask your servicer in writing |
| **78% of original value** (on the original schedule) | PMI must **end automatically** if you're current | None required |
| **Midpoint of the loan term** | PMI must end if you're current, even above 78% | None required |

### Requirements to request cancellation at 80%

To cancel at 80%, you generally must:

- **Ask in writing.**
- **Have a good payment history** — typically no payments 30+ days late in the past year and none 60+ days late in the past two years.
- **Be current** on your payments.
- **Show the property hasn't declined in value**, which may require an appraisal.
- **Certify there are no subordinate liens**, such as a second mortgage or home equity loan, if the servicer requires it.

## How long it takes: a worked example

Say you bought a **$350,000** home with **10% down** and borrowed **$315,000** at a fixed **6.5%** for 30 years. Your principal and interest payment is about **$1,991**.

- **80% of original value** is $280,000. On the regular schedule, your balance reaches that level after about **95 payments — roughly 7 years and 11 months.**
- **78%** is $273,000. Automatic termination happens after about **109 payments — around 9 years and 1 month.**
- **Paying an extra $200 a month** toward principal gets you to 80% after about **62 payments — roughly 5 years and 2 months**, almost three years sooner.

Your numbers will differ with your rate and loan amount. Our [mortgage calculator](/calculators/mortgage/) shows your amortization schedule and the effect of extra payments.

## How to get rid of PMI sooner

### 1. Make extra principal payments

Every extra dollar toward principal lowers your balance and moves the 80% date closer. Make sure your servicer applies extra payments to **principal**.

### 2. Use your home's increased value

If your home has appreciated or you've made major improvements, many servicers will consider cancellation based on a **new appraisal**. These rules come from the loan's owner (often Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac) rather than the federal law, and commonly require you to have owned the home for a minimum period — for example two or more years — with a lower loan-to-value threshold for shorter ownership. Ask your servicer for its exact requirements before paying for an appraisal.

### 3. Make a lump-sum payment or recast

A bonus, tax refund or other windfall can bring your balance below 80% in one step. Some servicers also offer a **recast**, which lowers your monthly payment after a large principal payment.

### 4. Refinance

If rates have dropped or your home has gained value, refinancing into a new loan at 80% loan-to-value or lower removes PMI. Refinancing has closing costs, so compare the total cost with the PMI you'd save. Our [refinancing guide](/refinancing/) explains the break-even math.

## FHA loans: MIP works differently

FHA loans don't use PMI; they have **mortgage insurance premiums (MIP)**: an upfront premium plus an annual premium paid monthly. For FHA loans with case numbers assigned **on or after June 3, 2013**:

| Down payment | How long annual MIP lasts |
|---|---|
| Less than 10% | **Life of the loan** |
| 10% or more | **11 years** |

Because MIP often can't be cancelled, many FHA borrowers remove it by **refinancing into a conventional loan** once they have about 20% equity.

**Other loan types:** VA loans have no monthly mortgage insurance (a one-time funding fee applies to many borrowers), and USDA loans carry an annual guarantee fee for the life of the loan.

## Lender-paid mortgage insurance

Some lenders offer **lender-paid mortgage insurance (LPMI)**: no monthly PMI, but a higher interest rate. Because the cost is built into the rate, LPMI usually **can't be cancelled** — only refinancing removes it. Consider how long you expect to keep the loan before choosing it.

## Step-by-step: requesting PMI cancellation

1. **Find the original value** (closing documents) and your **current principal balance** (latest statement).
2. **Divide** the balance by the original value. At 80% or less, you can request cancellation.
3. **Call or write to your servicer** and ask for its PMI cancellation requirements.
4. **Submit a written request** with any required documents or appraisal.
5. **Check your next statement** to confirm PMI has been removed — and that any escrow analysis reflects the change.

## The bottom line

On a conventional loan, mark your calendar for the month your balance is scheduled to hit 80% of your home's original value, and ask to cancel PMI then rather than waiting for the automatic 78% cutoff. Extra principal payments or a rise in home value can bring that date much closer.
