If you've ever paid your card in full and still watched your credit score dip, **credit utilization** is usually the reason. It's one of the few score factors you can change in a matter of weeks, which makes it worth understanding before you apply for a mortgage, car loan or new card. This guide is part of our [credit hub](/credit/) and builds on [how credit scores work](/guides/how-credit-scores-work/).

## What credit utilization means

Your **credit utilization ratio** compares how much revolving credit you're using with how much you have available. Revolving credit mostly means credit cards, plus personal lines of credit and some store cards.

> **Utilization = total revolving balances ÷ total revolving credit limits × 100**

In FICO's scoring models, **amounts owed** makes up about **30%** of the score, second only to payment history, and utilization is the heart of that category. VantageScore also weighs it heavily.

## How to calculate it (overall and per card)

Scoring models look at utilization two ways: across all your cards and on each card individually. Here is an example with three cards:

| Card | Credit limit | Reported balance | Per-card utilization |
|---|---|---|---|
| Card A | $5,000 | $1,500 | 30% |
| Card B | $3,000 | $900 | 30% |
| Card C | $2,000 | $0 | 0% |
| **Total** | **$10,000** | **$2,400** | **24% overall** |

Overall utilization is 24%, comfortably below 30%. But if Card B's balance were $2,700 instead, that card alone would be at 90%, which can weigh on your score even if your overall ratio looks fine.

## What ratio is "good"?

There's no single cutoff where your score jumps, but these ranges are a practical guide:

| Overall utilization | What it usually signals |
|---|---|
| **1%–9%** | Typical of people with excellent scores |
| **10%–29%** | Generally healthy |
| **30%–49%** | Starting to cost points |
| **50% and up** | Often a significant drag on scores |
| **0% on every card** | Fine, but sometimes slightly lower than a small balance |

The "under 30%" rule is a ceiling, not a target. If you're preparing for a big application, aiming for single digits gives you the most benefit.

## Why your utilization can be high even if you pay in full

Most issuers report your balance to the credit bureaus once a month, usually on or near your **statement closing date**, not your payment due date. If you charge $2,000 during the month and pay it in full after the statement arrives, the bureaus still see $2,000.

That's normal and doesn't cost you interest, but it does mean your reported utilization reflects your spending, not your payoff habits.

## 7 ways to lower your credit utilization

### 1. Pay before the statement closes

Find your closing date on your statement or in your card's app. Making a payment a few days before that date lowers the balance that gets reported. You can still pay the remaining statement balance by the due date.

### 2. Make two payments a month

Splitting your payment into mid-cycle and end-of-cycle payments keeps your running balance lower all month. It's especially useful if you put large recurring bills on a card.

### 3. Pay down the cards closest to their limits

Because per-card utilization matters too, bringing a maxed-out card down below 30% (or lower) can help more than spreading the same dollars across all cards.

### 4. Ask for a credit limit increase

If your income has grown or you've had the card for a while with on-time payments, your issuer may raise your limit. Same balance, bigger limit, lower ratio. Ask whether the issuer uses a soft or hard inquiry.

### 5. Keep old no-fee cards open

An unused card with a $5,000 limit still adds to your available credit. Closing it shrinks your total limit and pushes your ratio up. Use it for a small purchase every few months so the issuer doesn't close it for inactivity.

### 6. Consider a consolidation loan or balance transfer carefully

Moving card debt to an installment loan removes it from your revolving balances, which typically lowers utilization. A [balance transfer](/guides/what-is-a-balance-transfer/) moves debt between cards and can save interest, but it won't lower overall utilization unless the new card adds credit. Either option only helps if you don't run the old cards back up. Our [debt consolidation calculator](/calculators/debt-consolidation/) compares the costs.

### 7. Pay down the debt itself

The lasting fix is a lower balance. The [avalanche or snowball method](/guides/debt-payoff-strategies/) gives you a plan, and our [credit card payoff calculator](/calculators/credit-card-payoff/) shows how much faster extra payments get you to zero.

## A quick before-and-after example

Maria has two cards with $4,000 and $6,000 limits and carries $4,500 in total, an overall ratio of **45%**. She:

1. Pays $1,500 from savings toward the $4,000 card a few days before its statement closes.
2. Asks her other issuer for a limit increase from $6,000 to $8,000 and is approved.

Her balances drop to $3,000 against $12,000 in limits: **25% utilization**. When the next statements report, her score reflects the lower ratio. No new account was opened and no hard inquiry was needed.

## Common myths

- **"Carrying a balance builds credit."** It doesn't. Reported balances count whether you pay in full or not, and paying interest adds nothing to your score.
- **"My utilization from last year still hurts me."** In most scoring models in use today, utilization is a current snapshot. Newer models can consider balance trends, but lowering balances helps under all of them.
- **"0% is the best possible."** A small reported balance on at least one card can score slightly better than reporting nothing at all.

## The bottom line

Credit utilization is the fastest lever you have on your credit score. Track it overall and per card, pay before statements close when it matters, keep old cards open and work down balances over time.
