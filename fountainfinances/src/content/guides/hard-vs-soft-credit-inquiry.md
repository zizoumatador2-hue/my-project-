Every time someone looks at your credit report, it creates an **inquiry**. Some inquiries can lower your credit score a little; others have no effect at all. Knowing the difference helps you shop for credit confidently, and check your own credit as often as you like. This guide is part of our [credit hub](/credit/) and builds on [how credit scores work](/guides/how-credit-scores-work/).

## What is a credit inquiry?

A **credit inquiry** is a record that a person or company requested your credit report. Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, companies need a "permissible purpose" to see your report, for example, you applied for credit, they are reviewing an existing account, or you asked to see your own report.

Inquiries fall into two categories: **hard** and **soft**.

## Hard inquiries

A **hard inquiry** (or "hard pull") occurs when a lender checks your credit to decide whether to approve an application for new credit. It requires your permission, which you give when you apply.

Common triggers:

- Applying for a credit card.
- Applying for a mortgage, auto loan, personal loan or student loan (private lenders).
- Requesting a credit limit increase on some cards.
- Some apartment applications, utility accounts and cell phone plans.

Hard inquiries are visible to other lenders and **can affect your credit score**, because opening several new accounts in a short period is statistically linked with higher risk.

## Soft inquiries

A **soft inquiry** (or "soft pull") happens when your credit is checked for reasons other than a new credit application. Soft inquiries **never affect your credit scores**, and lenders reviewing your file cannot see them.

Common soft inquiries:

- **Checking your own credit**: through AnnualCreditReport.com, a bank app or a credit monitoring service.
- **Prequalification or preapproval** offers from card issuers and lenders.
- **Existing lenders** reviewing your account.
- **Employer background checks** (with your permission).
- **Insurance quotes** that use credit-based insurance scores.

## Hard vs. soft inquiry at a glance

| | Hard inquiry | Soft inquiry |
|---|---|---|
| **Caused by** | Applying for new credit | Checking your own credit, preapprovals, account reviews |
| **Your permission needed** | Yes | Not always |
| **Visible to lenders** | Yes | No |
| **Affects credit scores** | Slightly, for up to 12 months (FICO) | Never |
| **Stays on report** | Up to 2 years | Visible only to you |

## How much does a hard inquiry affect your score?

Less than many people fear. **FICO says a single hard inquiry typically takes fewer than five points off a FICO Score.** "New credit," which includes inquiries, makes up about 10% of a FICO Score: far less than payment history (35%) and amounts owed (30%).

A few details matter:

- **FICO only counts inquiries from the past 12 months**, even though they stay on your report for up to two years.
- **Thin files feel it more.** If you have few accounts or a short history, each new inquiry carries a bit more weight.
- **Many inquiries in a short time add up**, especially for credit cards, because each application is evaluated separately.

## Rate shopping: how multiple inquiries are counted

Credit scoring models recognize that smart borrowers compare offers. When you shop for a **mortgage, auto loan or student loan**, multiple hard inquiries within a short window are treated as **a single inquiry**:

- **Newer FICO versions** group inquiries made within **45 days**; some older versions use a **14-day** window.
- **FICO also ignores mortgage, auto and student loan inquiries made in the 30 days before scoring.**
- **VantageScore** groups similar inquiries within a **14-day** window.

Credit card applications are **not** grouped this way. Each card application is a separate hard inquiry.

**Practical tip:** do all your loan rate shopping within two weeks. That fits inside every common scoring window.

## How to limit hard inquiries

1. **Use prequalification tools first.** Many card issuers and personal loan lenders show likely approval and rates with a soft pull.
2. **Apply only when you need credit.** Space out credit card applications: several months apart is a sensible habit.
3. **Bundle loan shopping** into a short period, as described above.
4. **Ask before you agree.** Landlords, utilities and phone carriers vary; ask whether they run a hard or soft check.
5. **Keep a freeze in place between applications.** A [credit freeze](/guides/how-to-freeze-your-credit/) blocks new hard inquiries (including fraudulent ones) until you lift it.

## How to find and dispute unauthorized hard inquiries

You can see every hard inquiry on your free reports at **AnnualCreditReport.com**. Look for creditors you don't recognize. An unfamiliar hard inquiry can be an early sign of identity theft, because someone may have applied for credit in your name.

If you find one:

1. **Contact the company** listed to ask about the application.
2. **Dispute the inquiry** with the credit bureau that reports it (Equifax, Experian or TransUnion).
3. **If it is fraud, report it at IdentityTheft.gov** and consider placing a fraud alert or credit freeze.

Our guide to [reading a credit report and disputing errors](/guides/how-to-read-a-credit-report/) walks through the dispute process step by step.

## The bottom line

Check your own credit as often as you like: soft inquiries never hurt. Hard inquiries cost a few points at most and fade within a year, so they shouldn't stop you from applying for credit you genuinely need. Focus instead on the factors that matter most: paying on time and keeping balances low.
