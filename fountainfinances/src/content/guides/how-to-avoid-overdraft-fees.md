An overdraft fee can turn a $5 coffee into a $40 mistake. The good news is that most overdraft fees are avoidable with a few settings and habits, and some banks no longer charge them at all. This guide explains how overdraft and non-sufficient funds (NSF) fees work and nine practical ways to stop paying them. It is part of our [banking hub](/banking/); for broader account choices, see [checking vs. savings accounts](/guides/checking-vs-savings-account/).

## How overdraft fees work

An **overdraft** happens when you spend more than the available balance in your checking account. Your bank can then:

- **Pay the transaction** and charge an **overdraft fee**, leaving you with a negative balance; or
- **Decline or return the transaction** and, at some banks, charge a **non-sufficient funds (NSF) fee**.

Fees vary widely. At many banks a single overdraft fee has long been in the range of $30 to $35 per item, and several items in one day could mean several fees. Many banks have since lowered fees, added grace periods or eliminated overdraft and NSF fees entirely, so always check your own bank's current fee schedule.

Some banks also charge an **extended overdraft fee** if your balance stays negative for several days.

## Your rights under federal rules

Under Regulation E, banks **cannot charge overdraft fees on ATM withdrawals and one-time debit card purchases unless you opt in**. If you haven't opted in, those transactions are simply declined when you don't have enough money: no fee.

The opt-in rule does **not** cover checks, recurring debit card payments (such as subscriptions) or ACH payments like bill pay. Those can still trigger overdraft or NSF fees depending on your bank's policies.

## 9 ways to avoid overdraft fees

### 1. Opt out of debit card overdraft coverage

Check your account settings or call your bank to confirm you are **not** opted in to overdraft coverage for ATM and everyday debit card transactions. A declined card at the register is inconvenient; a $35 fee is worse.

### 2. Turn on low-balance alerts

Most banks let you set a text or app alert when your balance drops below an amount you choose: say $100. Add alerts for large withdrawals and for every deposit so you always know where you stand.

### 3. Link a savings account for overdraft transfers

**Overdraft protection transfers** move money from a linked savings account to cover a shortfall. Many banks charge nothing for the transfer, and others charge much less than an overdraft fee. Keeping a few hundred dollars in a linked savings account (ideally separate from your [emergency fund](/guides/emergency-fund-guide/)) is a cheap insurance policy.

### 4. Keep a buffer in checking

Treat a small amount ($100 to $200, or more if your bills are large) as zero. If your "real" balance never dips below the buffer, timing mismatches between deposits and payments won't cause overdrafts.

### 5. Track pending transactions, not just the balance

Card holds, pending payments and deposits that haven't cleared yet all affect your **available balance**. Check the available figure in your banking app before making a large purchase.

### 6. Time your bills around payday

If your paycheck arrives on the 1st and 15th, schedule automatic payments a few days after each payday. Most billers let you change your due date.

### 7. Use a budget for irregular bills

Annual premiums, subscriptions and other irregular costs are a common cause of surprise overdrafts. A simple [monthly budget](/guides/how-to-build-a-budget/) plus [sinking funds](/guides/sinking-funds/) for irregular expenses keeps them from catching you off guard.

### 8. Choose an account with no overdraft fees

Many online banks, credit unions and large banks now offer checking accounts that **don't charge overdraft or NSF fees**, or that simply decline transactions that would overdraw the account. Compare options on our [checking accounts comparison](/best/checking-accounts/), and read each account's fee schedule before opening it.

### 9. Ask for a refund

If you are charged a fee, **ask for it to be waived**. Banks frequently refund a first overdraft fee as a courtesy, especially for customers in good standing. Be polite, explain the circumstances and ask directly.

## Overdraft protection options compared

| Option | How it works | Typical cost |
|---|---|---|
| No coverage (decline) | Transaction is declined if funds are short | Usually free for debit/ATM; check your bank's NSF policy for other items |
| Linked savings transfer | Bank moves money from savings to checking | Often free or a small transfer fee |
| Linked credit card or line of credit | Shortfall is covered by borrowing | Interest and possibly a fee; can be expensive |
| Standard overdraft coverage | Bank pays the item | An overdraft fee per item, unless your bank has eliminated them |

## What to do if you overdraw your account

1. **Deposit money as soon as possible** to bring the balance positive and avoid extended fees.
2. **Pause automatic payments** that might hit while the balance is negative.
3. **Ask for a fee refund.**
4. **Find the cause** (a timing problem, a forgotten subscription, an irregular bill) and fix it with one of the strategies above.

Unpaid negative balances can lead the bank to close the account and report it to checking account reporting agencies such as ChexSystems, which can make it harder to open a new account. If your account is closed, look for "second chance" checking accounts.

## The bottom line

Most overdraft fees come from a handful of fixable causes. Opt out of debit card overdraft coverage, set alerts, keep a small cushion and link a savings account, or choose a bank that doesn't charge overdraft fees at all. Then put the money you save to work in a [high-yield savings account](/guides/what-is-a-high-yield-savings-account/) instead of paying it to your bank.
