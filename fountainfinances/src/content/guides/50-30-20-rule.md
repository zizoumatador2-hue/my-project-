The **50/30/20 rule** is one of the simplest ways to build a budget: instead of tracking dozens of categories, you divide your take-home pay into just three buckets. This guide explains how the rule works, what goes in each bucket, and how to adjust it when real life doesn't fit the percentages. It builds on our step-by-step [guide to creating a budget](/guides/how-to-build-a-budget/) and the budgeting section of our [complete personal finance guide](/#personal-finance-guide).

## How the 50/30/20 rule works

Start with your **monthly take-home pay** — the money that reaches your bank account after federal and state income tax, Social Security, Medicare and any payroll deductions. Then split it three ways:

| Bucket | Share | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| **Needs** | 50% | Rent or mortgage, utilities, groceries, insurance, transportation to work, child care, minimum debt payments |
| **Wants** | 30% | Restaurants, streaming and subscriptions, travel, hobbies, upgrades beyond the basics |
| **Savings & debt** | 20% | Emergency fund, retirement saving, other goals, payments above the minimum on debt |

The appeal is that you only need to watch three numbers. As long as needs and wants stay inside their limits and the savings transfer happens every month, the details can take care of themselves.

## What counts as a need

A **need** is an expense you must pay to stay housed, healthy, employed and in good standing with lenders. A useful test: if you stopped paying it, would something serious happen within a month or two — an eviction notice, a shut-off, a late fee on your credit report, losing the ability to get to work?

Typical needs include:

- **Housing:** rent or mortgage payment, renters or homeowners insurance, property taxes paid through escrow.
- **Utilities:** electricity, water, heat, a basic phone plan and internet you need for work or school.
- **Food at home:** groceries, not restaurant meals.
- **Transportation:** car payment, auto insurance, gas, maintenance, or public transit.
- **Health:** health insurance premiums, prescriptions and regular medical costs.
- **Minimum payments** on credit cards, student loans, auto loans and personal loans.
- **Child care** and child support.

The *basic version* of a need is the need; the upgrade is a want. Groceries are a need, but a premium meal-kit subscription is mostly a want. A reliable car is a need, but the difference between a modest payment and a luxury payment sits closer to the wants bucket.

## What counts as a want

**Wants** are the things that make life enjoyable but that you could pause without real hardship: dining out and takeout, coffee shops, entertainment and streaming services, vacations, gym memberships you don't strictly need, new clothes beyond replacements, hobbies, gifts and gadgets.

Thirty percent is a generous allowance on purpose. A budget that leaves no room for enjoyment is hard to keep, and a budget you abandon in February helps no one. The rule gives you permission to spend on wants — as long as it stays within the limit.

## What goes in the 20% savings bucket

The final 20% is what builds your financial future. In a typical order of priority:

1. **A starter emergency fund** of $1,000 to one month of expenses, kept in a separate, insured savings account. Our [emergency fund guide](/guides/emergency-fund-guide/) explains how much you eventually need.
2. **Retirement contributions up to any employer match**, since a match is an immediate return on your money.
3. **Extra payments on high-interest debt**, especially credit cards. See [avalanche vs. snowball payoff strategies](/guides/debt-payoff-strategies/).
4. **A full emergency fund** of three to six months of essential expenses.
5. **Other goals:** a down payment, a car replacement fund, education or additional retirement saving.

If 401(k) contributions are already deducted from your paycheck, you can count them toward the 20% (add them back to your take-home figure first so the math stays consistent).

## 50/30/20 budget examples

Here is how the rule plays out at three monthly take-home incomes:

| Monthly take-home pay | Needs (50%) | Wants (30%) | Savings & debt (20%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $3,000 | $1,500 | $900 | $600 |
| $5,000 | $2,500 | $1,500 | $1,000 |
| $8,000 | $4,000 | $2,400 | $1,600 |

**Example: $5,000 a month.** Suppose rent is $1,600, utilities and phone $250, groceries $500, car insurance and gas $300 and a student loan minimum of $150. Needs total **$2,800** — 56% of income, $300 over target. The fix doesn't have to come from savings: trimming restaurants and subscriptions from $1,500 to $1,200 keeps wants at 24% and protects the $1,000 savings transfer. ## When to adjust the percentages

The 50/30/20 split is a guideline. Common, sensible variations include:

- **60/20/20** — for high-rent cities where needs simply cost more. Wants absorb the difference.
- **60/30/10** — a temporary setting while you get through a tight period. Raise savings again as soon as you can.
- **50/20/30** or **40/30/30** — when you are paying off credit cards aggressively or catching up on retirement.
- **80/20** — a simplified version: save 20% first and spend the rest however you like.

What matters most is the direction: try to keep fixed costs from creeping up, and nudge the savings share higher each time your income rises.

## Pros and cons of the 50/30/20 rule

**Pros**

- Easy to start: three numbers instead of a detailed spreadsheet.
- Builds in saving every month.
- Leaves room for enjoyment, which makes it easier to stick with.
- Works well alongside automatic transfers.

**Cons**

- In high-cost areas, needs can't realistically fit in 50%.
- Classifying expenses as needs or wants can be subjective.
- It doesn't tell you *which* goal to fund first — you still need priorities.
- People with big debts or late retirement starts may need to save more than 20%.

If you prefer more control, a zero-based budget gives every dollar a specific job. If you have irregular expenses such as annual insurance premiums or holidays, add [sinking funds](/guides/sinking-funds/) on top of the 50/30/20 split so those costs don't blow up a single month.

## How to make the rule stick

- **Pay yourself first.** Schedule the savings transfer for the day your paycheck arrives so it happens before spending does.
- **Use separate accounts.** Keep bills in checking, savings in a separate [high-yield savings account](/guides/what-is-a-high-yield-savings-account/), and consider a dedicated card or account for wants.
- **Check in monthly.** A ten-minute review of your bank and card statements is enough to see whether each bucket is on track.
- **Revisit after changes.** A raise, a move, a new baby or a paid-off loan all change the numbers — update your split when they happen.

The 50/30/20 rule won't make every financial decision for you, but it turns "I should budget" into a plan you can set up in an afternoon. Once it's running, the [guide to saving money every month](/guides/how-to-save-money-every-month/) has practical ways to free up more room in each bucket.
