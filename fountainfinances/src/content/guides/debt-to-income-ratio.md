Before a lender approves a mortgage, car loan or personal loan, it asks a simple question: how much of your income is already spoken for? Your **debt-to-income ratio (DTI)** answers it. Knowing yours ahead of time tells you how much you can realistically borrow and what to fix before you apply. This guide is part of our [loans hub](/loans/) and pairs with [how much house can I afford](/guides/how-much-house-can-i-afford/).

## The DTI formula

> **DTI = total monthly debt payments ÷ gross monthly income × 100**

**Gross monthly income** is what you earn before taxes and payroll deductions. If you're salaried, divide your annual salary by 12. If your income varies, lenders typically average the last two years of documented earnings.

## Front-end vs. back-end DTI

Mortgage lenders look at two versions:

| Ratio | What's included | Classic guideline |
|---|---|---|
| **Front-end (housing) DTI** | Proposed mortgage principal and interest, property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance and HOA dues | 28% or less |
| **Back-end (total) DTI** | Housing costs **plus** all other monthly debt payments | 36% or less |

Together these form the well-known **28/36 rule**. It's a useful starting point, but lenders' actual limits depend on the loan program, your credit score, your savings and other factors.

## What counts (and what doesn't)

**Included:**

- Rent, or the full proposed mortgage payment for a home purchase
- Minimum payments on credit cards (the minimum, not your full balance)
- Auto loan and lease payments
- Student loan payments
- Personal loans and buy now, pay later plans that report
- Court-ordered child support or alimony

**Usually not included:**

- Groceries, utilities, phone and internet
- Car and health insurance
- Commuting, childcare and entertainment
- Retirement contributions and income taxes

That's why a DTI that looks acceptable to a lender can still feel tight in real life. Use our [budget calculator](/calculators/budget/) to see the full picture.

## A worked example

Jordan earns **$72,000 a year**, or **$6,000 a month** gross, and is shopping for a home.

| Monthly payment | Amount |
|---|---|
| Proposed mortgage (PITI + PMI) | $1,600 |
| Car loan | $400 |
| Student loans | $250 |
| Credit card minimums | $100 |
| **Total debt payments** | **$2,350** |

- **Front-end DTI:** $1,600 ÷ $6,000 = **26.7%**
- **Back-end DTI:** $2,350 ÷ $6,000 = **39.2%**

Jordan's housing ratio fits the 28% guideline, but total DTI is above 36%. Many conventional lenders would still approve this with good credit and savings. Paying off the $100-a-month card balance before applying would bring back-end DTI down to 37.5%.

## What DTI lenders accept

Limits vary by lender and by the strength of your whole application. As a general picture:

| Loan type | Typical back-end DTI range |
|---|---|
| Conventional mortgage | Often up to 45%, and up to 50% with automated underwriting approval and strong compensating factors |
| FHA mortgage | Commonly 43%, higher with compensating factors |
| VA mortgage | 41% is a guideline; residual income also matters |
| Personal loans and auto loans | Varies widely; many lenders prefer under 36% to 40% |

**Compensating factors** that help a higher DTI include a strong credit score, a larger down payment and cash reserves left over after closing.

## How to lower your DTI

### Reduce the debt side

- **Pay off small balances completely.** Eliminating a whole monthly payment does more for DTI than paying down part of a large one.
- **Avoid new debt before applying.** A new car payment months before a mortgage application can shrink how much you qualify for.
- **Refinance or consolidate carefully.** A longer term lowers the monthly payment, which lowers DTI, but may raise the total interest you pay. Compare options with our [debt consolidation calculator](/calculators/debt-consolidation/).
- **Look into income-driven repayment** for federal student loans if your payment is high relative to income. See [student loan repayment options](/guides/student-loan-repayment-options/).

### Increase the income side

- **Document all regular income**, including bonuses, overtime, side work and alimony received, if you have the required history.
- **Add a co-borrower** whose income and debts will both be counted.

### Adjust the loan

- **Choose a less expensive home** or make a larger down payment to lower the proposed housing payment. Our [home affordability calculator](/calculators/home-affordability/) shows how price and down payment change the payment.

## DTI and your credit score

DTI isn't part of your credit score, because credit reports don't include income. But the debts behind it show up in your score through [credit utilization](/guides/credit-utilization-ratio/) and amounts owed. Lowering card balances often improves both your DTI and your score at the same time.

## The bottom line

Calculate your DTI before you apply for any major loan. Aim for 36% or less if you can, know that many programs stretch further, and remember that a lender's maximum isn't the same as a comfortable payment.
