Before a lender approves a personal loan, it is answering two questions: how likely you are to repay, and what rate makes the loan worth the risk. Your credit score is part of the answer, but it is not the whole thing. Income, existing debt and recent credit activity all feed into the decision and into the APR you are offered.

This guide walks through each requirement, shows how much your rate can change the cost of the same loan, and lists what to do before you apply. To test your own numbers, use the [personal loan calculator](/calculators/loan/).

## The four things lenders check

| Requirement | What the lender looks at | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Credit score and report | Your score, payment history, balances, collections, bankruptcies | Predicts how likely you are to repay on time |
| Income | Pay stubs, tax returns, bank deposits, benefits | Shows you can afford the monthly payment |
| Debt-to-income ratio | Monthly debt payments divided by gross monthly income | Shows how much room is left in your budget |
| Recent credit activity | New accounts and hard inquiries in recent months | Many new applications can signal financial stress |

Lenders may also ask about employment, housing costs and the purpose of the loan, and they verify your identity and address. Requirements differ from lender to lender, which is why the same applicant can be declined by one lender and approved by another.

## Credit score: there is no single minimum

No federal rule sets a minimum credit score for a personal loan. Each lender chooses its own cutoff, and many publish only a general description such as "fair credit and above." What is consistent is how scores are used: the higher your score, the lower the risk to the lender, and the lower the rate it can offer.

Most lenders use FICO or VantageScore models, which run from 300 to 850. Your score is built mainly from your payment history and how much of your available credit you are using. Our guide to [how credit scores work](/guides/how-credit-scores-work/) explains each factor.

Before you apply, pull your free credit reports and check them for errors. A single collection account reported in error can move you into a higher rate tier.

## How your rate changes the cost of the same loan

For context, the Federal Reserve's G.19 consumer credit release tracks the average rate commercial banks charge on 24-month personal loans. In 2026 releases that average has been a little under 12% APR. Online lenders typically quote a wide range, and where you land in that range depends mostly on your credit profile and DTI.

Here is what different APRs mean for the same $15,000 loan repaid over three years. The rates are examples, not quotes:

| APR | Monthly payment | Total interest over 36 months |
|---|---|---|
| 11% | about $491 | about $2,679 |
| 17% | about $535 | about $4,252 |
| 24% | about $588 | about $6,186 |
| 30% | about $637 | about $7,924 |

Moving from a 24% APR to an 11% APR on this loan saves about $3,500 in interest and roughly $97 a month. That is why a few months spent improving your credit before you apply can be worth more than any other step.

### Watch the origination fee

Many lenders deduct an origination fee from the loan amount. If you need $15,000 in hand and the lender charges a 5% fee, you would need to borrow about $15,789. At an 11% interest rate over 36 months, that is a payment of about $517 and about $3,609 in total interest and fees, compared with about $2,679 for a loan with no fee. The fee is included in the APR, which is why APR is the right number to compare across lenders.

## Income: proving you can make the payment

Lenders want stable, verifiable income. Expect to provide recent pay stubs, W-2s or tax returns if you are self-employed, and sometimes bank statements. Many lenders count income beyond wages, such as Social Security, pensions or alimony, if it is regular and documented.

Some lenders set a minimum annual income. Others look only at whether the new payment fits your budget. Either way, be accurate: overstating income on a loan application can lead to a loan you cannot afford and can have legal consequences.

## Debt-to-income ratio: often the deciding factor

Your debt-to-income ratio is your total monthly debt payments divided by your gross monthly income. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's budgeting worksheet suggests keeping total DTI at or below 36%, and notes that some lenders go up to 43% or higher. Personal loan lenders set their own limits.

**Worked example.** Maria earns $5,000 a month before taxes. Her rent is not counted as debt by every lender, but her other payments are: a car loan, a student loan and credit card minimums totaling $1,200.

- Current DTI: $1,200 ÷ $5,000 = **24%**
- With a new $491 loan payment: $1,691 ÷ $5,000 = **33.8%**

If Maria is using the loan to pay off her credit cards, those minimum payments disappear, and her DTI after consolidation may be lower than it is today. Lenders that offer debt consolidation loans often take that into account, especially if they pay your creditors directly. Our [debt-to-income ratio guide](/guides/debt-to-income-ratio/) shows how to calculate yours.

## Recent credit activity and hard inquiries

Prequalification, where a lender shows you an estimated rate before you apply, usually relies on a soft inquiry. The CFPB explains that soft inquiries do not affect your credit score. A formal application normally triggers a hard inquiry, which can lower your score slightly for a short period.

A practical approach is to prequalify with several lenders, compare the estimated APRs, and submit a full application only to the one or two best offers. Opening several new accounts in a short span can make lenders more cautious.

## If you do not meet the requirements yet

- **Add a co-signer or co-borrower.** Some lenders allow it. That person becomes legally responsible for the debt, so treat the request seriously.
- **Lower your credit utilization.** Paying down card balances before you apply can raise your score within a billing cycle or two. See our [credit score improvement guide](/guides/how-to-improve-credit-score/).
- **Borrow less or choose a shorter term.** A smaller loan with a lower payment keeps your DTI down.
- **Look at credit unions.** Federal credit unions are member-owned, and many offer personal loans to members with a range of credit profiles.
- **Compare alternatives.** For card debt, a 0% balance transfer card or a nonprofit debt management plan may cost less. Our guide on [personal loans vs. credit cards](/guides/personal-loan-vs-credit-card/) compares the options.

Servicemembers on active duty and their dependents have extra protection: under the Military Lending Act, the Military Annual Percentage Rate on most consumer loans cannot exceed 36%.

## Checklist before you apply

1. Pull your credit reports and dispute any errors.
2. Calculate your DTI, including the new payment.
3. Gather pay stubs, tax documents and ID.
4. Prequalify with at least three lenders. Our [personal loan comparison](/best/personal-loans/) lists lenders that offer prequalification.
5. Compare offers by APR, origination fee and total repayment.
6. Run the final numbers in the [loan calculator](/calculators/loan/) and, for debt payoff, the [debt consolidation calculator](/calculators/debt-consolidation/).
